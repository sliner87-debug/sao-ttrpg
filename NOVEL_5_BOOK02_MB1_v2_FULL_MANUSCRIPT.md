## CHAPTER 1: THE PRESSURE GAUGE

The cold was a familiar companion. It clung to the sub-basement like morning fog on the Kearny marshes, seeping through cracks in the foundation and settling into my bones. But this cold was different. This cold came from *me*.

I wiped the last of the blood off my hex-brass knuckles with the rag, the mineral spirits cutting through the iron-scent with a sharp chemical bite. The red stains bloomed into the yellowed cloth like watercolors. Outside, the Sony MiniDisc player continued its low-fi dirge—a faded copy of some band I couldn't remember, the lead singer's voice garbled and distant.

The temperature around me was dropping again. I could see my breath misting in the air, watch the frost creep across the edges of the steel tools laid out on the workbench. Each one gleaming under the single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling. The cold wasn't accidental anymore. It was a choice, a statement. The Threefold Cord wrapped around my chest tightened, its weight familiar and grounding.

My Motorola Razr sat on the corner of the bench, its screen dark. No messages. No calls. Grimjaw was probably still topside, making sure the South Side didn't burn while I worked. He'd understand. He always did.

But the Lead-Bismuth Myocardial Geas fused to my sternum *throbbed*.

It wasn't pain—not exactly. More like a mechanical warning, a pressure gauge redlining. The thing Mara Vance had sewn into my ribcage with her clinical trauma-triage precision, binding me to her 314 dead commuters and 38 living orphans. A reminder that I wasn't just some South Side machinist anymore. I was something more. Something worse.

The throb came again, insistent. Demanding.

I set down the rag and reached for my coffee. Cold. I'd forgotten to drink it. The ceramic mug sat heavy in my palm, condensation beading on the outside. I took a sip anyway, the bitter liquid doing nothing to warm the space inside my chest where the Geas pulsed.

*Focus,* I thought. *Finish the job. Then we can figure out what the fuck it wants.*

But the Geas didn't care about my priorities. It had its own agenda, bound to Mara's white-lilac and cedar bio-wards, to the ash-gray static that bled from her like smoke. She was out there somewhere in the Neon Hollow, probably running triage on some new nightmare that had crawled through the Breach. Her hands steady. Her voice calm.

And me? I was here, in this freezing sub-basement, cleaning blood off my knuckles while the world burned.

The cold intensified.

I closed my eyes and let it wash over me.

* * *

**I walk over to the brass-and-steel cylinder that takes up half the sub-basement. The thing hums like a industrial-sized refrigerator, but instead of keeping food cold, it's keeping Danny alive.**

**The Arcane Iron-Lung.**

**It's a monstrous piece of kit, all hand-welded copper tubing and arc-welded steel plating, with a dozen different gauges and pressure valves running up its side like veins. At the center of it all is a clear plexiglass chamber where Danny lies, chest bare, tubes running into his throat and nostrils. His ribs expand and contract in perfect mechanical rhythm—*hiss-hiss, pump-pump*—the only thing that keeps black-slime from flooding his alveoli and suffocating him.**

**I lean in close, watching my little brother sleep. He looks so goddamn young. So goddamn small.**

**Eighteen years old, and he's been on this machine for seven years. Since the night the Experimental Sludge hit the sewers and turned half the South Side into a toxic hellscape. Since the night I was supposed to be watching him.**

**Bram Kovacs, the dwarven master-machinist from Local 405, is hunched over his Toughbook in the corner, running diagnostics. The little guy's a genius with engines—he can make a semi truck's V8 sing like a bird or weld a transmission back together from scrap. But this? This is his magnum opus. He built this Iron-Lung from scratch, cobbling together arcane engineering principles with 2007 industrial tech.**

**"Pressure's building again," Bram says without looking up. His voice has that thick, accented tone of a veteran machinist. "Third time this week. The filters can't keep up with the iron-gall buildup. Need to bleed the valves soon, or we're going to have a catastrophic failure."**

**I nod, jaw tight. I know what happens if this machine fails. I've seen it before—watched as other kids who got hit by the Sludge suffocated when their machines gave out. Watched their parents break. Watched their neighborhoods burn.**

**Can't let that happen to Danny.**

**"How long can you give me?" I ask.**

**Bram finally looks up, his weathered face grim. "Maybe six hours. Maybe eight if you're lucky. But Cole... you need to know. Every time we bleed the valves, every time we replace the filters, we're buying time. Not fixing the problem. The black iron's in his blood now. In his bones. It's a death sentence, no matter what we do."**

**I feel that old anger flare in my chest—the same anger that's been eating at me since 2000. The same anger that drove me to become the thing I am now.**

**But I push it down. Can't afford to lose control. Not here. Not with Danny so close.**

**"Six hours," I repeat. "That's all I need."**

**Bram studies me for a long moment, then shrugs and goes back to his diagnostics. He knows better than to argue. Everyone on the South Side knows about me now. Cole Reyes—the guy who takes on the Syndicate, who hits BATC facilities, who makes people disappear. They don't ask questions. They just... accept.**

**I move to the Iron-Lung's main valve cluster, a complex array of brass and copper fittings that Bram has labeled in his own handwriting. The pressure gauge reads 1200 PSI and climbing—well into the danger zone. Little droplets of blue-black sludge are already forming on the filter mesh, sizzling as they hit the air.**

**I grab a wrench from the nearby tool bench—heavy steel, weighty in my hand. Good. Solid. Real.**

**The first valve comes off with a hiss of escaping pressure. Immediately, a stream of black iron-gall sludge sprays out, splattering across the concrete floor. The stench hits me like a punch to the face—like burning metal and rotting meat, the same smell that still clings to parts of the South Side seven years later.**

**I wrench the second valve open. More sludge. More pressure bleeding off.**

**My hands move with practiced efficiency. This is the one thing I can control. The one thing I can fix. For now, at least.**

**As I work, my mind drifts back to that night in 2000. The screams. The smoke. The smell of the Sludge pouring through the streets like a black river. Danny, crying for me, begging me to save him. And me, too far away, too busy with my own problems to notice what was happening until it was too late.**

**The guilt sits in my gut like lead.**

**But guilt doesn't save lives. Revenge doesn't save lives. Only action does.**

**And right now, my action is this: keeping my brother alive, one valve at a time.**

**I reach for the third valve, and as I do, my Geas throbs in my chest—a sharp, agonizing pulse that syncs perfectly with the Iron-Lung's pistons.**

*Hiss-hiss, pump-pump.*

*Pain-pain, guilt-guilt.*

**I grit my teeth and keep working.**

**Four more valves to bleed. Four more hours of life to buy.**

**Then I have to go back out into the night. Back to the war.**

**Because if I don't, Danny's Iron-Lung stops. And I lose the last piece of family I have left.**

**My hands tighten on the wrench.**

**No. Not going to happen.**

**I'm Cole Reyes. I'm a South Side machinist. I'm a vigilante.**

**And I always finish the job.**

* * *

I checked my watch—11:48 PM—and turned off Danny's light with my thumb. The kid needed sleep more than answers.

The walk from our chop-shop apartment down to the docks took twelve minutes. I kept my hands in my pockets, feeling the weight of the trench knife against my ribs. North Jersey at night smelled like diesel exhaust and brine from the bay, cut through with that metallic tang everyone called 'the Weave' but really meant 'the Sludge.'

The message had come through clear as day: **"PYLON 2. TWELVE THIRTY. BRING LEAD."**

Not even a name. Not even a fucking courtesy. Just coordinates and a suggestion that whatever was about to go down required serious firepower.

I didn't ask questions anymore. Three years of this shit had taught me one lesson: when the Syndicate wanted you someplace at a specific time, showing up empty-handed got you dead or worse.

## CHAPTER 3: THE DEAD-DROP & THE SLIVER OF JUSTICE

The rust-pocked I-beam groaned as I applied pressure, my shoulder screaming protest from yesterday's thermite burn. Three hours past midnight in October means nothing down here—time bends weird in the Neon Hollow, like everything else since Y2K cracked the world open and spat out monsters into North Jersey's industrial heartland.

The brick came free with a shower of crumbling mortar.

My gloved fingers found the metal cylinder tucked behind it without needing light—the wax seal felt different against skin, not quite warm but not cold either. Blood-warm. Alive somehow. That made every instinct scream danger, but the Watching Party had been good for intel these last six months. Cauterized clean of any magical residue that might trip BATC scanners.

I broke the seal.

Steam curled from the photographs inside, condensation forming on the steel girders around me. Always happens when I think too hard about what I've done, what I've become. The hex-blood running through my veins makes everything colder, farther away. My brother Danny never came back from the Pylon fires in 2005, didn't have hex-blood or metahuman resilience, just seventeen years old and curious about the wrong things at the right moment.

Not thinking about him now. Can't afford to.

The first photograph showed Marcus Voss shaking hands with someone whose face was deliberately blurred. Corporate lawyer type by his suit—a three-piece Brioni that cost more than Grimjaw's annual union wages. Behind them: the skeletal framework of Pylon #2, still missing its Gold-Bismuth Collar after the syndicate bombing last year. Structural integrity reading critical across all three pylons according to Mara's readings.

Pylon #3 was scheduled for destabilization.

Pylon #1 was already compromised.

That left Pylon #4 as the only remaining active node keeping Newark Bay's Telluric currents stable enough to prevent another Millenium Breach cascade. And according to this intelligence—this wax-sealed secret—I'd stumbled into something bigger than South Side vigilante work.

Visible Head #2 wanted the entire North Jersey grid to fail simultaneously.

I flipped to the second photo. Same lawyer-suit. Different handshake partner.

BATC Director Elias Morvain.

Visible Head #3 had been playing both sides since before I started breaking Syndicate kneecaps in earnest.

My knuckles went white around the photograph edges. Morvain ran the Bureau's Telluric Engineering Corps—the same division responsible for maintaining Gold-Bismuth Collars on every pylon, every substation, every God-forsaken power conduit threading through Neon Hollow's infrastructure. He knew exactly how much pressure it took to crack a Collar from the inside out without leaving conventional evidence.

Without leaving anything but bodies.

The third photograph made my stomach clench. Sub-Station Seven. The one where Mara had burned 314 commuters alive while saving those thirty-eight orphans during her command rotation in 2005. Her lead-bismuth geas marked permanent now, seared behind her sternum like a brand she could never remove. She carried that weight differently these days—sharp-edged and reckless when BATC wouldn't authorize strikes against syndicate targets.

When I did instead.

Because someone had to pay penance for Danny. Someone had to serve revenge on the bastards who lit him up like a Roman candle at Pylon #2. And if all that bought me was a sliver of justice—that tiny fucking wedge that didn't bring back his lungs or erase what happened or make any goddamn sense—then so be it.

It would do for the moment.

The fourth photograph showed Sub-Station Seven's structural schematics with multiple red X's marking load-bearing supports. Someone planned another collapse, another mass casualty event designed to overload Telluric currents and trigger cascade failures across the entire Newark Bay grid.

Designed to breach the Millenium gap again.

I palmed the photographs, resealed them in their cylinder, and replaced it behind the brick. My breath came out white clouds in air that shouldn't be this cold three feet above groundwater level. The hex-blood making everything distant, numb, necessary.

Visible Head #3 needed to die harder than anyone yet.

But first: intelligence. First: leverage. First: I needed Grimjaw's engineering readouts from Local 405's last tunnel inspection—get a clear picture of which underground conduits ran parallel to Sub-Station Seven before making any moves.

Morvain thought he was playing grand strategy. Thought he'd calculated every variable except one:

I don't need fairness. Don't need proportionality or due process or any of that civic-society bullshit that got Danny killed while Syndicate enforcers walked away scot-free into their penthouses overlooking the Passaic River.

Justice could be a sliver.

Had to be a sliver.

Would have to be enough until it wasn't anymore.

I climbed down from the platform ledge, boots hitting cracked concrete that echoed through abandoned tunnels where no L-trains had run since 2001. When they tried to restore service after Y2K, found the tracks warped by ley-line interference—metal twisted into shapes defying physics like everything else in Neon Hollow these days.

The photographs burned against my chest pocket despite my best efforts not to think about them too hard.

Morvain. Voss. Visible Heads #2 and #3 both moving pieces on a board I couldn't see yet.

And my mother—the woman I'd buried in '98, who supposedly died from industrial exposure at her Textile Mill job—running some shadow faction called the Watching Party that fed me intelligence sealed in wax because she knew exactly what kind of monster her son had become.

What he needed to become.

The cold followed me out of those tunnels, frost crystallizing along rust-pocked beams behind my passage like I'd never been there at all.
