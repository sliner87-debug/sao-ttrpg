## CHAPTER 1: THE CREEPING FROST

The wrench slipped in my greasy fingers just as the temperature hit forty-three degrees.

I caught it against my shin—a dull thud that barely registered over the rattle of Danny's breathing from the back office. That sound. That fucking *sound*. Like someone trying to inhale through a rusted pipe filled with marbles and blood.

"Shit," I muttered, straightening up from the engine bay. My breath came out in a cloud that shouldn't have been there—not yet. Forty-three degrees inside, and the frost was already creeping across the Crown Vic's windshield in crystalline fractals. Each one of them looked like a spider's web made of broken glass.

The shop smelled wrong tonight. Usually it was all 10W-40 and transmission fluid, maybe a hint of burnt clutch plate when we were running hot jobs. Now there was something else undercutting everything—that metallic tang that meant my blood-iron hexes were running too cold, too tight. The oil in the Vic's pan had started to thicken, going viscous and slow.

Another wet cough from the back office.

I wiped my hands on a rag already black with grease, then immediately regretted it because now my palms were slick and useless. The sodium-vapor lamps outside buzzed their constant electrical hum—the city never slept, even at three in the morning, especially not in Neon Hollow where the streetlights fought shadows that didn't want to lose.

"Danny?" My voice came out rougher than I intended.

Tap-tap-TAP. Tap-tap-TAP.

That was his response. His rhythm. Always three beats, always that emphasis on the third. Danny's way of saying he was still here, still breathing, still alive in whatever hellscape his lungs had become since the Telluric Compressor blew six months ago.

I walked toward the back office, each step crunching on frost that had no business being there. The concrete floor should've been bone-dry—we kept the shop climate-controlled for exactly this reason, kept the humidity low so the brass mechanisms wouldn't seize up. But my hexes didn't give a fuck about climate control when the anxiety hit.

The door hung open. Inside, bathed in the sickly green glow of the monitoring equipment, stood the Iron-Lung.

It looked like something Salvador Dalí would've designed if he'd been drunk on mercury and hallucinating machinery. All brass pipes and glass chambers, copper coils wrapped around pulmonary capacitors, tubes snaking through impossible geometries. We'd built it together over two years—me doing the mechanical work while Danny studied the arcane theory, cross-referencing texts he'd stolen from the Sterling Manor archives. Back when we thought we could out-engineer biology itself.

Now it was all we had left.

Danny's face appeared in one of the larger glass panels, distorted by the curves but unmistakably him. Fifteen years old going on forty, dark circles under his eyes that matched the shadows underneath mine.

"Bro," he said, voice muffled and filtered through the machine, "you know your taste in music is objectively terrible."

The Sony MiniDisc player sat on the workbench behind me, still skipping on the same track it had been stuck on for an hour. Some shitty nu-metal song with too many breakdowns and not enough melody. I'd put it on just to have *something* fill the silence between his breaths.

"Says the kid who tried to make me listen to twenty-minute long black metal songs." I stepped inside, letting the heat from the Compressor wash over me. It felt good against the frost that had settled into my bones. "At least this has a chorus."

"A bad one."

"A *chorus*, Danny."

He tapped again. Tap-tap-TAP. Then he coughed—that horrible wet rattling sound—and I saw the way the brass pistons stuttered in response, how the pressure gauges spiked and dropped like they were having a seizure.

"How's she running?" I asked, though I already knew the answer. Could see it in the way condensation had started beading on the glass, in the faint whine coming from the compressor unit. The thing was deteriorating faster every week.

"The Cough in the Cold," Danny said, and then he laughed, which turned into another cough, which made everything rattle and groan. "Sounds like a bad poetry slam title."

"That's what you're calling it now?"

"Why not? You're the one who keeps freezing the whole fucking shop every time you get nervous." He gestured at the windows, where frost had started creeping down from the top frames. Outside, Neon Hollow looked like something from a noir film—all streaking headlights and neon signs reflecting off puddles of oil-slick water.

I moved closer to the Iron-Lung, studying the readings on the analog dials we'd salvaged from God-knows-where. Pressure dropping. Oxygen saturation falling. The copper coils showing green corrosion that shouldn't have been there yet.

"We need to work on the Telluric Core Compressor," I said quietly. "The upgrade. Sooner rather than later."

"Yeah." Danny's voice was smaller now, tired. "Soon."

There was nothing else to say after that. Nothing that wouldn't sound like goodbyes or lies or both. So I just stood there in the cold, watching my little brother breathe through a machine we'd built with stolen parts and forbidden knowledge, while outside the city hummed its indifferent song and the temperature kept dropping around us.

Frost bloomed across the metal pipes.

And somewhere in the darkness beyond our chop-shop, something waited.

* * *

I heard the boots before I saw him—three deliberate strikes against wet pavement, the kind of walk that meant business even when it didn't need to. Bram Kovacs pushed through my chop-shop door without knocking, canvas bag slung over one shoulder like it weighed nothing.

"The kid's fucked," he said flatly, shaking water from his beard. Outside, the January wind screamed through Neon Hollow, rattling windows already frosted at the edges where my hexes had been leaking all morning. Every time I thought about what was growing inside Danny's chest, the temperature dropped another degree.

Bram set down his bag and pulled out a pair of those stupid welders' goggles he insisted on wearing everywhere. "Got the diagnostic rig?"

"Back table." My voice came out wrong—tighter than intended. I'd been running on four hours sleep and zero coffee, maintaining the frost-cobalt state because anything less felt like letting go of the last rope holding me above water.

We worked in silence for a minute, unbolting the primary housing of Danny's Iron-Lung. The brass plates came away with a hiss of escaping pressure, releasing a plume of vapor that reeked of burnt ozone and something worse—the metallic tang of necrotic sludge. That smell had been following us since we'd pulled Danny out of the marsh two weeks ago.

Bram hooked up his heavily modded IBM ThinkPad, fingers dancing across keys while a green glow reflected off his goggles. The laptop whined as it booted into some custom Linux distro covered in arcane command prompts I couldn't begin to read.

"Filtration busbars are slagged," he muttered, more to himself than me. "Look at this shit."

I looked. What I saw made my stomach clench hard enough to hurt.

Through the transparent section of the filtration chamber, I could see them—black crystals of Black-Iron Sludge, each one no bigger than a grain of rice but moving with terrible purpose. They were bypassing our filters entirely, riding along the brass channels like parasites finding their way home. And they were close—millimeters from where Danny's pericardium should be, visible through another transparent panel in the chest housing.

"They're adapting," Bram said, his voice dropping to something almost reverent, which was worse than anger from him. "The sludge is learning. This brass rig we built? It's a band-aid on a bullet wound." He tapped the screen, bringing up a schematic overlay. Red zones bloomed across the image. "See here? Here? The crystal formation is fractal-based now. Every time we filter one path clear, three more routes open up. It's not mechanics anymore, Cole. It's biology."

My hands were shaking. Not from fear—I'd burned past that somewhere around hour forty-eight of this nightmare—but from the cold. The shop had dropped another five degrees since we started, frost creeping across the workbench edges. My blood-iron hexes were running hot with stress, bleeding cold into everything around us.

"We need to compress it," I said. The words came out flat, final. "Telluric Core approach. Run the core through a secondary loop, use the magnetic field to crystallize the sludge faster than it can adapt."

Bram finally looked up at me, and what I saw in those eyes wasn't encouragement. "That's theoretical, and you know it. We don't have the containment vessel specs worked out. You try running that much telluric current through untested brass, you'll cook the kid's heart before you slow the sludge down."

"Then what?" The question came out sharp enough to cut. "We watch him drown in his own chest? We built him an Iron-Lung so he could breathe, Bram. Right now he's got maybe—what? Forty-eight hours before those crystals reach his pericardium?"

The welder goggles reflected green light back at me, hiding Bram's expression. Outside, sirens wailed in the distance—probably the Pulaski Skyway, always some kind of accident on that stretch. Normal problems for normal people who didn't have to worry about Black-Iron Sludge growing inside their children's ribcages.

"We build the compressor," Bram said quietly. "But we do it right. Full schematics, test rig, everything by the book." He closed the laptop, unplugging it with practiced efficiency. "I've got blueprints from my grandfather's workshop—pre-war stuff, when Local 405 was doing arcane machinery before anyone called it 'arcano-noir.' There's a compression matrix design that might work if we adapt it properly."

"How long?"

"Fast as possible means six weeks minimum. Realistic is eight." He shouldered his bag again, already moving toward the door. "And Cole? Get control of your hexes before they ice this whole building solid. The kid doesn't need frostbite on top of everything else."

I stood there after he left, staring at the filtration chamber and the black crystals crawling through brass channels designed to stop them. Somewhere in Sterling Manor, whatever had birthed this sludge was still out there, still spreading through the marsh system beneath Newark Bay.

Six weeks. Maybe eight.

Danny only had two days.

My hands clenched into fists, and I felt the frost-cobalt surge harder, faster—the shop windows weeping ice down their panes while motor oil thickened in its cans. The rage wasn't helping. The fear wasn't helping. Nothing except finding another way forward would help now.

I pulled Danny's full schematic off the wall board and spread it across the main table, green diagnostic lights casting everything in an underwater glow. The Iron-Lung design we'd built in Piscataway during those desperate summer months of 2001 looked so clean then, so simple.

Everything looks simple before you see how badly it can fail.

The Black-Iron Sludge didn't care about our timelines or our craftsmanship. It just grew, adapted, pushed forward with the patient inevitability of entropy itself.

I picked up my wrench—weighted brass, union-machinist grade—and got back to work.

* * *

The monitor's sharp alarm cuts through the chop-shop's ambient hum like a blade through meat.

I'm on my feet before I register movement, boots crunching across fresh frost that shouldn't be there on the concrete floor. My breath hangs in the air—visibility low, temperature lower. The copper tang of blood-iron hits my tongue as my hexes lock down cold, dead-silent cobalt.

"Danny!" The name tears out of me raw.

Bram's already moving, his mechanic's hands dancing across the brass valves of the Iron-Lung apparatus. The machine coughs—a wet, struggling *hiss-thump*—that sounds too much like drowning for comfort. Danny's fifteen years old, stretched out on the reinforced medical slab, his chest rising and falling beneath the translucent crystal casing of the 2000-era brass lung.

"Pressure spike," Bram mutters, knuckles white around the pressure gauge. "He's compensating, but the seals are—shit."

The monitors flatline. Just for a second. A microsecond that stretches into forever.

My fingers clamp around the edge of the workbench. Frost creeps up the metal, spreading like veins across steel. A half-drunk cup of coffee sits on the desk—I didn't even see it earlier. Now it's solid ice, frozen mid-ring with the lip prints still visible, condensation crystals catching the harsh fluorescent light.

*Thump-hiss. Thump-hiss.*

The Iron-Lung fights to keep him alive.

"You need to step back." Bram's voice is steady—the kind of calm you only get from twenty years of watching people die in slow motion. He adjusts the main valve, and the rhythmic breathing steadies. "Cole. *Step back.*"

I can feel the frost creeping up my pant legs now, numbness spreading from my ankles upward. My heart hammers against my ribs, each beat forcing more of that metallic cold through my system. The temperature must be dropping ten degrees every time my pulse spikes.

"Tell me he's stable." Not a question. A demand.

Bram straightens up, wiping grease-stained hands on a rag that's seen better days. His eyes meet mine, and I know. I know before he opens his mouth because he's already looking at me the way people look when they're trying to find words soft enough not to break what's left.

"He's compensating," Bram repeats carefully. "But this isn't temporary anymore, Cole. The brass is failing. The crystal matrix is degrading faster than we anticipated."

"How fast?"

"The lungs are 2000-era tech. They weren't built for permanent installation, and definitely not for someone with Danny's... condition." He gestures at the sprawled form on the slab, at the delicate web of brass tubing and arcane crystal that keeps oxygen moving through my little brother's bloodstream while his real organs slowly shut down. "Three months. Maybe four if we're lucky and careful."

My hands are shaking. Frost pebbles off them onto the floor—*crunch crunch crunch*—each shard reflecting the emergency lights.

"You said 'if.'" My voice sounds wrong. Hollow. Like it's coming from somewhere underground. "What's the other option?"

Bram glances at the Iron-Lung, then back at me. When he speaks, there's something different in his tone—a weight he's been carrying and finally deciding to pass along.

"There's military-grade tech out there. Telluric Core Compressors. Pure arcane pressure delivery systems designed for deep-cover metahuman ops." He pulls out a cigarette, lights it with unsteady hands. "The Black-Circuit Syndicate's Inner Council acquired a shipment last month. Rumor is they're sitting on at least six units in their Sterling Manor facility."

I stare at him. At the smoke curling from his lips, at the frozen coffee cup, at the rhythmic *thump-hiss* of my brother's borrowed lungs.

"Military grade." I test the words like tasting poison. "You mean illegal. You mean dangerous. You mean exactly the kind of thing that got us into this mess in the first place."

"The Iron-Lung was supposed to be temporary," Bram says quietly. "Until we found a cure. Until Danny's body could heal itself." He takes another drag, exhales gray into frost-laden air. "That was three years ago, Cole. And now the only thing that can push enough pure pressure to keep those crystals away from his heart is tech so hot it'd melt you if you looked at it sideways."

Silence settles over the chop-shop like snow.

I walk to the workbench, fingers trailing across metal that shrieks under my touch—frost expanding wherever skin meets surface. The coffee cup sits there accusingly solid, lip prints preserved in ice.

"How much?"

Bram knows what I'm asking without elaboration. "More than Local 405 sees in two years of protection money." He pauses. "And even if we had it, the Syndicate doesn't sell to outsiders. They don't negotiate. They don't compromise."

"Then we don't ask." The words come out flat, final. Dead-ice cold.

His eyes widen slightly—the first crack in Bram's professional mask since we started this conversation.

"We buy a seat at their table," I continue, staring at my reflection in the frozen coffee surface—distorted, fragmented, barely recognizable. "We take one."

"You're talking about going up against the Black-Circuit Inner Council." Bram's voice carries an edge of something between concern and disbelief. "Cole, they eat people like us for breakfast."

"They don't know who I am yet."

The frost on the floor spreads wider as my pulse kicks harder, colder. The temperature in here must be dropping into the thirties by now—the oil drums are beginning to glisten with condensation turned white.

Danny makes a soft sound on the slab. Just a whisper of breath through the brass tubes.

*Thump-hiss.*

My little brother, alive because of twenty-year-old tech that's failing faster every day. Three months. Maybe four if we're lucky and careful.

I look down at my hands—they're completely white now, veins traced in cobalt blue beneath skin so cold it almost burns. When I breathe, it fogs more than it should indoors.

"Get me everything you can find on Sterling Manor," I say quietly. "Personnel files. Security layouts. Supply routes. The names of everyone who works there."

Bram watches me for a long moment—measuring something he doesn't like but isn't going to stop anyway.

"And after?" he asks finally.

I pick up the frozen coffee cup carefully, holding it to the light. Through the ice, I can see the lip print clearly—the shape of someone's mouth, preserved forever in cold.

"After," I say, "we go to war."

The *thump-hiss* continues its steady rhythm behind me while outside the chop-shop windows, the winter wind howls across Neon Hollow, carrying the smell of Newark Bay and promises of things to come.

## CHAPTER 2: THE SYNDICATE SOLUTION

The Toughbook's screen casts its sickly green glow across my knuckles as they fly across the rubberized keyboard. Each keystroke produces that distinctive *clack-clack-clack* against the metal workbench—sharp, industrial, final. Beneath me, the Undercity tunnels hum with the ghost-rattle of third-rail current.

Stale Marlboro smoke hangs thick in the air of this particular dead-drop, mixing with the metallic tang of machine oil and something darker—something organic and wrong that I've learned to associate with the deeper levels where things that shouldn't exist crawl between the support beams. My breath comes out in visible puffs despite the oppressive heat radiating from the server towers surrounding us.

*"Fuck,"* I mutter, scrolling through another layer of encryption. The Black-Circuit Syndicate doesn't fuck around when it comes to protecting their IP. They're running military-grade scramblers on everything worth stealing, routing their manifests through at least three different dead-satellite relays before dumping them into the public feeds.

But nothing's truly invisible if you know where to look for the shadows.

I find it two hours past midnight—buried in a maintenance log for Sub-Station Seven. A single line item, buried between routine conduit checks and a report about rats chewing through insulation: **TELLURIC CORE COMPRESSOR - UNIT 07429-X - STERLING MANOR WAREHOUSE C**

My heart does that thing where it stops being a muscle and starts being a stone in my chest. It's real. Danny can actually be saved.

The specs scroll across the screen. God-tier tech that shouldn't exist outside black-ops facilities. Capacitor arrays designed to store enough raw electrical potential to power a small city block—or burn one clean to ash. Exactly what he needs to stabilize his core before the feedback cascade tears him apart molecule by molecule.

The motorola Razr on the desk next to me starts vibrating like a man having a seizure, rattling against the cold steel with enough urgency to draw blood. I grab it without looking away from the screen.

"Reyes." The voice crackles through—Grimjaw, calling from somewhere in the sprawl between Kearny and Newark Bay. "You still breathing over there?"

"I'm here." My own voice sounds flat, tired down to my bones. "Got something."

"What kind of something?" There's an edge of interest underneath the gruff exterior. Grimjaw runs the Local 405 coalition—three dozen street crews and independent operators who've decided that the Syndicate's monopoly on high-end tech has gone on long enough. We're talking ex-military hardware, metahuman augmentation tech, digital weapons systems that could punch holes in reality if you knew how to aim them right.

"The good kind," I say. "But we need to move fast. Sterling Manor warehouse district. Tonight."

A pause while he processes this. When Grimjaw speaks again, there's something harder in his tone—the sound of gears locking into place. "How fast are we talking?"

I pull up the manifest cross-references, watching as the data streams across multiple windows. "According to their shipping logs, they've got a military-grade Necro-Thermite shipment moving through the 405 district at oh-four-hundred. Thirty crates. rival cartel trying to muscle in on Black-Circuit territory."

"Jesus Christ." Now there's genuine concern threading through his words. "That shit goes off near any population center—"

"It won't." My hand moves to the Toughbook's trackpad, bringing up the routing information. "They're keeping it underground. Moving it by subway freight car through the abandoned tunnels beneath Pulaski Skyway. It's the only route they can take without drawing heat from the Feds."

Another silence, longer this time. I can hear him breathing on the other end—that wet rasp that comes from too many years sucking factory air in places like this.

"If we hit that convoy..." He's thinking out loud now, running the arithmetic of violence in his head like a priest counting rosary beads. "We'd have leverage. Real leverage. Not just against the Syndicate—against everyone operating in North Jersey."

"You're saying yes."

"I'm saying..." A long exhalation. "I'm saying get your gear ready. I'll start pulling the crew together. Where do you want to meet?"

"The old diner on South Street. The one with the flickering neon sign. Midnight." I pause, staring at the Telluric Core Compressor specs one more time. Danny's face flashes behind my eyes—the way he looked before the accident, before the core went critical and started eating him from the inside out. "And Grimjaw? Bring every heavy weapon you've got stashed away. This is going to be bloody."

"No shit," he mutters, and then the line goes dead.

I close the laptop, plunging the chop-shop into darkness except for the emergency red lights mounted along the ceiling beams. They cast everything in shades of crimson—a preview of what's coming.

The frost-cobalt aura coils tighter around me, that familiar pressure building behind my sternum as I let the cold settle into my bones. Out there in the tunnels, something moves between the shadows. Something hungry.

But tonight, I'm hungrier.

I pick up my gear bag and head for the exit, boots crunching over broken glass and things I don't care to identify. The dark wire stretches ahead of us all—the thin thread connecting this moment to whatever comes after. And we're about to cut it.

* * *

The back alley reeked of grease and stale beer, steam rising from a rusted grate like a dragon's dying breath. I leaned against the brick wall, my frost-cobalt aura coiled tight around me, watching the shadows between the flickering neon signs of the diner. Red. Yellow. Red. Each pulse made the puddles on the pavement look like bleeding wounds.

Grimjaw arrived first, his massive frame filling the mouth of the alley. The Orc enforcer moved with surprising quiet for someone built like a freight train, his leather jacket creaking with each step. Behind him came the others—two Ogre-Kin twins carrying sawed-off shotguns that looked comically oversized even in their meaty fists, a crew of three Goblins with bolt cutters hanging from their belts like badges of office, and finally, three Street-Elves in optical-camo jackets that shimmered like oil slicks under the neon glow.

"Hey, Cole." Grimjaw's voice rolled out low and gravelly, pure South Side enforcement. "Brought your party animals."

I pushed off the wall, feeling the cold radiate through my coat. "You all know why we're here."

One of the Goblin wire-cutters, missing an ear and sporting a jagged scar across her jaw, spoke up fast. "Word is you want to hit cartel armor. That's suicide pay, friend."

"The pay's thirty crates of thermite," I said flatly. "Black-Circuit Syndicate cargo, heading through the Pulaski Skyway at 0400 tomorrow. Their convoy's got three armored trucks and six bike escorts. Heavy weapons."

A Razor-Wire Street-Elf named Knife pushed her goggles up, her eyes reflecting the neon like a cat's. "Sniper positions confirmed along Route 1-9. We can pin the bikes before they scatter. Optical-cam will make us ghosts to their thermal scans."

Grimjaw scratched his tusk with a calloused thumb. "That's a lot of metal between us and those crates. How we cracking the lead truck?"

"Thermite charges on the hinges." I pulled out a folded schematic, spread it against the brick. "Ogre-Kin twins handle security—these two guards won't know what hit them until their doors are molten slag. Goblins cut the comms lines at Sub-Station Seven, blind them for exactly ninety seconds. Elves provide overwatch from the Kearny Marshes elevated roadway."

The larger Ogre-Kin twin, his face half-scarred tissue, laughed—a wet, rattling sound. "So we're supposed to waltz up to three armored trucks and pop the locks? That's heat, Cole. Heavy heat."

"You get paid double if it goes smooth. Triple if it gets messy." I let my aura expand slightly, felt the temperature drop around us. Watched them all instinctively shift their weight, hands moving closer to concealed weapons. "I need you there because you're the best at this. Not because you're heroes."

Knife's partner, a Street-Elf called Ghost, tapped his Nextel pager. The chirp echoed off the brick walls. "We've run harder scores than this. But cartel armor?" He shook his head. "They don't negotiate. They don't warn. They just put bodies in piles."

"They'll be expecting muscle," I said. "Not precision. We move like surgeons, not butchers."

Grimjaw stepped forward, crossed his arms. His jacket strained across his shoulders. "What's your angle here, Cole? Why risk Syndicate retaliation? You building an empire or something?"

"Just evening odds." I watched steam rise from the grate again, thought about County General ER where I'd left that ambulance driver with a pulse but no answers. Thought about Newark Bay and the things crawling beneath its surface. "Syndicate's been running North Jersey tech monopoly since 2001. Time someone reminded them they can bleed."

The missing-ear Goblin cracked her knuckles. "Alright then. What time do we move?"

"2300 tonight. Sub-Station Seven rendezvous point. Come alone, come ready." I folded the schematic back up, tucked it inside my coat. "And leave your personal bullshit at home. This is business."

The group started dispersing, melting back into the neon-lit shadows between the buildings. Grimjaw stayed behind for a moment, studying me with those dark, intelligent eyes.

"You really think thermite charges will crack armored plating?" he asked quietly.

"I think desperation makes good soldiers." I turned toward the alley exit. "Be there or don't. Doesn't matter to me either way."

Behind me, I heard him chuckle—that deep, rumbling sound of someone who'd survived too much to be scared anymore. "Wouldn't miss it for the world, friend. Wouldn't miss it."

I stepped out onto the South Side street, the cold night air biting at my face. Above us, the Pulaski Skyway stretched like a concrete artery, carrying its secrets through winter darkness. Tomorrow would bring fire and thunder and bodies in piles.

But first, I had one more stop to make. One more piece to put in place before the world burned.

* * *

The Undercity tunnels reeked of ozone and diesel fumes, a toxic cocktail that burned my sinuses as I descended the rust-streaked stairs from Kearny Avenue. My boots echoed against wet concrete, each footfall bouncing back through the darkness like a countdown.

"Three blocks north," one of the Street-Elves muttered, her fingers dancing across a cracked PalmPilot. Blue light washed over her angular features, making her look half-mechanical herself. "Convoy hits the chokepoint at 2300 hours. Two modified cargo-haulers, armor plating, rear-mounted plasma cannons."

I didn't need to see the screen. I could feel it—the metallic tang of blood-iron magic coiling in my veins like a spring wound too tight. My breath formed white clouds in the frigid air, and where each mist touched the ground, frost spread in crystalline patterns. The metahumans around me shifted their weight, checking weapons, adjusting armor. They felt it too. That dead-silent chill that meant Cole Reyes was thinking about breaking things.

Grimjaw stood at the perimeter, his massive frame blocking what little light filtered down from street level. His pistol hung loose in one scarred hand, barrel pointed up toward the tunnel ceiling. "You're sure about this, Reyes?" He didn't look at me when he spoke. Just kept his eyes on the shadows. "We're talking Black-Circuit Syndicate. Not some street gang pushing synth."

"Yeah." My voice came out flat, emotionless. Like reading numbers off a clipboard. "I'm sure."

The Elf looked up from her device, her pupils dilated wide in the dimness. "Sir, the arithmetic still doesn't add up. We take the Necro-Thermite, we have leverage. But they'll hunt us. Every crew, every—"

"I know the math," I cut her off, stepping closer. The temperature dropped noticeably as I moved through the group. "We're not keeping it. We're hijacking it. Stashing it. Then we use it to force a sit-down with their broker."

A low growl rumbled from somewhere deep in Grimjaw's chest. "That's suicide. You can't negotiate with those people."

"Watch me."

One of the Ogre-Kin stepped forward, his bulk making the tunnel feel smaller. His respiratory system whistled with each breath—damaged lung tissue from years of factory work. "My cousin works County General ER. Saw your brother last week. Said the kid needs a tube change, might need surgery. That's gonna cost more than any of us make in five years."

He paused, looking down at me with eyes that held too much understanding.

"So why are we doing this again?"

My jaw tightened. The frost spread faster now, creeping across the walls like veins of ice. "Because my brother needs a breathing tube. Anyone who has a problem with that can walk away right now."

Silence. Thick and absolute except for the distant hum of subway trains passing overhead.

No one moved.

Grimjaw exhaled slowly, then racked the slide on his pistol. The sound echoed like a gunshot in the confined space. "Alright, boys. Let's go hijack a war."

The Elf's fingers flew across the PalmPilot screen. "Chokepoint is here." She turned the device toward me. A holographic map flickered to life, showing the transit tunnels beneath Newark Bay. A red line traced the convoy route, stopping where three tunnels converged under an old viaduct. "Single access point. Two exits. If they come in hot, we'll have maybe ninety seconds before reinforcements."

"I can work with ninety seconds," I said quietly.

What I didn't say was what I'd do if those ninety seconds weren't enough. What I didn't mention was how deep the frost could bite when my blood-iron magic really opened up. How bone became brittle as glass when it touched that cold.

They didn't need to know those details yet.

"Move out," Grimjaw barked. "Reyes takes point. Elves on overwatch. Everyone else stays low until we're on top of them."

We descended deeper into the darkness, our footfalls swallowed by the hungry blackness of the Undercity. My breath misted ahead of us all, a ghost leading ghosts toward something that felt less like hope and more like a promise made to someone who wouldn't live long enough to collect.

## CHAPTER 3: THE 405 CONVOY RAID

The rain hits different at night in Neon Hollow.

I feel it before I see it—that copper-tang in the back of my throat that means the blood-iron's waking up. Grimjaw leans against the concrete barrier beside me, his mechanical jaw clicking rhythmically as he chews on nothing but ambient radiation. We're sheltered from the worst of it here under the viaduct, but the temperature's still dropping fast enough to make my breath fog.

"Wind's shifting northeast," he rumbles, his voice like gravel scraped across pavement. "Gonna freeze solid within the hour."

I don't answer. My attention's locked on the I-405 on-ramp three hundred yards south, where two stolen '99 Crown Vics sit angled across both lanes like a steel choke chain. Their high beams punch through the freezing drizzle in parallel columns of white, illuminating nothing but rain and the black mouth of the tunnel beyond.

The Razor-Wire Street-Elves are ghosts above us—perched on those concrete support pillars like gargoyles made of light-bending tech and bad intentions. Their optical-camo jackets render them practically invisible unless you know exactly where to look, which means they're already dead if anyone spots them. My gloved fingers grip each one with deliberate care—the last thing we need is for the rust to give way and send us plunging into what lies below. The air hits me first, a physical blow of sulfur and rot that makes my eyes water even behind the protective goggles.

"Christ," Grimjaw mutters behind me, his voice distorted by the respirator strapped across his muzzle. "Reminds me of that abattoir job in Irvington."

I don't respond. Words would waste breath in this place. Instead, I focus on the frost-cobalt current building in my palms—that familiar prickle of arctic energy that's become my constant companion since the Breach cracked open reality back in '99. The hex responds sluggishly here, fighting against the ambient heat rising from the sludge below. It's wrong. Everything about this place screams *wrong*.

Korg goes next, his massive frame having to turn sideways to fit through the maintenance hatch. The Ogre-Kin's waders slap against his tree-trunk thighs as he drops the final few feet onto something that squelches wetly beneath him. Then there's Thorne, quieter than Korg but no less imposing—a mountain of muscle and scar tissue who hasn't spoken a word since we suited up topside.

My boots hit the surface, and immediately I feel it: the unnatural warmth seeping through the thick rubber. This isn't normal groundwater. This is Sterling's legacy, the black-iron runoff from those illegal dumps, still radiating whatever cursed vitality the old bastard infused it with before the Feds finally shut him down.

The sodium-vapor emergency lights cast everything in diseased amber, creating pools of sickly illumination separated by chasms of absolute darkness. We stand in waist-deep liquid that should be frozen solid given the winter temperatures above ground, but instead radiates a damp heat that makes my skin crawl. The surface tension is all wrong—too viscous, too alive.

"Spread out," I command, my voice barely above a whisper despite knowing the sound won't carry far over the constant *drip-drip-drip* echoing off the corroded pipe walls. "We're looking for tracks, tunnels, anything that suggests habitation."

Grimjaw hefts his razor-wire net, testing its weight. "You sure these things are even real, boss? Could be just urban legend. Another one of Jersey's tall tales."

"The chemical analysis doesn't lie." I crouch low, examining the sludge near my feet. Even through the protective suit, I can feel my frost-cobalt hex working overtime, pushing back the toxic heavy metals trying to seep into my bloodstream. Tiny crystals of ice form around my fingers as I stir the muck. "Sterling dumped enough transmuted iron and alchemical binding agents down here to create something new. Something that's been living in these pipes for five years."

Korg's nostrils flare wide, his sensitive nose working overtime despite the respirator. "Smells like dead magic and bad meat, boss. We shouldn't be down here." His tail swishes nervously behind him, betraying anxiety beneath the brute exterior.

"We're already down here," Grimjaw snaps. "Might as well finish the job. We came to catch the devil's pets, so let's hunt."

The flickering lights choose that moment to die—the nearest one sputtering twice before plunging us into darkness broken only by our headlamps cutting weak beams into the gloom. The effect is immediate: the confined space feels smaller, more oppressive. And in that sudden silence after the light dies...

A sound.

Low. Chittering. Multiplied by dozens, maybe hundreds. It echoes from deeper within the tunnel system ahead of us, accompanied by frantic splashing that sends ripples across the sludge surface. Whatever made that noise, it's big. Or there are many of them.

"Did you hear—" Thorne starts, but I'm already moving forward, my frost-cobalt hex intensifying until a blue-white glow emanates from my palms, illuminating the tunnel with cold, clean light that pushes back the disease-yellow dark.

The tracks appear almost immediately—three-toed prints pressed into the hardened sludge at the tunnel's edge where the liquid level drops lower. They're fresh. Recent. And they're leading us exactly where we need to go.

"Found them," I call back, keeping my voice steady even as my heart hammers against my ribs. "Move up. Stay alert. These things were born in poison; they won't fight fair."

* * *

The beam of my flashlight cuts through the thick, oily miasma like a knife through warm lard. The cistern stretches before us—a massive concrete cathedral swallowed by darkness and filth. Water, if you can call that black, viscous shit water, sloshes ankle-deep across the floor. The stench hits me like a physical blow: ammonia, sulfur, burnt plastic, and something else. Something alive and wrong.

"Christ Almighty," Grimjaw mutters behind me, his voice muffled by the filter mask he'd finally put on. Smart man. "You sure about this?"

I don't answer. My eyes are adjusting to the gloom, picking out shapes clinging to the walls—hundreds of them, maybe thousands. They're motionless now, but I can feel their hunger radiating off them in waves. Can smell it cutting through the chemical soup.

They're bigger than any rats should be. Bigger than dogs, some of them. The Sludge-Run of 2000 had done more than just poison the groundwater. It had rewritten the goddamn genetic code of every living thing unlucky enough to drown in it.

My frost-cobalt magic coils around my fingers like living wire, its cold blue glow pushing back against the unnatural heat emanating from the sludge below our feet. The two forces war against each other in the confined space, creating pockets of steam that writhe like ghosts.

"Light 'em up," I say quietly.

Grimjaw switches on his tactical light. The sudden brightness reveals the nightmare in full detail. The rats' fur is gone, burned away or dissolved by the toxic runoff, leaving pink, glistening skin stretched tight over bodies twisted with tumors and growths of fused metal. Their teeth—*fuck*, their teeth—are jagged things of black iron, fused directly into their jaws. Some of them have too many. Some have none at all, replaced entirely by drills of crystallized sludge.

And they're waking up.

A high-pitched shriek pierces the air—the sound of a thousand rusty hinges being torn open simultaneously. Then another. And another, until the cistern rings with their chorus of madness.

"They see us!" one of the Ogre-Kin barks, raising his pipe.

The swarm descends.

I watch them come in slow motion—a wave of starving, mutated horrors pouring down the walls like waterfalls of bone and iron. Their claws scrape concrete. Their teeth chatter together in anticipation, sparks flying from the friction.

Grimjaw's MP5 coughs twice. Two razor-rats explode in bursts of red mist and black ichor. But for every one he drops, ten more pour into the gap.

"Hold your fire!" I shout. "We need them alive!"

"What?!"

But the Ogre-Kin are already swinging those massive pipes. *CRACK.* A rat's skull collapses like an eggshell. *THUMP.* Another goes flying, limbs splaying at impossible angles. The violence is immediate and brutal, each impact echoed by the wet crunch of breaking bone and the shower of blood and Sludge.

No, not just Sludge. That black-iron mixture that had turned these creatures into living weapons.

One breaks through toward me, its mouth open impossibly wide, rows of metal teeth gleaming in Grimjaw's light. Too fast to dodge. I slam my palm against the concrete floor, sending frost-cobalt magic racing across the surface in a spreading web. The rat hits it mid-leap, its feet touching the flash-frozen patch—

—and keeps running, the ice shattering beneath its weight but slowing it enough. My other hand comes up, catching it by the throat as it lunges for my face. Its breath washes over me: rotten meat, chemical burn, and something worse. Something that smells like time itself has gone sour.

"Got one," I grunt, shoving it toward Grimjaw. He snatches it out of the air with his free hand, the rat twisting and shrieking in his gauntleted grip. Those iron teeth spark off the metal plating.

"Bag it! We need a dozen of the most vicious ones!"

Another shriek cuts through the chaos—not from the rats. This one comes from deeper in the cistern, resonating up through the sludge itself. Low. Hungry. Large enough to make my stomach clench.

Grimjaw sees the look on my face even through the mask. "Cole?"

"We have maybe ninety seconds before—"

The water below us begins to churn.

* * *

The crown vic's engine dies with a cough, leaving us in silence punctuated only by the *tick-tick-ticking* of metal contracting in the January cold. I kill the headlights. Newark Bay stretches out behind us like a wound that won't close—a spreading stain of industrial decay bleeding into the darkness beyond Pylon #5.

"Fuck me sideways," Grimjaw mutters from the passenger seat, peeling off his welder's gloves with fingers that shake just slightly. "Remind me never to take you spelunking again."

I don't answer. My hands are already working the trunk release, the heavy *clunk* echoing through the empty alleyway. Steam rises from our clothes where sludge still clings—viscous black shit that reeks of chemical runoff and worse things. Things that shouldn't exist. The copper cauldron sits waiting, its rounded belly catching what little moonlight filters through the skeletal frames of rusted warehouse windows.

It's beautiful in an obscene way. Bram Kovacs didn't just machine this thing; he *forged* it. Three feet across, thick-walled copper hammered until it gleamed like dark honey, reinforced with iron bands at the rim and locked down with four heavy latches. Built to contain nightmares. Built for exactly this.

The sacks first. Three burlap sacks writhing with desperate hunger.

My frost-cobalt magic is still crackling along my knuckles, residual ice forming crystals on my fingertips as I reach for the first sack. The cold doesn't go away—not completely. It never does anymore. But the ambient heat radiating off those sludge-fed rats hits me like stepping into an oven after standing outside in winter. The temperature differential makes my teeth ache.

Grimjaw grabs the second sack without being asked. His troll metabolism runs hot anyway, but even his ears flatten against his skull as the unnatural warmth hits him. "Steroid-burn," he hisses through clenched tusks.

We upend the first sack together.

The razor-rats spill out in a chittering mass—twelve of them, maybe fifteen. Bigger than normal rats. *Way* bigger. Their fur has gone patchy, revealing skin underneath that glistens wet-black in the dim light. And their teeth... Christ, their teeth. Iron-gray fangs that curl backward, designed for tearing through things they shouldn't be able to tear through.

They hit the bottom of the cauldron with wet impacts, scrambling over each other, already starving after being separated from their colony. Already looking around for something to kill.

Two more sacks follow. Twenty-five rats total. Enough to make Sterling Manor's nights interesting.

The copper lid comes next—a matching masterpiece of engineering. Grimjaw and I lift it between us, sliding it down onto the rim. The iron latches engage with heavy, satisfying *clinks*.

Then we lock them. One by one. Four latches. Four locks.

The scratching starts immediately. Desperate, frantic clawing against copper walls. *Scritch-scritch-scritch*. A sound like fingernails on a blackboard, amplified a hundred times. The whole cauldron vibrates, shakes, rocks slightly as the trapped rats throw themselves at the sides.

I rest my palm flat against the lid.

The heat pulses through the copper even here. I can feel them moving inside—that frantic, hungry chaos. Can hear their teeth scraping against metal that their iron jaws can't penetrate. Not yet. Not unless Bram's math was wrong.

"Sterling called us rats in the gutters," Grimjaw says quietly. His eyes are fixed on the shaking cauldron, that grim smile still pulling at his tusks. "Let's see how he likes sharing a room with them."

My hand stays on the lid. The vibrations travel up my arm—faster now, more desperate. They know what's coming. Some animal instinct tells them they've been collected. Hunted.

"Tomorrow night," I say, my voice coming out flatter than I intended. Dead. Empty. "We balance the first scale."

Grimjaw nods once, sharp and decisive. No questions about what scales or what balancing. He knows. We both do.

I step back from the trunk as Grimjaw slams it shut. The sound echoes off the warehouse walls—*BANG*—followed immediately by an answering chorus of scratching and shrieking from inside the cauldron. A symphony of rage and hunger contained in four inches of copper and iron.

The crown vic's engine catches on the first turn of the key. Headlights sweep across broken glass littering the alley floor, casting long shadows that dance like dying things.

We drive toward Sterling Manor while behind us, locked in darkness, twenty-five starving razor-rats scream their fury into a copper prison built to carry them to hell.

The night swallows our headlight beams whole.

Behind us, the cauldron shakes violently, rattling its locks, testing its cage. The sound follows us down the empty streets of Neon Hollow—a promise of violence yet to come.

## CHAPTER 12: THE SLIVER OF JUSTICE

The break room reeks of stale coffee and ozone. Fluorescent tubes overhead sputter and die in sequence, casting the cramped space into stuttering intervals of cold white light. Red-optical grins, showing too many teeth. "And we bring the noise."

Fourteen minutes to get in, do what needs doing, and get out. In the middle of the goddamn night, with half of Jersey's supernatural underworld probably waiting for us to slip up.

Bram stops his lathe mid-cut. "Now? It's nearly 02:00."

The van waits in the lot, stolen plates glinting dully under the sodium lights. I climb in without hesitation, let the engine rumble to life beneath me. The dashboard clock reads 1:16 AM.

Time moves differently when you're hunting men who've sold themselves to worse things than death.

I pull onto the street, heading east toward Newark Bay. The Kearny Marshes lie ahead—swamps choked with something that used to be water before the breach turned it thick and wrong. Sometimes at night you can hear screaming from in there. Never anyone you know. Never anyone you'd want to.

But Sterling Manor sits beyond all that. Past the industrial wasteland, past where civilization decided it wasn't worth saving.

And we're going to burn it down.

The radio crackles static as I merge onto Route 280 North. My hands stay loose on the wheel even though my jaw is tight enough to crack teeth. Planning's half the battle but knowing the ground—that's what separates professionals from corpses.

By the time I reach the Pulaski Skyway overpass, my mind has already mapped three approaches and four exits. The BATC patrols Wraith mentioned show up right where he said they would—predictable machines running predictable patterns.

There's always an exploit. There's always a weakness.

You just have to be willing to walk through hell to find it.

## CHAPTER 13: THE WHITE-LILAC LIGHT

The cold bit through the van's insulation like teeth. I huddled over the scanner, my breath fogging the plastic controls in rhythmic puffs that matched the static-laced dispatches bleeding through the speakers. Twenty-three hundred hours. The sodium-vapor lamps along the I-95 overpass were just beginning their sickly yellow glow against the dying winter light.

"Unit seven-seven-nine, respond to mile marker forty-two. Ten-forty-five."

I jotted it down. Crown Vic, 1999 model, dark blue paint job already showing rust around the wheel wells if you knew where to look. Standard patrol pattern. Nothing unusual.

The BATC had gotten sloppy lately—or maybe I'd just gotten better at reading their patterns. Hard to tell sometimes when you spent as much time inside other people's heads as I did. The voices never quite left. Mara said it was a sickness. She probably wasn't wrong.

My fingers drummed against the steering wheel. The Sterling Manor hit was tomorrow night. All the pieces were falling into place, but something still felt off. Like I was missing a gear in the mechanism. I'd driven this route six times already, timing traffic flows, noting patrol shifts, cataloging every potential complication.

The windshield wipers scraped against a fresh sheet of gray snow. North Jersey winters didn't fuck around.

"Dispatch, this is Unit seven-seven-nine. On scene at forty-two. Looks clear."

I leaned back, letting the tension ease from my shoulders. Good. Clean. The way it should be.

Then the world turned white.

A screech—metal on metal, the sound of something massive being torn apart—and then BOOM. The overpass shuddered under me. My coffee mug jumped across the dashboard, scattering lukewarm shit all over the floor mats. Through the side window, orange flames were already climbing toward the sky, thick black smoke billowing out like some god's lungs exhaling poison.

"What the fuck?" I grabbed the scanner. "All units, what have we got? Mile marker forty-two?"

Static answered me first. Then: "Unknown MVA. Bus and tanker collision. Multiple injuries confirmed. All available units respond code three."

My stomach dropped. No no no no no.

"Dispatch, this is three-four-seven, en route from Route Seven. ETA four minutes."

"Three-four-seven, copy that. Multiple casualties. Fire suppression in progress."

I slammed my fist against the dash hard enough to feel the impact in my wrist. A fucking city bus. A fuel tanker. Of course. Because the universe had a sick sense of humor.

The sirens started building now—a chorus of wailing steel as every BATC unit within five miles burned rubber toward ground zero. I watched the lights flash past my position, red and blue strobes turning the falling snow into a disco for disaster.

And there it was. My exfil route. Choked solid with exactly the kind of emergency response I couldn't predict or control.

I reached for the modded radio again, twisting through frequencies until I found what I needed—the civilian traffic channels. If I could get eyes on alternate routes before they gridlocked completely...

"Attention motorists, I-95 northbound remains closed between exits forty-one and forty-three. Detour routing—"

A shadow moved in my peripheral vision.

I froze, hand still on the dial. Through the windshield, past the thick curtain of smoke and snow, I caught movement near the burning wreckage. An SUV—dark, unmarked, the kind of vehicle that meant money and authority even without the light bar. It skidded to a halt sideways, blocking two lanes like it owned them.

Then she stepped out.

Trench coat. Commander's boots. The silhouette cut sharp against the flames, and something in my chest tightened. Not fear. Recognition. The way your body remembers trauma before your mind catches up.

I grabbed the binoculars from the passenger seat, training them on the figure. She moved with purpose, striding directly toward the burning bus while every other first responder maintained safe distance. The heat would be intense enough to blister skin at twenty feet.

"Fuck," I whispered.

The binoculars picked up details: leather trench, black underneath, hair pulled back tight. And there—a flicker around her hands. Not the garish neon shit most metas threw around. Something else entirely. Pure white, tinged with violet. Like lilacs in frozen firelight.

My breath caught.

No. No fucking way.

But there it was again. That same signature. The one I'd seen three years ago when Mara dragged me half-dead out of Sub-Station Seven. The weave that shouldn't exist outside of theory texts and old women's fairy tales about things that were supposed to stay buried after 2000.

Clean. Uncorrupted. Impossible.

The radio crackled: "On-site command, this is medical response. We're getting reports of civilians trapped inside the bus. Fire suppression can't get close enough."

The white-lilac light flared brighter for just a second. Then the figure—Commander or whatever the hell she was—disappeared into the smoke.

I dropped the binoculars onto the seat beside me.

"This is bad," I said to the empty van. "This is really fucking bad."

Because if she could do what I thought she could do... if that weave meant what I remembered from the fragments Mara had shown me...

Then Sterling Manor wasn't just a hit anymore.

It was a target.

* * *

The cold was different today. Sharper. Like broken glass pressed into my bones.

I crouched behind the Jersey barrier on the Pulaski Skyway overpass, binoculars trained on the I-95 collision below. Two semis had jackknifed across both lanes—one spilling its load of machine parts, the other belching black diesel smoke that rolled thick as a funeral shroud across the winter afternoon. The highway was gridlocked, cars honking their frustration at being trapped.

Then I saw her.

Commander Mara Vance moved through the chaos like she owned it. No tactical team. No backup. Just her, in full BATC combat gear that should have screamed "military enforcement" but somehow read more like a physician's scrubs. She'd already cut through the passenger door of the burning bus—flames licking up the windows, fed by the ruptured fuel cells—and was pulling out a kid maybe seven years old, unconscious, smoke-stained.

My breath caught.

She didn't hesitate. Didn't check for reinforcements. Her hands moved in those precise patterns I recognized from the briefings—the weaving gestures—but what emerged wasn't the ash-gray static that would later define her work after the Sterling Manor incident. This was something else entirely.

Pure white-lilac light.

It erupted from her palms like a living thing, clean and sharp as starlight condensed to flame. Not the corrupted, hungry magic I'd been tracking in the Marshes. This was pristine. Untouched. Fresh cedar smoke cut through the acrid diesel stench, making my nostrils burn with something almost sweet.

The white-lilac weave wrapped around the child's chest like a second skin, pushing back flames that should have consumed them both. The fire *retreat*ed—actually backed away from her light, cowering. She carried the kid out like he weighed nothing, her boots crunching on broken glass, and the whole scene looked painted in high-contrast noir: her perfect geometry of power against the messy orange chaos of mundane destruction.

"Get the trauma-ambulance over here!" Her voice cut through the engine roar and crackle of flames. Commanding. But underneath it ran this current of pure, fierce compassion that made something twist in my chest. "I've stabilized his airway with a weave, but he needs O2 now! Kid's got first-degree burns on his hands—watch the circulation!"

The paramedics scrambled. The fire department arrived, their trucks screeching to a halt. Nobody questioned her presence. Nobody asked for credentials. They just moved because she told them to move, because that kind of certainty doesn't come from a badge—it comes from knowing exactly what you're capable of and having zero doubt about using it.

She turned toward the bus again, ready to go back in for more survivors, and her eyes swept the overpass.

For a fraction of a second—less than a heartbeat—I felt it.

Warmth.

Hearth-coal heat that shouldn't exist brushing against my frost-cobalt chill, making my fingers tingle where they gripped the binoculars. Mara Vance's gaze passed right over my position, those sharp green eyes missing nothing yet somehow seeing straight through me to whatever lay beyond the concrete barrier.

Then it was gone. She was already moving again, diving back into the burning wreckage without a second look at where I crouched frozen in my watcher's perch.

My breath came out white in the cold air. The frost-cobalt ache returned, worse than before, like she'd left ice in my veins where that brief warmth had touched.

Below, Mara Vance saved two more people before the firefighters finally got the blaze under control enough for proper extraction. Each rescue looked the same: that clean white-lilac light, the cedar smoke cutting the darkness, the precise medical efficiency of someone who'd done this a thousand times and would do it a thousand more.

I watched until the last survivor cleared the wreckage.

Watched as she stripped off her weave gloves with hands that only shook slightly from adrenaline.

Watched her decline a paramedic's offer to check *her* vitals with a dismissive wave that said "not my priority" loud and clear.

Then I backed away from the barrier and melted into the winter afternoon, carrying the image of that pure, uncorrupted magic burned into my mind like a brand.

The Commander from before Sterling Manor didn't wait for backup because she never needed it.

And I understood, suddenly, why what happened in 2005 tore her apart so completely.

She'd spent years building walls against exactly what the breach required her to become.

* * *

The binoculars lower from my eyes and I exhale through my nose, watching my breath cloud white in the cab of the van. Three-forty PM and the sun's already dying behind Newark Bay, bleeding orange and sick into the winter water.

My chest feels hollow where the Lead-Bismuth Geas Coil sits coiled tight against my sternum, its rhythmic ticking a metronome counting down to something I'm not ready to name. The cold from outside seeps through the windows despite the heater running—some days it's like the frost wants inside your bones, wants to be part of you.

Below me on the interstate overpass, the BATC transit perimeter is forming up. Yellow caution tape already stretched between Jersey barriers. Inside that containment zone, Mara's still working. Even at this distance, even through layers of glass and winter air, I can make out that particular quality her light has—that *purity*. White-lilac and clean as fresh snow, untouched by the grime that coats everything else in this county.

She saved someone today. Some drunk-ass idiot who ran his car into a concrete divider doing sixty. Should've been a body bag situation. Would've been, if she hadn't shown up and wove her threads into whatever broken parts needed fixing.

I'm planning to starve Sludge-rats on Julian Sterling.

The contrast hits me like ice water poured down my collar.

My hands rest on the steering wheel—cold enough that I can feel the chill through my gloves—and I flex my fingers. Blue-black iron-gall residue flares faintly at my knuckles where I'd been gripping too hard. That chemical ghost of violence. Of what I do when no one's watching but the Watching Party.

*Some people get to save the world,* I mutter to the empty van. My voice sounds wrong here, too loud. The phantom scent of cedar clings to everything inside—old habit, always keeping sage bundles under the seat—but it's fading now, losing its fight against the cold. *The rest of us just dig the graves.*

Mara's light doesn't come from a Geas Coil or some cursed artifact passed down through generations of Reaches. Doesn't cost anything except time and focus. She could walk away tomorrow and still be herself—still whole. Still capable of saving lives instead of ending them.

I couldn't.

Not anymore.

The Coil ticks its patient countdown. Somewhere deep in the machinery of my chest, lead-bismuth alloy remembers every kill, every wound, every drop of blood spilled by my hand since Father handed it over in County General ER. Seventeen years old and already marked as property. Already owned.

Below, the BATC team is establishing their outer cordon. Time to move. Can't risk getting pinned down with witnesses who might remember a white work van parked on an overpass with tinted windows and too much engine modification.

I turn the key and the engine sputters once before catching, diesel rumbling smooth as water over stones. Warm air finally kicks up from the vents, pushing back the frost that was trying to claim the cabin.

As I pull away—the van rolling smooth into traffic heading south—I catch one more glimpse in the rearview mirror. That white-lilac light still burning steady at ground zero of the accident scene. A beacon. A promise.

Something I'll never have and don't deserve.

My Nokia pager buzzes against my hip, sharp and insistent. The vibration cuts through the engine noise like a summons from something older than traffic laws and jurisdiction boundaries.

I reach for it with my left hand while steering with my right, pulling into the breakdown lane just long enough to check the display. Blind Gnome frequency broker—that's what passes for encrypted comms when you're dealing with the Watching Party.

*The drop. Check the drop. The Watching Party delivered.*

Fuck.

I pull back onto the highway, accelerating hard toward Kearny Marshes where they leave the packages. Always somewhere wet and forgotten. Somewhere the mundane world doesn't look close enough to see what crawls between the tall grass.

The Coil ticks its approval or disapproval—hard to say which anymore. Both feel the same these days.

Mara's light fades behind me in the mirrors, swallowed by distance and winter dusk.

And I drive toward another delivery from people who watch but never act, only provide. Tools for the necessary dark while someone else gets to be the light.

*Always someone else,* I think, pushing the accelerator down harder.

*Always someone else.*

## CHAPTER 14: THE WATCHING PARTY'S GIFT

The envelope weighs more than it should. I feel it in my palm even before I open my coat—dead weight, like holding someone's last breath in your hand.

Behind me, the defunct diner exhales its death-rattle: a compressor wheezing somewhere inside, the metallic groan of settling aluminum siding. The rusted utility box squats against brickwork that's seen better decades, maybe better centuries. Someone's tagged over the faded "EATS" sign with a crude sigil—three circles overlapping, the kind of thing that makes cops nervous and makes me think of things that used to live under bridges before we paved them all over.

I break the wax seal first.

It snaps clean, sharp as a promise. Black as motor oil, stamped with that fucking eye—the Watching Party's mark, all razor-wire edges and too-knowing geometry. My thumb comes away stained. I can still feel the heat where the wax had been pressed down, warm against whatever cold place this came from.

Inside is what I expected: an optical disc in a protective sleeve, and a folded note on paper so thin you could read through it if the light was right. Which it isn't. Never is in these parts after dark. Just sodium vapor bleeding orange through everything, turning blood into rust and making honest shadows impossible to trust.

The note reads: *Three favors. We have watched. You will pay. —Spire-shadows*

I pocket both items without reading further. Some messages are meant for privacy.

The walk back to the chop shop takes longer than it should. Kearny Marshes breathe fog across the highway median, and I can hear the Pulaski Skyway moaning its concrete song in the distance—that sound like an animal too big to die but never quite alive enough to matter. Car headlights sweep past in either direction, everyone hurrying somewhere they already regret going.

Local 405 comes into view like a ship emerging from smoke. Danny's tech bay windows glow electric blue against the industrial decay, his servers humming that deep rhythmic pulse that sounds almost like a heartbeat if you're not paying attention to the fact that it's mechanical and therefore has no soul.

Danny's Arcane Iron-Lung dominates the center of the bay, all brass pipes and copper coils connected to his little body by cables thick as garden hoses. The Telluric Core Compressor thrums steady behind him, powering whatever fragile thing keeps him alive while his mind walks the wire between worlds.

"Got it," I say.

He doesn't look up from his monitors. Fourteen years old and he looks older than me some days, or maybe younger—I've stopped being sure which is worse.

The Street-Elf runner, name of Vex, materializes from the shadows near the workbench. She's young too, maybe twenty, with those distinctive pointed ears barely visible under her dyed-black hair and eyes that reflect light wrong, like she's seeing in different spectrums. "The Watchers don't hand those out for free." Her voice carries that particular musical quality all Street-Elves have, even when they're trying to sound street. "You owe the Spire-shadows a favor now, Cole."

"I'll find something."

* * *

The chop shop reeks of ozone and motor oil, a combination that should be impossible but somehow defines this place. I'm hunched over Danny's shoulder as he works the ThinkPad like it's an extension of his nervous system, those scarred knuckles flying across keys worn smooth from years of frantic typing.

"Got it," he mutters, the word barely audible through the hiss of his Iron-Lung respirator. "Motherfucker used a commercial-grade cipher, but the Gnome didn't lie about the backdoor."

The blueprint shows redundant power systems, independent air filtration, even an emergency teleportation circle—the kind that burns out after single use but gets you clear of whatever nightmare you're hiding from.

"Whoever owns this place is expecting company," Danny observes. "Either that or they've got serious trust issues."

Escape routes are equally limited—a shaft leading up through three stories of building, another that terminates in what looks like a sealed maintenance tunnel.

"You seeing those guard rotations?" Danny highlights another data stream, and personnel files cascade across the screen.

High-Spire Elves. Of course. Their names scroll past—Shael'vithis, Thurin'duin, a dozen others with names that twist the tongue. Current assignments show them working in six-hour shifts, overlapping coverage that leaves no gaps. Twenty guards total, armed with weapons that glow with enchantments even in their photographic profiles.

"The manor itself has forty-seven rooms," Danny continues, somehow maintaining focus despite the sheer volume of information flooding the screen. "But only twelve show regular activity patterns. Your target's been spotted in the third-floor observatory twice this week." He glances at me, and there's something in his expression—concern maybe, or resignation. "This is suicide, Cole."

Maybe he's right. But I've already made my peace with that particular outcome.

"Print everything you can about the observatory," I say. "Guard schedules, architectural details, power conduits. If there's a way in, I need to see it."

Danny doesn't argue further. His fingers move across the keyboard, and a printer whirs to life in the corner, beginning to spit out page after page of schematics and data. The metallic tang of toner mixes with the ozone smell, sharp and electric.

Outside, Newark Bay churns under winter darkness. Somewhere beyond the chop shop walls, people are living normal lives, unaware that the High-Spire looms over them all like some sleeping god waiting to wake hungry.

The clock on Danny's monitor reads 8:47 PM.

Forty-six minutes until the exhaust vent cycle begins again.

I reach for the printouts as they emerge from the printer, each page a piece of the puzzle we're assembling. Sterling Manor wants to keep its secrets buried. Wants to stay pristine and untouchable behind wards and weapons and ancient magic.

But every fortress has cracks.

All I need to do is find one wide enough to slip through.

* * *

The chop shop smells like burnt coffee and desperation. Danny's tech bay is a cathedral of humming servers and blinking LED ghosts—all that digital static keeping watch while we plan our war.

Grimjaw looms over the ThinkPad, his scarred face half-lit by the pale glow of the screen. His breath fogs the air with each exhalation. Winter's got its teeth sunk deep into these Jersey nights, and it won't let go until spring bleeds out across the Kearny Marshes.

Military contract work, probably off-world before Julian Sterling scooped him up. The augmentation scars trace down his neck—expensive tech, the kind that lets you see in the dark and heal from wounds that'd kill a baseline human twice over.

"He dies first," I say flatly. My stylus taps against the PalmPilot's screen as I sketch patrol routes, timings, blind spots. The plastic clicks are sharp little gunshots in the frozen air.

Bram materializes beside me without warning, moving quiet as smoke. He's been doing that more lately—slipping through the shadows like he's forgetting he's supposed to be solid. "The telluric turrets run on a six-minute rotation cycle. Danny found the gap." His voice is soft, almost gentle. Doesn't match the way his fingers absently check the weight of the depleted-bismuth rounds in his pocket. Those slugs punch through magick like it's wet paper.

"Forty-five minutes," I mutter, highlighting the window in cold blue light. "Exhaust shaft access point B-7. We come in hot through the maintenance tunnels, bypass the turrets entirely. Bram takes the lead—you put down the guards before they can even think about casting shields. Grimjaw and I handle Thorne and his officers."

The pizza box on Danny's workbench is a fossil now, grease congealed into waxy sheets. Stale cheese smell mixed with ozone from the servers makes my stomach turn, but I don't look away from the schematics. Can't afford distractions when we're mapping out our route through hell.

"Thorne knows his shit," Grimjaw warns, not taking his eyes off the photo. "Commanded a squad of Blackwood mercenaries on Kepler Station. Saw them burn an entire civilian sector because some corporate exec wanted the land reassessed."

"That was their mistake," I say quietly. "Julian Sterling thinks money buys loyalty. Thinks augmentation makes you untouchable." The stylus clicks again. Route mapped. Timings locked. "Doesn't know what happens when someone isn't on the payroll."

Bram shifts beside me, leather jacket creaking. "The gear's ready. Thermite charges for the vault door. EMP grenades to fry their comms the second we breach. Danny wired up a few surprises if their security drones decide to get friendly."

Turn to face Grimjaw and Bram properly. The frost-cobalt focus settles over me like armor, cold and absolute. Everything else fades—the chop shop, the servers, the goddamn winter breathing down our necks. Just this moment, just these two, just the mission ahead.

"The intel is solid," I say. "We prep the gear tonight. We go tomorrow. 0200 hours. Newark Bay side, maintenance entrance behind the County General ER."

Grimjaw nods once, sharp and decisive. His brother's death has been weighing on him since we pulled him out of the Kearny Marshes, half-dead and full of rage. Tomorrow he gets his reckoning.

Bram just smiles that thin smile that doesn't reach his eyes anymore. He's already in his headspace—the quiet place where violence lives easy.

I pocket the drive and stand, joints popping from too many hours hunched over screens and schematics. "Get some rest if you can. Won't be much sleep after tonight."

As I move toward the door, Grimjaw's voice stops me: "Cole. After Thorne..."

"Yeah?"

"I want to be the one who breaks his clipboard."

I glance back at him, see the hunger in those dark eyes. Brother's death demanding payment. Fair enough.

"Your call," I say. "After he's dead, you break whatever the fuck you want."

## CHAPTER 15: BALANCING THE FIRST SCALE

The whetstone bites into the blood-iron with that distinctive *shhhk-shhhk*, throwing up sparks that hiss against the concrete floor like angry insects. I'm hunched over my workbench in the chop shop's back room, the overhead fluorescents flickering with each stroke—a faulty starter capacitor I never got around to replacing. Doesn't matter now anyway.

The blue-black residue runs down the pincer's edge, smoking faintly where it touches the metal. Gall and iron, bound with something older than language. This tool has bitten through more than just steel. I've felt it crunch through bone, through the crystalline shields those High-Spire fuckers wear like second skin. The metal remembers every kill. Sometimes at night, if the warehouse is quiet enough, I swear I can hear them whispering.

"Reyes."

Bram Kovacs appears in the doorway, backlit by the sodium lights of the main bay. He's hauling a crate that clinks heavy with promise—depleted-bismuth slugs, specially machined. Each one costs a fortune on the black market, but they're the only thing that reliably shatters elven bio-wards without requiring a full ritual circle.

"Here," he says, setting the crate down hard enough to rattle my tools. "Thirty rounds. That's all we could scrape together." His breath fogs in the cold air. Winter's been particularly vicious this year, seeping through the warehouse walls like a dying man's last gasp.

I wipe the pincers clean with an oily rag, then set them aside. Time for the real work now.

The MP5s are already laid out—both of them, the rune-etched twins Danny helped me salvage from that arms cache three years back. The runes glow dully under their layers of cerakote, feeding off the ambient magical field. They don't fire magic themselves, but they make bullets hungry for it. Makes them punch harder, tear deeper.

I pop the first magazine, start loading. The bismuth slugs are heavier than standard 9mm, painted a sickly gray-green. They smell wrong—metallic and slightly sweet, like infection. Each one goes into the magazine with a solid *clink*. Twenty-nine times. Thirty.

*Shhhk-shhhk-shhhk.*

Behind me, Bram's doing something at my workbench, organizing charges. I can hear him shuffling equipment, the squeak of his boots on concrete. The Motorola Razr sits open beside my elbow, its screen glowing blue in the dim light. Countdown timer running: **06:47:32**. Six hours until H-hour. Sterling Manor will be expecting us—or someone. The High-Spire never sleeps when there's vigilante activity in their territory.

"Packed it with the Necro-Thermite we stole," Bram says suddenly. "From that security depot in Secaucus." He holds up what looks like a baseball-sized chunk of clay, covered in adhesive and wires. "It'll melt through a bank vault, let alone a manor door. Give 'em hell, Cole."

I take the charge without looking at it, set it carefully in a padded case. The thermite isn't the problem—they'll feel it coming from miles away, heat signatures blooming like flowers before death. But sometimes you need to kick down doors that were never meant to open.

My gear is laid out on the bench—a tactical vest heavy with magazines, a medical kit that's mostly morphine and tourniquets, and beneath it all, nestled in custom-molded pockets, the telemetry wire connecting my Geas Coil to Danny's Iron-Lung. That's the secret weapon. The thing that lets me breathe in the toxic hell of the Kearny Marshes while I carry the weight of whatever dark shit is waiting for us inside Sterling Manor.

The smell hits me then—Hoppe's No. 9 gun solvent mixed with the copper tang of spent brass. It's the smell of preparation, of violence about to happen. My hands don't shake. They're steady as they've ever been. 

Somewhere in the loading bay, something metallic crashes to the floor.

"Special cargo's ready!" Grimjaw's voice carries over the hum of fluorescent lights. "Ready to move!"

I look at my work one last time. Everything's in place. The weapons are loaded, the charges packed, the connections secure. In six hours, we go through that door. In six hours, we find out what Sterling Manor is hiding.

In six hours, the first scale tips.

I close the Motorola Razr with a decisive *snap*, plunge the workshop back into relative silence except for my breathing and the distant sound of rats scurrying between the walls. Starving things, always hungry, gnawing at the foundation.

Just like us.

* * *

The loading bay reeks of oil and decay. I step around pools of something dark—motor fluid, maybe, though the way it shimmers under the flickering fluorescent light suggests otherwise. My breath makes clouds in air that should be warmer than this. The radiator's dead in my van, which sits like a sleeping beast in the shadows beyond the yellow striped lines.

Grimjaw's already there, hulking silhouette hunched over the copper cauldron. It's massive—a goddamn thing they'd use for brewing in some Old World foundry, all hammered metal and verdigris stains that glow sickly green under the lights. The hydraulic lift whines as he adjusts the height, and I hear them before I see anything else.

*The scratching.*

Christ. That sound gets inside your skull, works its way behind your eyes until you're sure you can feel little claws skittering across your brain. The copper vibrates with their movement, rhythmically, frantically. Starving. Always starving.

"They haven't eaten in three days," Grimjaw says without looking up. His voice is gravel and rust, the kind of tone that belongs to someone who's already made deals with demons and paid the price. "When we open that lid..."

He doesn't finish. Doesn't need to. We both know what happens when desperate things finally get fed after this long. I've seen what those razor-rats do to fresh meat. Seen what Sterling's alchemical tinkering turned them into back in '99—the year everything went sideways and the Sludge started remembering things it shouldn't.

I walk closer, boots echoing on concrete that's cracked and stained. Frost starts creeping along the edges of the loading dock, frost-cobalt magic bleeding through from whatever bleeds beneath Newark's bones. Can't stop it. Can only watch it spread like infection.

The smell hits me next. Not just the concentrated stench of Sludge—metallic and sweet-rot all at once—but something worse underneath. Hunger. Pure distilled hunger that radiates off the cauldron in waves thick enough to taste copper on my tongue.

"You sure about this?" Grimjaw asks, finally meeting my eyes. His own are yellowed around the edges where the mutation took hold, back when he was still trying to be a good cop instead of what he became. "Because once we load this... there's no coming back."

I don't answer. What's the point? We're past arguments now, past morals and ethics and whatever version of justice the courts pretend to uphold. Sterling Manor waits across the bay, all Gothic spires and corporate secrets wrapped in thorns that bleed frost. He's got people in there—innocent ones maybe, probably not, but innocent is relative when you're talking about men who traffic in souls.

Still, I feel it. The weight of what this makes me.

The copper cauldron sits there on its lift, scratching and squealing, full of starving fury brewed from the worst of what humanity can create. And I'm going to unleash it.

"Let's load it," I say.

Grimjaw nods, already moving to grab one end while I take the other. The metal's cold enough to burn through my gloves. Together we shift it onto the dolly, muscles straining against dead weight that somehow feels alive. Every scratch sends shivers up my spine like fingernails dragged down blackboards made of bone.

The van's rear doors gape open behind us—dark mouth waiting to swallow our vengeance whole. We maneuver the cauldron inside with careful movements that speak of old habits from before things went wrong. Before I quit being just another machinist who fixed engines and started fixing problems nobody else would touch.

Once it's positioned, Grimjaw starts securing the cargo ratchets. *Clack-clack* goes each strap as it locks into place around the copper pot. Metal biting metal. Finality.

"They'll tear him apart," Grimjaw mutters, fingers working the ratchet handles until his knuckles go white. "Every scrap. Nothing left."

I should feel something about that. Guilt or rage or regret. Instead there's just... emptiness where conscience used to be. A hollow space shaped like all the compromises I've made since 1999.

This is the Sliver of Justice.

Not mercy. Not redemption. Just balance weighed in blood and teeth and corporate stock options burned away by hunger so absolute it devours everything.

My reflection stares back at me from the frost-ice creeping across the van's windows—a ghost already haunted by what tomorrow will bring.

* * *

The loading bay reeks of gun oil and starving rats. I stand on an overturned crate, the only elevation in this concrete pit, and watch my people gather like ghosts at a wake.

Eleven of us. Maybe twelve if Grimjaw's contact actually shows.

The sodium-vapor lights flicker overhead—someone shot one out last week and nobody bothered fixing it. Shadows crawl across rust-streaked walls while cold seeps through cracks in the foundation. Winter's got its teeth sunk deep into North Jersey tonight, and the temperature keeps dropping.

I can feel it anyway. The frost-cobalt magic humming just beneath my skin, waiting. Already the air around me is turning crystalline.

"Listen up," I say.

My breath mists. So does everyone else's, but mine hangs longer—tiny ice crystals that catch the sickly orange light before dissolving into nothing.

Grimjaw leans against the far wall, his scarred face half-hidden in shadow. His pack—the three Ogre-Kin he brought from the docks—stand shoulder-to-shoulder behind him. Their red eyes glow faintly in the darkness. They don't blink. Never do when they're focused.

Bram's there too, checking mags with methodical precision. He doesn't look up when I speak, but his ears twitch. He's listening.

And then there are the Razor-Wire twins. Street-Elves, both of them, with those telltale silver tattoos crawling up their necks. They move like smoke, always have. Tonight they're still as statues, rifles cradled in arms that probably haven't stopped shaking since they saw what Sterling's goons did to the sandhogs.

"They won't know we're coming until we're already inside," I continue.

I step down from the crate. My boots hit concrete with a sound like finality.

"The front gates get breached at 02:37. That's when the guard shift changes—ten minutes of chaos while the High-Spire mercs argue about whose turn it is to patrol the east wing. We hit hard and fast during that window."

The twins nod in unison. Eerie shit, how synchronized they are.

"We split into three teams. Grimjaw takes his boys through the service tunnels—" I point toward the old sewer access point someone wisely blew open last month "—that'll drop you into the sub-labs. You know what to do down there." I meet each of his glowing eyes in turn. "Leave nothing standing."

Grimjaw's grin spreads slow across his face. "Pleasure doing business, Cole."

"Bram, you and me take the main staircase. Straight up through the foyer. The guards there are just muscle—untrained, overconfident, used to shooting scared civilians." My jaw tightens. "They won't know what hit them."

Bram finally looks up. His yellow eyes reflect the sodium light like a cat's. He nods once.

"That leaves Sterling Manor proper for the twins. You two go high—the rooftop access, third-floor balcony. You've got your cutting gear?" They pat their packs in response. "Good. Get inside, secure the upper floors. Anyone tries to flank us from above, you put them down permanent."

I rack the bolt on my MP5. The sound echoes off every surface—a sharp crack of final preparation.

"They're expecting an assault," I say quietly now, voice carrying despite its softness. "Sterling knows what he did. He knows we'd come eventually. But he thinks he's prepared. He thinks his money can buy enough firepower to stop us."

I walk among them, meeting every pair of eyes.

"He doesn't understand that this isn't about stopping us anymore. It's about consequences. About balance."

The frost-cobalt magic pulses harder now, responding to the weight of what's coming. The temperature drops another few degrees. Someone coughs—cold air catching in their throat.

"For the sandhogs who drowned in those sub-labs." My voice is dead-silent. Absolute. "For the Orcs who got burned alive because Julian Sterling decided some lives weren't worth the cost savings. For everyone on the South Side who ever looked up at his ivory tower and knew, with bone-deep certainty, that they were nothing but cattle to him."

Grimjaw's hand rests on his weapon. The twins have already raised theirs—silent agreement.

"We don't take prisoners tonight. We don't show mercy. We drag that son of a bitch out of whatever hole he crawls into when things get rough, and we introduce him personally to everything he's unleashed on this city."

Bram spits into the shadows.

"The first scale gets balanced tomorrow night," I finish. "All of it comes due."

Silence. Thick as smoke.

Then Grimjaw laughs—that low rumbling sound like grinding machinery—and racks his shotgun.

The Ogre-Kin follow suit. Then Bram. Then the twins.

A chorus of bolts clicking home. Magazines slamming into place. The metallic song of weapons being readied for war.

It echoes through the loading bay like a promise.

I smile.

Cut to black.
