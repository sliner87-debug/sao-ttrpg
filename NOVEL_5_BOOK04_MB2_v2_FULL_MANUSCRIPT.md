## CHAPTER 1: THE IRON-LUNG & THE GNOME-FORGED FREQUENCY

The stairs down are always colder than they should be. I feel it first in my forearms—that bone-deep chill that isn't quite natural, like walking through a meat locker that someone forgot to plug in. The gauze wrapped around my wrists has gone stiff with dried blood-iron residue, black against the gray concrete.

I'm dripping something onto the steps. Each footfall leaves a dark smear behind me, metallic and wrong-smelling. The burns from the catwalk haven't stopped weeping their frost-cobalt shit yet.

The sub-basement door hangs open at the bottom landing. Light spills out—not electric, but the warm orange glow of brass fixtures burning kerosene. I can hear him before I see him. Bram's been working on the manifold since before sunrise, probably won't stop until he passes out face-first in his coffee.

"Christ's machine-guns, Cole!" His voice echoes up the stairwell, gravelly as crushed asphalt. "You look like you went swimming in a goddamn foundry."

I push through the door. The chop-shop spreads out below—a warren of workbenches cluttered with disassembled engines, brass tubing stacked like cordwood, and the massive silhouette of the Arcane Iron-Lung taking up most of the center space. It's all glass bells and copper piping, Victorian mad science married to union-grade hydraulics. Danny's inside, visible through the reinforced panes, his chest rising and falling to the rhythm of those massive bellows.

Bram doesn't look up from where he's hunched over the pneumatic manifold, one arm deep in the machinery's guts. He's got his torque wrench in his free hand, turning it with the practiced ease of someone who's made this adjustment a thousand times. His apron is stained with oil that's been there so long it's started to crack like leather.

"Hand me that three-eighths socket," he says around the cigarette dangling from his lips. "This pressure-valve is tighter than a corporate auditor's ass."

I grab the socket from his tool tray—still hot to the touch—and pass it down without a word. My breath comes out in clouds. The temperature in here has dropped at least ten degrees since I walked in.

Bram catches sight of my forearms, eyes narrowing. "Jesus, Mary, and Joseph. What happened up there?"

* * *

The Toughbook's harsh LED glow throws Danny's pale face into sharp relief against the chop-shop's darkness. My brother's fingers dance across the mechanical keyboard inside the Iron-Lung's pressurized gauntlets, each keystroke precise despite the bulky rig. The decrypt program emits a high-pitched digital whine that grates against my teeth.

"These Gnome hardware-smiths are paranoid, Cole," Danny's synthesized voice crackles through the speakers. "It's a rolling 256-bit hex-cipher wrapped in a telluric feedback loop."

I lean over his shoulder, watching the cascade of code scroll down the screen. Bram hovers nearby, muttering about cipher-keys and overengineered security protocols. The old man's right—Gnomes always complicate things. But I slipped that transponder into Mara Vance's belt during our close-quarters clash at Pylon #2 for a reason.

My frost-cobalt blood-iron begins to hum beneath my skin, a familiar cold that drops the ambient temperature ten degrees. I channel the energy toward the laptop's overheating motherboard, feeling the machine's heat dissipate under my hex-work. The cooling fan kicks into overdrive with a mechanical shriek.

"Keep pushing, Danny. Stabilize the thermal spread."

Danny's fingers don't falter. "Already on it, hermano. Just need another thirty seconds before the frequency sync locks."

Bram shifts his weight, the floorboards creaking. "If this thing was planted by Syndicate tech, we're looking at military-grade encryption. Could take hours to crack."

"Not hours," I rumble, my voice dead-silent and low. "Minutes. These Gnomes work for both sides—they cut corners where it matters."

The PalmPilot in my hand displays the transponder's weak ping, tethered to the Toughbook's input port. Mara's signal flickers across the screen, moving through what looks like the Kearny Marshes. But there's something else—a secondary data stream bleeding into the primary channel.

My blood runs cold for reasons unrelated to the hex-work.

"Danny, switch the audio feed to full spectrum. Now."

The decrypt finishes with a sharp chime. The Toughbook's speakers erupt with static before clarifying into a live audio broadcast. My eyes narrow as two distinct conversations overlap:

"...should have moved the shipment off McCarter Highway by now..."

"...watching Pylon #2, but nothing on the catwalks..."

Syndicate. And BATC. On the same encrypted trunk line.

"You seeing this?" Bram's voice cuts through the revelation.

I am. And suddenly everything makes terrible sense. Mara Vance wasn't just Syndicate muscle working alone. She was a node in something larger—something that bridged rival factions through Gnome-brokered technology.

Danny's gauntleted hands freeze over the keyboard. "Cole... the cross-channel communication is active. They're coordinating."

Through me. Through the transponder I'd planted thinking I was tracking one woman.

* * *

The screen glows like a sickly sun in that basement, bathing our faces in that pale blue light. My knuckles ache from gripping the edge of the workbench. Three organizations. Three. Sharing infrastructure. Sharing tech. Sharing secrets while we're down here playing grab-ass with scrap metal and hex-work.

"Jesus fucking Christ," Danny breathes. His voice cracks. Kid's seen some shit in his short time working for me, but this? This is different. This is the kind of conspiracy that gets you disappeared, that makes you wake up in a puddle of your own blood somewhere near the Passaic River with no memory of how you got there.

Bram's already moving before I can process it. The *clack-clack* of him racking his MP5 echoes off the concrete walls like a church bell announcing the apocalypse. He drops his wrench—*clatter*—and brings the weapon up, muzzle pointed at nothing yet ready for everything. That rune-etched slide catches what little light there is, glowing faint blue-green.

Then it happens.

A sound from above. Not footsteps—not yet. Something heavier. The entire shop floor vibrates through my boots, rattling loose tools across every surface. Metal on metal. The reinforced garage door—that three-inch steel slab that's stopped more than one angry husband looking for his wife's boyfriend—shudders again. Harder this time.

*Thud-thud-thud.*

Someone's testing it. Testing the locks. Trying to see if they'll give.

Bram's already got the bolt thrown on the basement door behind us, backing toward Danny with that MP5 trained on the stairs. Smart kid. Getting between the threat and the asset.

"That ain't the cops, Reyes." Bram's whisper cuts through the ozone-thick air. "Cops knock with a warrant, not a battering ram."

My eyes find his. Hold. Then drift past him to where Danny stands white-faced against the Iron-Lung's main housing unit, hands raised like he's surrendering even though nobody's pointed a gun at him yet. Good. Should be scared. Everyone should be scared right now.

I reach down to my ankle holster without thinking, pulling out the sawed-off. The weight settles into my palm familiar as an old lover. Four rounds. Depleted-bismuth slugs. Each one costs more than most people make in a week, but money doesn't mean shit when you're facing something that can shrug off regular lead like it's paper.

The shells slide home with that perfect *snick-snick* sound. Cold. Heavy. Final.

Frost-cobalt blood-iron hexes flare through my veins—not from anger, not from fear, but from pure cold calculation. My breath fogs in front of me. Ten degrees colder, easy. The concrete floor might start weeping frost if I let it go much longer.

"Keep the lung running, Bram." My voice sounds wrong. Flat. Dead. Like someone else is speaking through my mouth. "I'll take out the trash."

I don't wait for acknowledgment. Already moving toward the stairs, boots silent on the grimy concrete. Each step up feels like wading through ice water. The sodium-vapor lights above bleed through the grate at street level, casting everything in that sick yellow glow that makes everyone look like corpses waiting to happen.

*Thud-thud-thud.*

They're working the door harder now. Impatient.

My hand finds the release latch. Rests there. Waiting.

Three seconds pass. Five. Ten.

Then I kick it hard and fast.

The garage door groans as it rolls upward—metal screaming against metal—and suddenly I'm staring into two massive silhouettes blocking out what little light bleeds down the alley. Sodium-vapor throws them into sharp relief: broad shoulders, heavy coats, faces lost in shadow.

Neither one moves yet.

Just stands there. Watching. Waiting to see what I'll do.

My finger rests alongside the trigger guard. Not on the trigger. Not yet.

"You boys lost?" My voice carries across the empty lot behind my shop. Echoes off brick walls. Comes back hollow-sounding, like a ghost asking the question instead of me.

They don't answer.

Instead, the bigger one reaches up and flips back his collar.

And that's when I see it—the glint of something metallic pinned there. Catches the sodium light just right.

A badge.

Fuck.

## CHAPTER 2: THE BROKER'S INQUISITORS

The sodium lights make everything look like it's rotting from the inside out—yellow-orange and sickly, casting long shadows that twist around the dumpsters like dying things trying to reach up from their graves.

I step into Chop-Shop Alley and I know immediately something's wrong.

The air tastes different. Metal. Sharp. Like blood on cold steel.

Two figures block the far end, massive even in the dim light. Ogre-Kin. Their breath comes out in clouds of vapor, each exhalation thick enough to see in the November night. The bigger one shifts his weight, leather creaking against armor underneath. His face is a ruin of old scars, jaw jutting out like he's chewing on concrete.

"Cole Reyes," the scarred one says, voice like gravel in a cement mixer. "We got business."

My boots touch the puddle near the alley entrance and it doesn't just reflect the streetlight—it *freezes*. Thin ice spreading outward in spider-web patterns, frost-cobalt bleeding through my blood-iron where I'm standing.

Sledge—because I know that name even before he speaks it—watches the ice but doesn't flinch. Professional. "Broker sent us. Says your aim was a little wide at Pylon #2. Says maybe you're going soft on the badge." He takes another step forward, the second Ogre-Kin moving to cut off any retreat toward the street. "Get in the trunk. Downtown for an audit."

The Crown Vic behind them is a '99, black, parked crooked across the curb. Trunk already popped. They planned this.

"I don't go anywhere with anyone unless they earned the right," I say, voice flat. Calm. "Tell the Broker my aim is exactly what it needs to be."

Sledge's smile shows too many teeth. "That ain't how this works, machinist. You work for the Syndicate now. That means you answer when we call."

I count the angles while we talk. The fire escape ladders bolted to the brick walls, third rung from the top rusted loose—I remember that from last spring. The blind spot between dumpster three and four. The way the sodium light leaves a darkness pool under the Crown Vic's chassis.

"Last chance," Sledge says, hand disappearing into his duster. When it comes out again, it's holding something heavy, rune-etched iron that gleams wet in the sickly orange light.

But I'm already weaving frost-cobalt through the iron of the fire escape above them, feeling the cold bite deep into the metal structure.

* * *

The air cracks like a whip.

I don't think—I *pull*. Blood-iron lances from my palms, invisible chains of frozen force that wrap around the bolted fire-escape above. The rusted iron groans, metal screaming against stone as I wrench it downward with everything I've got.

The second Ogre-Kin—twin to the one bleeding out behind the Crown Vic—has just enough time for eyes to go wide before three tons of industrial scrap crashes down on his skull. The impact is wet and final, bone crunching inside the cage of his helmet as the metal settles with a mechanical sigh.

No mercy. Not here. Not now.

"Cole!" Mara's voice carries from somewhere distant, muffled through concrete walls. She's still alive in there. Still fighting. That's all that matters.

Sledge breaks cover from behind the sedan's engine block, and he's faster than his brother was. His telluric armor—a swirling aura of brown earth-magic—crackles to life across his massive frame. He brings up some kind of compact submachine gun, spitting 9mm rounds in controlled bursts.

I slide left on asphalt slick with rain and blood, frost-cobalt spreading from my fingertips across the ground where I touch. Each bullet screams past, so close I can feel the heat of their passage even through my aura. One grazes my shoulder—doesn't even break skin, the kinetic energy deflected into ice crystals that shatter mid-air.

Then I'm up, shotgun coming around in a smooth arc. Time dilates. I see the rounds leaving his weapon, spinning lazily through space. See the sweat beading on Sledge's jaw beneath his helmet. See the pulse in his neck, frantic and animal.

He's aiming center mass when I fire.

The depleted-bismuth slug—loaded specially for occasions like this—roars from the barrel at hypersonic velocity. No sound, just the physical *impact* as it punches through his telluric shielding like paper. The magic disintegrates in a shower of dead sparks, and the slug continues straight through his chin, under the tongue, and out the back of his skull in a spray of grey matter and bone fragments.

Sledge drops his weapon. It clatters against wet pavement. He takes two steps forward before his legs give out, falling face-first into a spreading puddle of his own brains.

I don't wait. Can't wait. Already moving forward, boot planted against his spine as I drive him harder into the gutter. Once. Twice. Three times until something breaks wetly inside his chest cavity.

"Stay down," I snarl.

He's already gone, eyes glassy and fixed on nothing. His lips move once, twice, forming words in some guttural Orcish dialect that sounds like grinding machinery. Blood bubbles from his mouth. Then he goes still.

Behind me, the fire-escape groans again. I glance back—the section I ripped down has settled fully now, completely crushing what's left of his brother into the alley floor. Good enough.

My burner Motorola Razr starts vibrating in my jacket pocket. Insistent. Restricted caller ID flashing across the cracked screen: UNKNOWN NUMBER.

I ignore it.

The shotgun clicks empty as I rack another round—a standard 00 buck load this time, no exotic metals. Just raw stopping power. "Mara!" I shout toward the warehouse door. "How many more you got in there?"

No answer. Just silence, broken by distant sirens growing closer from the direction of McCarter Highway.

I check Sledge's body for weapons, find a belt knife—some kind of ceremonial blade with runes carved along the fuller—and pocket it. The submachine gun is too bulky, probably untraceable anyway. I leave it where it fell.

The phone vibrates again.

"This ain't over," I mutter, staring at the corpse. Someone sent these freaks after us. Someone who knows Mara's face, knows where we were headed. That means our position is compromised. Hard.

I need to get her out of here before backup arrives. Need to find out what Vane Thorne wanted bad enough to send two Ogre-Kin assassins. Need to—

The Razr buzzes a third time.

"Fuck it."

I pull it out, thumb hovering over the accept button. The display reads: URGENT CONTACT. Below that, a single line of text appears:

*WE KNOW WHAT YOU ARE, COLE REYES.*

The sirens are closer now. Red and blue lights beginning to flash across the far end of the alleyway.

My finger hovers.

Then presses.

The connection clicks live, and a voice—synthetic, distorted through some kind of digital scrambler—fills my ear:

"You've been selected for an evaluation. Your performance will determine your continued... existence."

I grip the phone tighter. "Who is this?"

But the line has already gone dead.

Behind me, something moves inside the warehouse. Not footfalls—too light for that. More like... breathing? No, not quite. Like metal shifting against metal.

I spin toward the sound, shotgun raised.

The door hangs open slightly now. A crack of darkness visible beyond it.

And from within that darkness comes another sound—the wet slap of flesh on concrete.

Multiple sets of footsteps.

"We got company," I whisper into the dead phone, then pocket it without waiting for response. "Mara, if you can hear me—stay back from the door."

* * *

The air tastes like ozone and dying stars.

I stand over what's left of Sledge, my breath misting in clouds that shouldn't exist in November heat. My body does this sometimes—drops everything around me to freezing when the iron gets thick in my veins. Frost spreads across the pavement like cancer spreading through lungs. Like the black tar Danny breathed into his before he was old enough to vote.

The chop-shop reeks of motor oil and rust, but underneath that is something worse. The copper smell of fresh blood mixing with the acrid bite of diesel exhaust. My knuckles are split open, embedded with fragments of teeth. Someone's teeth. Sledge's teeth. I don't remember hitting him that hard.

I crouch down, ignoring how my knee cracks—a sound like ice breaking on the Passaic River—and start patting down the corpse. Professional. Methodical. This is work now. Was work two minutes ago. The line blurs in ways that would make a priest weep.

No wallet. No ID. Smart. There's always a burner phone though. There's always one.

My fingers close around cold plastic in Sledge's jacket pocket. A Motorola Razr, matte black, probably paid for with cash from whatever job the Broker had him running. I flip it open. The screen glows sickly blue against the darkness of the alleyway, illuminating the frost creeping up the brick walls.

Three text messages. All from an unknown number.

*"Package delivered."*

*"Target acquired."*

*"The Watching Party thanks you."*

Well. Fuck.

I shove the phone in my own jacket and grab Sledge under the arms. He's dead weight in more than one sense of the word. Heavy. Warm blood soaking through my gloves as I drag him across the pavement toward the disposal chute—an industrial-sized vat of hydrofluoric acid that the chop-shop uses for "disposal." Efficient. Quiet. Permanent.

The bodies hit the ground with wet thuds. One, then two. Sledge and his partner, whose name I never bothered learning because names matter less when people choose to operate outside of them.

"Sorry," I mutter to no one. To myself. To Danny's ghost haunting my lungs along with everyone else's.

The hiss of the acid welcomes them like a lover. Chemical dissolution. Nothing left but stories and teeth fragments in my knuckles.

I stand there for a long moment after they've disappeared into the greenish foam, watching nothing. My breath plumes white in air that shouldn't be cold enough to do this. The Neon Hollow skyline presses down on me—a weight of glass and steel and too many secrets buried beneath asphalt and concrete.

The sliver of justice doctrine.

I call it that in my head sometimes when I need to sleep without screaming. Threefold cord: Penance, Revenge, and that third strand—frayed, thin, barely holding anything together—that I label Justice even though we both know it's a lie.

Penance is easy. Every punch I throw at someone who hurt someone else is penance for the times I didn't. For the nights I stayed home while monsters walked free. Revenge is honest work at least. Eye for an eye leaves everybody blind, sure, but blindness beats being food.

Justice? That's the sliver.

Never enough. It doesn't scrape the black iron out of Danny's lungs. Doesn't bring back the dead or un-break the broken or make anything right except for one small equation: someone who deserved pain felt pain instead of causing more pain somewhere else.

One sliver in a mountain of rot.

But here's what I tell myself in the freezing darkness of my own aura:

A sliver of justice for someone is infinitely better than no justice for anyone.

And tonight, Sledge had justice. Rough. Bloody. Final. The kind that makes frost spread across alleyways like death spreading through the city.

It will do for now.

Because what else am I going to do?

I pull out his burner phone again, scroll through contacts. Nothing useful—not even names, just numbers. But there's another text notification waiting:

*"The Broker is blind. The Watching Party sees. Check locker 402 at Blue Line transit hub."*

My jaw clenches so hard something pops in my skull.

The Broker. Blind.

That changes everything.

Or maybe nothing.

Either way—I've got a locker to check and a city full of people who need their slivers before they starve on empty scales.

I start walking toward McCarter Highway, leaving frost melting into bloodstains behind me like evidence dissolving with morning light.

## CHAPTER 3: THE WATCHING PARTY DEAD-DROP

The Crown Vic's engine dies with a final wheeze that echoes off the brick warehouses lining the South Side streets. I kill the headlights and sit there in the darkness, listening to rain drum against the roof. The sodium-vapor lamps outside paint everything in that sickly orange glow—the color of infection, the color of this whole damn city bleeding out into 2007.

My breath fogs the windshield. Not from cold. Never from cold anymore.

I slide out of the driver's seat, boots hitting pavement wet enough to mirror the neon sign of some all-night bodega three blocks down. The smell hits me immediately—ozone and motor oil and something darker underneath it all. Decay. Magic leaves a residue like roadkill leaves a stain.

Two blocks. That's what I give myself before someone notices I don't belong here, even if they can't put their finger on why. The kind of wrongness that makes your teeth ache.

The Blue Line Transit Hub rises up ahead like a tombstone made of concrete and bad architectural decisions. Some modernist fuck thought stacking slabs of gray misery would be beautiful once upon a time. He was wrong. The place looks like a bombed-out cathedral now, its arches spilling shadows thick as tar across the concourse.

A figure detaches itself from beneath one of those arches—a Goblin by the look of him, all twitchy limbs and eyes too big for his skull. Street-slang pours out of him like vomit: *"Spare a cred-chip, heavy? Just need a bump for the rail. My splice is screaming, man. Fucking screaming."*

I toss him a five without making eye contact. Don't want to know what he means by 'splice.' Don't want to know half the shit that goes down in this city anymore. The bill catches the light and he snatches it with fingers that shake like he's plugging into an electrical socket.

"Take a walk," I tell him, voice low. "This sector's about to get cold."

He's already backing away, muttering about how he didn't see nothing, hear nothing, but his feet are moving and that's what matters.

Locker 402 sits in the bank near the defunct ticketing booths. Rust has eaten through the metal like cancer, leaving weeping orange stains down the doors. I feed quarters into the mechanism—heavy clink, mechanical groan—and the door pops open with a hiss of displaced air.

Inside: black wax sealed tight around an envelope, resting on iron shavings that smell sharp enough to make my nose wrinkle. Warded, probably. Trapped maybe.

I pull out my PalmPilot, thumb scrolling through the detection protocols Mara insisted I learn. The screen glows blue-white in the darkness:

**NO ACTIVE MAGICAL TRAPS DETECTED**
**WARNING: RESIDUAL COLD-SOURCE SIGNATURE**

Of course there's frost-cobalt residue. Everything I touch these days gets coated in it eventually. I'm surprised it doesn't snow every time I take a piss at this point.

My fingers break the wax seal.

The envelope opens.

* * *

The envelope sits on my lap like a loaded weapon wrapped in wax paper. I'm parked under the skeletal canopy of the Blue Line overpass—rusted steel girders dripping condensation that freezes mid-air before shattering against the Crown Vic's hood. The heating system wheezes its last breath before dying completely, but I don't care. My hands are already numb from gripping the steering wheel.

I slide the knife under the wax seal.

It doesn't break clean. The blade catches, then the whole thing gives way with a wet crack that sounds too loud in the confined space. Black wax smears across my thumb as I peel back the flap. Inside: heavy parchment, the kind that costs more than most people make in a week, and a stack of glossy eight-by-tens held together by a rubber band.

The note first.

My fingers uncurl it slowly, and even in the dim dashboard light, I can read the handwriting—crisp, precise, with an old-fashioned flourish that makes my teeth ache:

*"Cole Reyes. Your vigilance has not gone unnoticed. We watch you watching them. Marcus Voss. High-spire corporate Elf. Shareholder in the grid that failed at Sub-Station Seven. He knows why those transformers blew. Why the union men died screaming. Why the air still smells like burnt copper three weeks later."*

I read it again. Then once more.

"Marcus fucking Voss," I say to the empty car, voice low and gravelly. The name tastes like rust in my mouth. Every South Sider knows Voss—a high-rise bloodsucker who buys up property while the neighborhoods rot around him. Owns half the Passaic River waterfront through shell companies and political bribes.

The photos spill into my lap.

Grainy night shots, probably taken with a long lens from across the shipyards. The drydock at Pylon #3—abandoned Union machinery, broken cranes hanging like nooses against the sky. But there's movement in these images. Men in tactical gear moving crates. Heavy stuff, the way they're grunting and straining under the weight. And beyond them, partially obscured but unmistakable: Marcus Voss himself, silk suit pristine despite the industrial filth, directing operations with hand signals.

Undocumented cargo. In the dead of night. While the union tries to figure out how their members ended up as grease stains on concrete walls.

My jaw clenches so hard something pops in my neck.

Mara's down in the BATC archives right now, digging through digital corpses for answers about Sub-Station Seven. If she finds what I think she'll find—the transaction records, the maintenance logs showing deliberate sabotage—that trail leads straight to Voss. Which means if he's moving tonight...

I grab the Nextel walkie-talkie from the passenger seat, thumb hovering over the push-to-talk button. Grimjaw's number is already pre-programmed. My Orcish second-in-command, currently running surveillance on the Kearny Marshes for any sign of those fucking Weavers.

*Thwack-thwack-thwack.* The windshield wipers beat an impatient rhythm as freezing rain streaks across the glass, distorting the skeletal cityscape beyond. Newark Bay lies somewhere out there in the darkness, its waters black as oil, reflecting nothing.

I hit talk.

"Grim," I say when his gruff voice crackles through, "we've got a situation at Pylon #3 Shipyards. Voss is making a move—tonight. Need you to pull back from the marsh and—"

Static cuts me off mid-sentence.

Not just static. A high-pitched whine that makes my ears ring and sets every nerve ending on fire. The photos scatter across the passenger seat, and for one horrifying moment, I swear I can see them *moving*, the figures in the grainy images turning their heads toward me despite the frozen shutter speed.

The frost-cobalt chill that never leaves me suddenly spikes like I've been dunked in dry ice. My breath comes out in clouds so thick they fog the inside of the windshield.

Something's here. Watching me watch them.

I slam my fist against the steering wheel, killing the radio with the impact. The dash lights flicker once, twice, then stabilize. When I look down at the scattered photos, they're just photos again. Dead paper. Motionless.

But the feeling remains—that crawling sensation between my shoulder blades that means eyes are on me.

*"We watch you watching them."*

Fucking right you do.

I scoop up the photographs with shaking fingers, stuffing them into my jacket pocket alongside the note. Marcus Voss thinks he's invisible up there in his glass tower, pulling strings while men die in the dark. Thinks his money buys him invisibility.

He's wrong.

And tonight, I'm going to make damn sure he understands what happens when the South Side finds you.

Even if it costs me this fucking chill running through my veins like liquid nitrogen.

Even if it means walking into whatever trap he's laid for anyone stupid enough to come looking.

The Crown Vic's engine turns over with a reluctant cough. Once. Twice. Then it roars to life, spewing black exhaust into the freezing rain-soaked night as I peel out from under the Blue Line overpass, heading south toward Passaic River.

Toward Pylon #3.

Toward Marcus Voss and whatever the hell he's hiding in those drydocks.

The windshield wipers keep time: *thwack-thwack-thwack.*

Like a countdown.

Like a heartbeat before violence.

* * *

The Nextel chirped twice at 2:47 AM—a harsh digital bark that cut through the dead air of the South Side streets. I was hunched behind the wheel of my Charger, parked under the skeletal shadow of the Pulaski Skyway's support columns, watching steam rise from the cracked pavement like ghosts from New Jersey's industrial past.

"Cole." Grimjaw's voice came through crackling and distorted, his usual rumble compressed by the cheap radio equipment. "What the fuck are you thinking?"

I keyed the mic without responding immediately. My breath hung in the air—frost-cobalt chill rolling off me in visible waves, condensing on the windshield. The temperature had dropped below freezing two hours ago, and the whole city felt like it was holding its breath.

"You awake?" Grimjaw tried again, and I could hear the edge of irritation underneath. Good. He needed to understand this wasn't a negotiation.

"Always awake," I said finally, keeping my voice low enough that it wouldn't carry beyond the car's interior. "Tunnel four. Get me access."

A long pause. In the distance, beyond the Newark Bay shoreline, I could see the navigation lights of container ships moving through the black water like luminescent insects. The Pylon #3 drydock sat dark against the horizon—a massive concrete monolith where Voss Maritime kept their decommissioned vessels rotting in saltwater graves.

"Boss, we're talking about corporate security. Voss has mercenary teams crawling all over that sector since Sub-Station Seven went tits-up." Grimjaw's accent was pure North Jersey—no fantasy affectations, no pretend medieval speech. Just an Orc who'd worked the docks for twenty years and knew how to swear in three languages, none of them Elvish. "You want me to get my guys killed over some hunch?"

"Not a hunch." I reached across to the passenger seat, fingers closing around cold steel. The shotgun magazines made satisfying metallic *clacks* as I slammed home fresh rounds—the sound of preparation, of purpose. "Just keep the perimeter clear. Four guys maximum. Eyes and ears only."

The smell inside the Charger was stale cigarette smoke and old coffee. My hex-reserves—blood-iron containers filled with concentrated magical energy, hidden under the floor mat—felt warm against my palm when I checked them. Three full vials. Enough for what I had planned.

"You're pulling my guys off third shift for a ghost hunt," Grimjaw rumbled back, his patience wearing thin. "Voss's corporate suits have that drydock locked up tighter than a drum. We'll pop the grating on tunnel four, but you're on your own once you're inside."

"That's all I need." I released the mic button and watched my breath crystallize in the air. The frost-cobalt chill wasn't just magic anymore—it was part of me now, seeping into everything I touched. The door handle felt like ice when I grabbed it, though I hadn't breathed on it.

I stepped out into the November darkness. The industrial seawall stretched before me—a cracked concrete barrier covered in graffiti tags and rust-streaked chain-link fencing. Beyond that, the entrance to the maintenance tunnels, half-hidden by fog rolling in from the bay.

My boots crunched on broken glass as I approached the water's edge. The smell hit me immediately—that distinctive combination of brine, diesel fuel, and chemical runoff that meant New Jersey waterways. Oil-slicked and freezing, probably contaminated with who knew what chemicals from decades of industrial abuse.

The Nextel chirped again.

"Tunnel four is open," Grimjaw said simply. "Thirty seconds window. Make it count."

I dropped the radio into my jacket pocket without responding. Thirty seconds was all I needed.

## CHAPTER 13: THE THREEFOLD BLADE AND THE WHITE ASH

## CHAPTER 14: THE WARD-CRACKER AND THE COURIER

## CHAPTER 15: LOADING THE DEPLETED BISMUTH

The air down here tastes different—cold and wet and sterile all at once, like breathing through a wound that's starting to heal but hasn't quite closed yet. I bypassed the main garage without a second glance. Didn't need to see the twisted metal sculptures I'd been working on. That was yesterday's work. Tonight belongs to something else entirely.

My boots hit the concrete steps leading down to the sub-basement with hollow *thunks* that echo off the damp stone walls. Each step feels heavier than the last, gravity pulling harder with every foot I descend. That's how it always is before. Before the violence. Before the blood.

The brass-and-glass metronome waits for me at the bottom.

It hums.

*Ka-thunk, hiss. Ka-thunk, hiss.*

Danny's Iron-Lung never stops, day or night. Never sleeps. Just keeps pumping filtered air into my little brother's ruined lungs while the runes pulse their sickly green light across his pale face. He's curled up inside the machine like he's sleeping in some kind of futuristic coffin made by gods who don't give a fuck about mercy.

I stop three feet away from it, hands hanging loose at my sides. My coat hangs open—I'm wearing the good vest tonight, the one with the pockets sewn deep enough to hold what I'll need later. But right now those pockets are empty except for weight and memory.

The condensation on the inner glass catches the pulsing light, making patterns that look almost beautiful if you squint. Danny's breath fogs that same glass from the inside, each exhalation leaving its ghost behind.

"Found the third one, kid," I say quietly. My voice sounds wrong down here—too loud, too raw against all this silence and machinery. "The guy who signed the work order for the sludge. I'm gonna balance the ledger tonight."

The Iron-Lung keeps its steady rhythm. Doesn't care about promises. Doesn't care about revenge or justice or any of the pretty words people use when they want something bad enough to kill for.

*Ka-thunk, hiss.*

My hand rises without me telling it to. Fingertips touch the warm glass. The heat from the runes transfers through, just barely enough to feel. On the other side of this barrier, Danny's chest rises and falls beneath the sterile white gown we put him in three months ago. Three months since the Passaic River decided it wanted to keep him forever, until I dragged him back out of its black embrace with nothing but bare hands and rage.

He looks younger asleep. Looks like he did before everything went sideways, before the company decided our neighborhood wasn't worth the cleanup costs. Before Voss and his suits decided an eighteen-year-old machinist's apprentice was a reasonable price to pay for quarterly profits.

I watch the condensation drip down the glass. Each droplet races toward the bottom, merging into larger ones, gaining momentum as they fall.

Killing Voss won't heal these lungs.

The thought arrives clean and sharp as broken glass. True. Undeniable. I could put six bullets through the son of a bitch's skull at point-blank range—I've been planning exactly where those holes should go for weeks now—and Danny would still be inside this machine. Still breathing recycled air. Still trapped in a brass-and-glass prison that costs more every month than I make working two jobs.

It's fundamentally not enough.

But then I look at Danny's face—at how peaceful he looks when he's sleeping, how the lines around his mouth have smoothed out—and something twists hard and deep in my chest cavity. My jaw clenches so tight my teeth ache.

Not enough isn't the same thing as nothing at all.

The Iron-Lung hums its eternal song: *ka-thunk, hiss.*

"Yeah," I whisper against the glass, so quiet I can barely hear myself. "I know."

My reflection stares back at me from the dark surface—a ghost made of light and glass and desperation. Behind that ghost, Danny sleeps on. The green runes pulse. The machine breathes for him because he can't do it himself anymore. Because some men in Neon Hollow decided profit margins mattered more than boys with dreams of building things instead of breaking them.

I straighten up, pulling my hand away from the glass. Cold air hits my fingertips where warmth had been. The separation feels like loss.

"I'm gonna murder every last one of them," I say, louder now. Clearer. Like a vow. "Every executive who signed off. Every engineer who calculated the cost-benefit analysis. Every security guard who stood watch while the sludge flushed into our water supply." 

The words taste like copper and ozone.

"They're all going to pay, Danny. That's a promise."

*Ka-thunk, hiss.*

The heavy steel door above us grinds open with a shriek of tortured metal. I don't turn immediately. Let whoever's coming take their first few steps down. When I finally look over my shoulder, Bram Kovacs stands in the doorway, silhouetted against the garage light behind him. He's holding something—steel lockbox, reinforced, probably weighs forty pounds easy. His face looks wrong somehow. Too pale. Eyes too wide in the half-light.

"Reyes," he says, voice cracking on my name. "You need to see this."

The way he says it—urgent but scared, like he's carrying a bomb instead of a box—I know whatever's inside has just complicated everything already complicated about tonight.

I turn fully now, facing him. Behind me, the Iron-Lung keeps its metronome beating. *Ka-thunk, hiss.* The sound follows me up those concrete steps, counting down seconds until violence, counting down Danny's borrowed time.

Whatever's in that box just made the ledger longer.

* * *

The scent hits me first—that familiar cocktail of machine oil, ozone from the welding torch still cooling somewhere in the back, and the sharp chemical tang of Hoppe's No. 9 solvent. Underneath it all, stale coffee and cigarette smoke clinging to every surface like a ghost that won't be exorcised.

Bram Kovacs is already at the workbench when I come up from the sub-basement, his broad shoulders hunched under that same stained canvas jacket he's been wearing since '03. The fluorescent light overhead flickers—one of those industrial buzzers that never quite dies but never quite lives either—and casts everything in a sickly yellow glow that makes the grease stains on his sleeves look almost black.

"Got what you need," he says without looking up, his voice that thick North Jersey rumble that sounds like grinding gears. He sets the lockbox down with a solid thunk on the scarred oak workbench, the kind of table that's seen a hundred years of real work, nothing fancy.

I don't answer right away. Just stand there for a second, letting my eyes adjust to the light after the dim red emergency bulbs of the lower level. My frost-cobalt wards hum low and steady against my ribs, that familiar weight I've gotten used to over the last few weeks. Mara's white-lilac scent lingers faintly in my nose—that memory-tether she taught me to hold onto—but right now, everything else is business.

"You get the specs right?" I ask finally, moving closer to the bench. The lockbox is military-grade, brushed steel, the kind that costs more than most people make in a month.

Bram snorts, turning the combination locks with practiced ease. *Click-click-whirrr.* "Please. You think I'd fuck this up?" He lifts the lid.

Inside, nestled in custom-cut foam, are rows of shotgun slugs unlike anything you'd find at any gun show or black market stall. Each one gleams dully under the flickering light, the bismuth alloy catching just enough to show its metallic sheen. But it's not the polish that gets your attention—it's the weight.

I pick one up. Christ. It sits heavy in my palm, dense as lead but different somehow, like holding a chunk of something alive instead of just metal. Bram milled these things to 0.02mm tolerance. That's precision that belongs in a clean room, not a chop shop in Kearny.

"Depleted bismuth core," Bram says, watching me turn the slug over in my fingers. "Wrapped in a telluric frequency casing. You fire this into one of those High-Spire kinetic shields Voss runs on his estate? The reaction'll punch through like it ain't even there."

I roll the slug between my thumb and forefinger. Feel the weight settle into my bones. This isn't street-level gear. This is the kind of hardware that makes statements.

"And the recoil?"

"Will snap your shoulder clean off if you ain't braced." Bram's eyes meet mine, dead serious. No bullshit, no dramatic speeches about destiny or honor or any of that fantasy crap. Just facts delivered straight from years of experience and a union card that predates half the syndicates running North Jersey.

"I'll brace," I say simply.

He grunts approval and starts counting out the shells, arranging them on the bench with the careful precision of someone who takes pride in their work, even when that work involves making tools designed specifically to kill people with magic defenses.

The lockbox holds twenty rounds. I watch him lay them out one by one until all twenty sit in a neat row under that shitty fluorescent light. Outside, thunder rumbles across Newark Bay—far enough away still that we're not worried about flooding down below, but close enough to know the storm's moving this way.

"How much?" I ask.

Bram names a price that would make most guys sweat. I don't flinch. Pull three strapped stacks from my jacket pocket—the Syndicate cash, still warm from whatever bank they'd just hit—and set them next to the shells.

He weighs them in his hand without counting. "Pleasure doing business."

No lie, no drama. Just the transaction. That's how it works with Bram. You get what you pay for, and there are no strings attached after the money changes hands.

I start loading my shotgun then. The mechanical *clack-clack* of each shell sliding into the magazine tube echoes off the concrete walls of the garage, loud as a clock ticking down to something terrible. One. Two. Three. Four. Each slug heavier than the last somehow, like I'm loading more than just ammunition.

Thunder crashes again, closer now. Rain begins hammering against the garage door windows.

By the time I seat the twenty-third round—which stays outside the chamber because I never run hot—I can smell the ozone from the lightning strikes mixing with the oil and solvent. The wards around my chest pulse once, twice, like a heartbeat anticipating violence.

"Good luck," Bram says quietly behind me.

I don't turn around. Just slam the magazine home with a final decisive click.

"Don't need luck," I tell him. "Just precision."

* * *

The basement air tastes like ozone and gunpowder, familiar and clean compared to the rot that's been festering in my bones since I first laid eyes on the Morvain Estate. I run my palm along the shotgun's pump-action, feeling the cold steel through my oil-stained gloves. Metal. Real. Solid.

Outside the sub-basement windows, something massive cracks across the sky—lightning that turns night into day for one impossible second, revealing the Passaic River churning black beneath the Pulaski Skyway. Rain hammers down so hard it sounds like bullets on corrugated metal.

I rack the Remington 870. The sound echoes off concrete walls, sharp and final. The first depleted-bismuth round slides home with a *click* that feels heavier than lead. Good. Let it be heavy. Let it carry the weight of what needs doing tonight.

My frost-cobalt wards are coiled tight in my chest, humming against my ribs like frozen wires. They know. They've been singing their warning song for three days now, ever since I found that fucking journal hidden in the estate's eastern wing. Ever since I read about the Third Strand.

I move to the garage door mechanism—a rusted chain pull that shouldn't work but does because nothing in this town follows normal rules anymore. My boots squelch in oil puddles on the concrete floor. The Motorola Razr sits heavy in my jacket pocket, its blue glow already bleeding through the leather when I reach for it.

The screen illuminates my face as I flip it open: Mara's number, already pulled up from memory. Her encrypted contact, listed only as "M."

I type slowly, methodically:

*"Head #3 is inside the Morvain Estate. Tonight we collect the third strand of the cord."*

Send.

The phone snaps shut with a *clack* that makes me grimace. Too loud. Everything feels too loud tonight—the rain, the blood rushing in my ears, the prophecy that won't leave me alone even in sleep.

The Threefold Iron Blade.

That's what the journal called me. What the old-timers at the South Side machinist shop whispered when they thought I wasn't listening. Three strands. Three heads. Three nights of killing that started with Mara and ended—tonight—with whatever nightmare's waiting inside those Gothic spires overlooking Newark Bay.

Another lightning strike. Closer this time. The flash paints everything in cobalt blue—the truck, the tools scattered across workbenches, the burlap sack in the corner that definitely wasn't there yesterday and that I'm not thinking about.

I shoulder the shotgun. It settles into place like it's part of my skeleton now.

Outside, the storm waits.

Inside, my wards coil tighter, ready to spring.

The third strand. The final piece of the cord that binds this whole sick fucking city together—or tears it apart.

I push through the garage door into driving rain that hits like needles, cold enough to make frost-cobalt wards sing their approval. The truck engine roars on the first turn of the key, drowning out thunder, drowning out doubt.

North. Toward Neon Hollow. Toward the Morvain Estate.

Tonight we end this.
