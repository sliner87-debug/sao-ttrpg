## CHAPTER 1: CHAPTER 1

The basement smells like blood and copper.

I stand over Danny's bed, hands wrapped around the cold brass casing of the Iron-Lung, feeling the rhythmic pulse of the compressor through my palms. *Ca-chunk. Shhhhh.* The sound has been my companion for three years now—the mechanical heartbeat that keeps my little brother alive while the arcane sickness eats him from the inside out.

Amber light spills from the sodium vapor lamp overhead, casting everything in that sickly yellow glow that makes the glass cylinders look like they're filled with honey instead of the black sludge that's crystallizing in Danny's chest. I check the pressure gauges—still holding at 28 psi, right where it needs to be. The brass fittings are tight. No leaks. For now.

"Hold on, kiddo," I whisper, watching his ribs expand beneath the weight of all that machinery. Sixteen years old and hooked up to something that looks like it belongs in a Victorian asylum. "Just hold on a little longer."

The Panasonic Toughbook on the side table blinks green, its screen showing the EKG readout—a steady mountain range of peaks and valleys. Heartbeat normal. Lung function stable. For now. The diagnostic software I pirated from County General runs its scan, and the numbers don't lie: the crystallization is slow, but it's relentless. Like rust, like rot, like every other thing in this godforsaken South Side that just keeps eating away until there's nothing left.

But tonight, I'm not here to watch him die slowly.

Tonight, I'm here to make sure some people die fast.

I turn away from the bed, my boots scuffing against the concrete floor. The workbench is cluttered with tools—wrenches, pliers, a blowtorch, things I've cobbled together over the years. Most of them aren't legal. None of them are safe. But when you're fighting monsters that don't follow the rules, you can't exactly bring a knife to a gunfight.

Except tonight, I am bringing knives.

Three of them, sharpened until they could split a hair. Coated in iron-gall residue mixed with bismuth powder—the kind of alchemical cocktail that burns through metahuman flesh like paper. I found the recipe in an old workshop manual Danny gave me back in '02, before everything went sideways. Before he took a lungful of wrong-air down by the marshes and brought home something that shouldn't exist.

*Ca-chunk. Shhhhh.*

The Iron-Lung keeps its rhythm while I work, each compression forcing sterilized air past the blockages in his chest, keeping him alive even as the sickness spreads. I load the knives into a custom sheath strapped to my forearm, then check the brass knuckles in my coat pocket. Loaded with tungsten carbide spikes. Good for cracking bone. Better for cracking skulls.

Outside, somewhere beyond the sealed basement door, I hear sirens. Not close—maybe a few miles off. Probably Sub-Station Seven burning again. There's always something burning on this side of Newark Bay. Always smoke, always fire, always someone screaming about the things that crawl out of the marshes after dark.

I lean against the workbench, closing my eyes. The cold from the concrete wall seeps through my jacket, but it doesn't touch the frost-cobalt hex humming just beneath my skin. That came after Danny got sick—like my body was trying to compensate for what he'd lost. Blood-iron metallurgy turned to dead-silent zero-remorse. The healers at County General said it wasn't natural. Said I should be dead or transformed.

I told them to fuck off.

"Danny," I say quietly, turning back to face him. His eyes are closed, lashes dark against his pale cheeks. "You remember what I told you about the Threefold Cord?"

His chest rises. Falls. The Iron-Lung *ca-chunks* its answer.

"Penance for what we couldn't stop. Revenge for what they took from us. And a sliver of justice—however sharp we can make it." I tap my knuckles against the brass casing, feeling the vibration travel through the pipes and valves. "Tonight isn't going to scrape the black iron out of your lungs. Won't fix what happened down by Pulaski Skyway when those bastards decided your life was cheaper than their cargo."

The temperature drops another degree around me. Ozone and chilled copper fill my nostrils.

"But it's better than letting them breathe tomorrow."

A sound echoes down from the stairwell above—heavy boots on concrete. Rhythmic. Deliberate. Dwarven steel-toed boots by the sound of them, descending with purpose.

I reach for the door handle, fingers closing around cold metal.

* * *

The basement reeked of machine oil and old sweat. I was elbow-deep in the guts of a disassembled M4 when I heard the familiar scrape of boots on concrete steps. Bram Kovacs descended into my workshop like he owned it—which, technically, part of it he did. Local 405 had fronted me the lease on this South Side chop-shop back in '03.

"Reyes." His voice cut through the whine of my angle grinder. I didn't look up from the trigger assembly I was working on.

"The package is here," Bram said. He dropped something heavy onto my workbench. The canvas duffel hit with a metallic thud that made my teeth ache.

I killed the grinder. The sudden silence rang in my ears, but beneath it, I caught the distant wail of sirens—multiple vehicles, moving fast to the north. Orange light flickered across the small basement window near the ceiling, casting everything in sickly flames.

Sub-Station Seven was burning.

Bram's shadow stretched long across the concrete floor. He lit a cigarette, the flare illuminating his scarred face, the crooked nose from a press accident back in '98. "North grid's lighting up like a Roman candle," he said around the smoke. "Scanner says Sub-Station Seven just blew its primary busbars. You sure we don't pivot?"

My hands were already pulling open the duffel. Inside, nestled in foam cutouts, were rows of depleted-bismuth slugs. Each one gleamed dull gray under the fluorescent lights, etched with armor-piercing runes that would shatter High-Spire kinetic wards like glass. Custom machined. Perfect weight distribution. Exactly what we needed for Sterling Manor tonight.

The ambient temperature dropped ten degrees as my blood-iron hex locked into frost-cobalt. Ozone and chilled copper filled my nostrils.

"Sterling's drinking champagne while the city burns," I said flatly. "We stick to the schedule."

Bram watched me load the slugs into my rune-etched MP5 magazines. His expression didn't change, but he stubbed out his cigarette against the workbench leg. "Grimjaw's team is ready. Just say when."

I racked the charging handle. The weapon hummed faintly, blue-black iron-gall residue coating the barrel. "Signal him. We move in thirty."

* * *

The trunk slammed shut with that satisfying metallic *thunk* that meant weight—serious weight. I wiped the sweat from my brow despite the November chill crawling up through my boots from the concrete basement floor.

"Christ, Cole," Bram grunted beside me, shoulder-checking one of the tactical bags. "You packing lead bricks in these things?"

I didn't answer. Couldn't. My mouth tasted like copper pennies and old blood. The hex was sitting heavy in my marrow now, that dead-silent frost-cobalt state where everything sharpens into brutal clarity. Ten degrees colder than it should be around me, breathing out little clouds that smelled wrong—ozone and iron-gall residue sizzling in the air.

We hauled the gear up three flights of rust-stained stairs, through the chop-shop's main floor with its disassembled engines scattered like mechanical corpses under yellow work lights. Out through the back exit into an alley that reeked of rotting garbage and puddles reflecting neon signs from Neon Hollow six blocks over.

My '99 Crown Victoria sat waiting, black and anonymous under a flickering streetlight. I popped the trunk with the fob, and there it was—my mobile armory. Spare mags stacked neat as cordwood. Breaching charges wrapped in bubble wrap. Zip-ties coiled like sleeping snakes. And that heavy copper pot, gleaming dull orange under the sodium-vapor glow. Foreshadowing something bad. Always is.

Bram whistled low. "Jesus H. Christ."

I started loading. Methodical. Each item finding its place like pieces on a board game where losing meant dying. The weight settled into the springs, making them compress just right. Perfect balance for what we were about to do.

Slammed the trunk shut.

The sound echoed off the brick walls.

I circled around to the driver's side, yanking open the door hard enough to make the hinges complain. The interior still smelled like burnt coffee and leather conditioner—the last honest thing in my life. I reached behind the seats, pulled out the Sony MiniDisc player, plugged it into the cassette adapter with practiced efficiency.

*Click-whirrrr.*

Static. Then Tom Waits' gravel-throat voice crawled through the speakers: *"...walk down the block to the old pool hall..."*

"Christ," Bram muttered again, sliding into the shotgun seat. He had his modified PalmPilot already in hand, thumb flying across the screen as he checked the frequencies hacked by those UnderCity Gnomes. Their code-work was beautiful when you knew how to read it—all those little green numbers cascading like water.

"The Gnome boys got the Watching Party guard codes locked," he said, eyes never leaving the screen. "Perimeter is blind for exactly four minutes. Tops."

Four minutes. Four hundred seconds to get inside Sterling Manor's grounds. To find Vance before she finished whatever hellstorm she'd started at Sub-Station Seven.

I threw the Crown Vic into drive. The engine roared—a deep, guttural sound that made the whole car shake. My knuckles went white on the wheel.

"We're burning daylight," I said.

Tires squealed against wet asphalt, scattering trash and puddle-water in twin sprayers behind us. The speedometer needle climbed past forty, fifty, sixty as we arrowed toward the High-Spire outskirts. Neon Hollow fell away behind us, its garish glow replaced by the sick orange reflection of Sub-Station Seven in the rearview mirror.

Burning.

Always fucking burning.

I pushed harder on the accelerator.

The road ahead was dark now—no streetlights out here where the money lived. Just blacktop and the occasional flicker from a distant security light. Then the trees started appearing, manicured and perfect lining both sides of what became a private road. Wrought-iron gates up ahead, barely visible through the windshield.

"Three minutes," Bram announced.

I killed the headlights with one flick of the stalk.

The Crown Vic rolled forward into darkness, engine still roaring, carrying us toward Sterling Manor like a steel coffin full of bad intentions.

## CHAPTER 2: CHAPTER 2

I killed the Crown Vic's engine three hundred yards out, letting the sedan coast into the drainage culvert beneath the High-Spire ridge. The November rain hammered against the roof like machine-gun fire—cold, relentless, perfect for what we had planned.

"Down," I said to no one in particular, sliding out into the ankle-deep water pooling at the culvert bottom. Ice crystals formed along the concrete walls, frost-cobalt magic bleeding from my fingertips without conscious thought. The temperature dropped another five degrees around me.

Grimjaw was already there when I emerged, a mountain of green-skinned muscle wrapped in black tactical gear. His tusks caught the ambient light filtering down from above, making them gleam like polished bone. Behind him, twelve members of Local 405 materialized from the shadows—goblins, ogre-kin, street-elves, dwarven sandhogs. All of them carrying more firepower than most city blocks could handle.

"You're late, Reyes," Grimjaw rumbled, his voice like gravel tumbling through a cement mixer. He extended a meaty paw toward me.

I ignored it and looked past him at the assembled crew. They were good—their body language tight, controlled, faces smeared with grease paint that turned them into shadows with eyes. Weapons clicked as safeties came off. Magazines checked. Loaders seated with soft, wet snicks.

"They know why we're here?" I asked, pulling out my PalmPilot.

"The Sterling Manor contract." Grimjaw's expression didn't change. "The union vote last week made it official. We take back our people, or we take back their lives."

I watched the rain run down his face, mixing with the war paint. Twelve years since I'd pulled him out of that High-Spire cage, but he still remembered what they'd done to his family. What they'd *tried* to do.

"Sterling's got eight High-Spire retainers on the outer ring," he continued, dropping his voice another register. "Elves. Fast, but they rely on the Telluric wire to do the heavy lifting. Cut the wire—"

"We cut their throats." I finished the sentence for him, my breath pluming in the cold air. The frost-cobalt magic pulsed stronger now, responding to the violence already building in my chest. "No noise."

A younger goblin near the back—couldn't have been more than seventeen if he was a day—shifted his weight. Made a sound low in his throat.

Grimjaw's head snapped around so fast I heard vertebrae crack. "Problem?"

"No problem, boss." The goblin's yellow eyes went wide. "Just... you sure about this?"

"I'm sure the Telluric Perimeter Wire is going to make very interesting music tonight," I said, holding up the wire-cutters I'd taken from Sub-Station Seven. They were heavy steel, industrial-grade, and already beginning to frost over at the jaws. My magic had other ideas about how this operation would go down.

Grimjaw grunted approval and handed me an IBM ThinkPad, ruggedized and waterproof. The screen glowed green in the darkness, showing Sterling Manor's active security grid. Red dots marked guard positions. Yellow lines traced camera coverage. A blinking blue icon showed the Watching Party's current location inside the manor grounds.

"Bram's team spoofed the perimeter sensors ten minutes ago," Grimjaw explained. "We've got a four-minute window before the system recognizes the false data."

I stared at the screen, watching the seconds tick by. My palm was sweating despite the freezing rain. This was it—the point of no return. After this, there was no going back to being just Cole Reyes, machinist from the South Side.

"There's a loading dock on the east side," I said, tracing a route with one finger. "Less visible from the main house. We can get in clean if we move now."

The crew tensed, weapons coming up. Eyes gleaming in the dark. Ready for blood.

"Remember what we talked about," I said, raising my voice so everyone could hear. "Tactical discipline first. Emotional payback second. We're here to free metahumans, not start a war we can't finish."

"You keep saying that, Reyes." Grimjaw checked his rifle magazine—full, locked, loaded. "But wars have a way of starting themselves."

My PalmPilot buzzed.

The encrypted message appeared: **WATCHING PARTY GUARD CODES SPOOFED. PERIMETER SENSORS BLINDED. FOUR MINUTE COUNTDOWN COMMENCING.**

Grimjaw looked at me. I looked at him.

"Let's make some music," I said quietly.

And then we moved into the rain-soaked darkness toward Sterling Manor, armed and ready for whatever waited inside those walls.

* * *

The wire hummed—a low, angry vibration that made my teeth ache behind the safety goggles I'd stolen from the machinist's locker at Sub-Station Seven. Telluric perimeter wire. Old magic married to new tech, running hot enough to cook a man who didn't know how to read its signature. I did. Three years maintaining the power grid for PSE&G had taught me which wires sang death and which ones whispered warnings.

This one sang death.

I knelt in the muddy shoulder of the Kearny Marshes access road, hidden behind a rusted chain-link fence that probably hadn't kept anyone out since the Carter administration. Fifty yards ahead, past manicured hedges that cost more than most people's mortgages, Sterling Manor rose against the November sky like a tombstone carved from limestone and old money.

Frost-cobalt magic pooled in my palm—my inheritance from a mother I never knew, a gift I'd spent twenty-five years trying to control. The cold bit into my skin, familiar and hungry, racing up my forearm toward the heavy bolt cutters gripped in both hands. The steel handles began to sweat ice crystals, frost spreading across the metal like a creeping disease.

Behind me, Grimjaw stood silent as a monument, his massive frame blocking what little light filtered through the pollution-choked air. He'd been quiet since we left County General, watching me prepare with those dead-eyeballs that saw too much and said nothing. Around him, fifteen Local 405 members checked weapons, comms, and the nervous tics that came before a job like this. Street-Elves adjusted their rifle scopes. Ogre-Kin flexed fingers the size of baseball bats. A human medic I didn't recognize—a woman named Singh according to Grimjaw's briefing—checked her trauma kit for the third time.

"Telluric wire carries a signature burst when it breaks," I muttered, not taking my eyes off the glowing thread barely visible in the darkness. "Standard security response is seven seconds minimum. We have five."

"That's your problem now." Grimjaw's voice rumbled like distant thunder. "Cut it clean or we're all dead inside those walls."

I pressed my freezing hands tighter around the bolt cutter handles. The frost-cobalt hex surged down the steel, racing along the joints and blades until the entire tool shimmered with impossible cold. Temperature dropped below zero, then below absolute zero—the magic eating away at the fundamental heat of the metal until the bolt cutters became something colder than physics should allow.

The Telluric wire pulsed ahead of me, glowing faintly with trapped lightning. Standard security said it would explode into an arc-flash the moment any significant current drew from it. Electric fence on steroids, designed by High-Spire engineers who thought the South Side was full of stupid laborers who couldn't read schematics.

They were half-right.

I lunged forward, clamping the frozen jaws onto the humming wire. My heart hammered against my ribs. One second. Two. The frost-cobalt magic hit the superheated Telluric line and...

Silence.

The wire stopped humming. Stopped glowing. In the space between one heartbeat and the next, it turned brittle as glass, frozen solid by magic that shouldn't exist outside of theoretical physics. Ice crystals spread across its length, spiderwebbing the structure.

I squeezed.

*Tink.*

The sound was tiny—a small, delicate breaking like a wine glass dropped on carpet. The Telluric wire shattered, fragments falling to earth in a cascade of frozen light. Dead-silent. No explosion. No alarm burst. Nothing but the sudden, gaping darkness where security had been moments before.

"Go," I hissed, already moving toward the breach point in Sterling Manor's wall. Grimjaw pushed past me, his bulk carrying him through the gap without slowing. Local 405 members poured through behind him—flooding into gardens lit only by distant streetlights bleeding over the estate walls.

A Street-Elf sniper mounted up on an Ogre-Kin's shoulders, clearing the twelve-foot barrier with practiced ease. His rifle came up instantly, sweeping the garden grounds below.

"Outer grid is dark," he whispered into his throat mic, voice tight with adrenaline. "We're in the garden. Smells like expensive fertilizer and dead men."

Mud squelched beneath tactical boots. Someone stumbled over an ornamental fountain. A curse muttered in a language older than English. I wove between the hedges, bolt cutters still gripped in my frost-numbed hands, scanning for movement ahead.

Flashlight beam.

Sweeping across the garden path, maybe twenty yards ahead, accompanied by the crunch of gravel under heavy boots. Two silhouettes materialized from around a marble statue—High-Spire elven guards in full tactical gear, their weapons already rising toward the breach point they'd just discovered.

They saw us.

We saw them.

The distance collapsed to nothing in my peripheral vision as I dove sideways, hitting the damp grass hard enough to knock the breath from my lungs. Bullets stitched the air where I'd been standing milliseconds before. Grimjaw roared—a sound that shook loose leaves from nearby trees—and charged forward like an avalanche.

My hand found the bolt cutters still clutched in my grip. Frost-cobalt magic flared again, desperate and wild, pouring into the steel jaws.

* * *

The Telluric wires sang—a high-frequency whine that set my teeth on edge even through the rain-soaked air. November cold bit through my jacket as I crouched beside Grimjaw near the topiary sculpture, watching the perimeter guards make their patrol pass.

"Two more minutes," I whispered, feeling the familiar cold gather in my fingertips. The wire-cutters hung heavy in my left hand, their steel jaws already beginning to glaze over with frost-cobalt condensation. The magic came easier now, after all these years working the night shift at County General's machine shop—long hours with metal and compression had taught me how to make tools obey.

Grimjaw grunted beside me, his bulk barely contained by the shadows. His yellow eyes tracked the guards' movement, calculating angles and reaction times with that Orcish precision that made him worth every penny of the retainer I couldn't afford. He was borrowed time, dangerous company, but tonight we needed someone who didn't flinch when things got messy.

The guards paused at the junction point, their corporate-tactical armor gleaming wet under the security floodlights. Elven High-Spire, if the pointed ears and expensive equipment were anything to go by. One of them knelt, running a gloved finger across the grass, then looked up sharply at his partner.

Frost.

My mistake. Should've been more careful with the temperature manipulation. The first guard reached for his radio, his expression shifting from confusion to alert concern.

That's when we moved.

I exploded from the darkness like a frozen blade, my hand clamping down over the nearest elf's mouth before he could draw breath to speak. My frost-cobalt magic surged through the contact point, racing along his neck, freezing the blood in his carotid artery with such sudden, violent efficiency that his body went rigid, then slack within a heartbeat. His eyes went wide, then glassy as the ice crystals formed beneath the skin, turning it a pale, sickly blue.

Behind him, Grimjaw was already on the second guard. No finesse, no magic—just pure, brutal physics. He stepped forward as the elf turned, one massive hand shooting out to grip the back of the guard's head while the other clamped around his chin. Then came the sound—a wet, sickening crunch that echoed through the garden despite the rain, the distinct pop-pop-pop of cervical vertebrae separating.

The corpse dropped like a sack of meat.

I dragged my kill into the bushes without looking at his face, my expression flat, clinical. This wasn't about anger or revenge—not yet. This was reconnaissance, elimination, preparation. The guards had made their choice when they signed contracts with people who hired out execution squads. Their deaths weren't on me any more than the rust was on the iron I worked with every night.

Grimjaw wiped a drop of arterial spray from his knuckles with his thumb, then sucked it clean. "Too slow," he rumbled, his voice like grinding gears. "High-Spire soft. All show, no substance."

"Three minutes." I checked my watch, the luminous dial cutting through the darkness. The BATC emergency channels were probably already screaming about Sub-Station Seven by now—that distraction we'd carefully engineered to pull resources away from Sterling Manor. "Move to the terrace."

We crossed the manicured lawn in bounds, the grass crunching under our boots, each footfall careful and deliberate. Through the driving rain, Sterling Manor loomed ahead—a monument to old money and newer corruption, its facade lit against the night sky. And there, just visible past the decorative hedgerow, the massive glass terrace doors glowed warm and golden.

Classical music drifted out through the cracks—some baroque piece I couldn't name, all strings and harpsichord. Inside those doors was everything we'd come for. Inside was Visible Head #1, and the answers he owed me for what happened to Maria at County General last spring.

Inside was first blood in a garden that would soon run red.

I raised my hand, signaling Grimjaw to halt as we reached the hedge line. The glass doors were perhaps forty feet away now, close enough to see movement inside—shadows passing across the light, the silhouette of someone moving between rooms.

Time slowed down to the rhythm of my heartbeat, each pulse another second closer to whatever came next.

"Ready?" I asked quietly.

Grimjaw's grin was all teeth in the darkness. "Been ready since you hired me, machinist."

## CHAPTER 3: CHAPTER 3

The night air bit at my exposed skin as I crouched behind the manicured hedges of Sterling Manor's west terrace. November cold, but it was nothing compared to what was building inside me—the frost-cobalt pressure that wanted out, that always wanted out when my psychogenic bleed got too thick in my bloodstream.

"Locks are solid," whispered Mika from where she knelt beside the French doors, her gloved fingers working over the deadbolts with practiced precision. Her breath plumed white in the darkness. "Three internal bolts, two external. All magnetic-kinetic sealed."

I didn't need to see the locks to know they'd be tough. Sterling Manor belonged to Vincent Moretti, and Moretti had more money than sense when it came to security. The kind of money that bought things that went *chunk-chunk-chunk* when you tried to pick them normally.

But normal wasn't something we dealt in.

I pressed my palm against the cold glass, feeling for the resonance frequencies of the locking mechanisms through the doorframe itself. My 4th-Law awareness let me taste the iron-sweet flavor of the electromagnetic fields holding those bolts in place. Too easy. Like plucking fruit.

"Stand back," I muttered, drawing a rune-etched blade from my thigh holster. The metal sang its harmonic frequency as I scored three quick passes across the threshold—blood-iron geometry, old magic married to new physics. The hex wouldn't break the locks. It would make them break themselves.

The sound was like someone snapping steel cables inside a coffin. Three sharp *CRACK-CRACK-CRACK* echoed through the empty terrace.

Mika's eyes widened in the dim light filtering from the manor's interior windows. She pulled the doors open without another word, revealing a corridor of such opulent excess it made my teeth hurt. Imported marble flooring, mahogany wainscoting that probably cost more than Bram's apartment building, recessed lighting casting everything in warm amber glow.

That warmth lasted exactly three seconds after I stepped through the threshold.

The temperature dropped like I'd walked into a meat locker. The air crystallized around my face with each exhalation. Frost began creeping up the walls immediately, spreading like a living thing along the expensive woodwork. Behind me, I heard Kovacs curse softly.

"What the fuck, Reyes?"

"Don't ask." I kept moving, MP5 raised and ready. The bismuth-depleted slugs in the magazine felt heavy in their casings—good weight, good balance. Mika slid in behind me, her own weapon covering our six. Bram took up position at the front door we'd just breached, watching for any movement outside.

We were maybe twenty feet down the service corridor when voices drifted toward us from somewhere deeper in the manor. Laughter. A cup clinking against something hard.

*"—can't believe Moretti expects us to do another sweep at midnight,"* a male voice said. *"Guy's paranoid as shit."*

*"Pay is good though,"* another answered. *"Better than the last gig."*

Human security contractors. Private muscle hired by Moretti because he didn't trust the local firms—or maybe because he needed plausible deniability. Either way, they weren't on our side.

I raised one hand, fist closed. Mika stopped. Bram went still behind us.

Three men rounded the corner ahead, still laughing among themselves. One held a steaming cup of coffee. They wore black tactical gear, but nothing that would stop what I was carrying.

They saw us simultaneously. Eyes widening. Hands dropping to weapons.

I didn't hesitate.

The MP5 bucked against my shoulder three times in quick succession—*thwip-thwip-thwip*—the sound eaten by the silencer even as blue-black iron-gall residue puffed from the barrel like smoke from an old gunpowder weapon. The depleted-bismuth slugs punched through their kinetic vests like paper, each impact spraying red mist into the suddenly frigid air.

The first guard dropped without even finishing drawing his sidearm. The second stumbled backward, blood bubbling from his lips as he collapsed. The third managed half a step before my third round caught him in the throat, severing whatever he'd been about to say into a wet gurgle.

The coffee cup fell from the dead man's fingers, shattering on the marble floor with a sharp *CRACK*. Hot liquid splashed across the white stone, and I watched it freeze solid in seconds, turning from brown liquid to a slushy crust as my aura finished doing its work.

Bram stepped over the bodies without breaking stride, his own weapon still raised and scanning. *"Kinetic wards didn't even slow the bismuth down. Good machining, if I do say so myself."*

I pointed down the hall with my free hand, not taking my eyes off the corridor ahead. The cold was getting worse now, frost spreading faster along both walls like veins of ice reaching toward something deep inside me that I refused to acknowledge.

*"Security hub is next,"* I said quietly. *"Cut their eyes."*

Mika checked her tactical display—holographic readout projected from the smart-glasses she wore. *"Reinforced oak doors, fifty feet north. Thermal signatures consistent with at least four personnel inside."*

We stacked up outside those doors exactly forty-five seconds later. Behind us lay three corpses freezing solid on expensive imported marble. Ahead waited Moretti's security team.

And somewhere beyond them was the reason we'd come.

* * *

The oak doors explode inward like matchsticks.

I don't even break stride as I channel the kinetic burst—pure mechanical precision translated into brute force. Splinters spray across marble floors, scattering like teeth. Behind me, Grimjaw moves like a shadow given muscle, two Ogre-Kin flanking him with suppressed weapons already raised.

Inside the security hub, four High-Spire elves barely have time to register our entry before their world goes red. The room reeks of that distinctive sterile-sweet scent that clings to corporate types—expensive cologne mixed with the acrid tang of fresh coffee gone cold in designer mugs.

CRT monitors dominate the far wall, their green-phosphor glow casting everything in sickly light. Four screens showing different angles of the manor's interior. Four screens about to die.

"Contact!" One elf—that's all he gets out before Grimjaw's already moving, his .45 barking twice. Center mass. The elf crumples.

The other three scramble for their sidearms holstered at their hips, but they're too slow, too comfortable, too used to guarding paper-pushers instead of actual threats. An Ogre-Kin drops one with a single shot through the throat. The second tries to bring up an expensive-looking pistol, and Grimjaw's there, driving his fist into the elf's solar plexus hard enough to crack ribs.

That leaves number four—the lead retainer, judging by his better suit and the way he was standing behind the main console. He fumbles with his weapon, panic creeping into those ice-blue eyes.

I'm already across the room, frost-cobalt bleeding from my fingertips, coating my combat knife in that telltale iridescent sheen. The temperature in here has already dropped twenty degrees; I can see my breath pluming white in front of me.

The elf fires wild as I close. The bullet shatters a monitor behind my head, sending glass spitting everywhere, but I don't flinch. Can't afford to. My left hand catches his wrist—bones crack under the pressure—and I slam his hand down on the desk.

He screams. That pretty corporate accent cracks like ice on a winter lake.

"You're dead, street-trash! Sterling will—"

Grimjaw's beside us in a heartbeat. His pistol comes around, and he smashes the butt into the elf's temple. Once. Twice. The screaming stops, replaced by wet choking sounds.

"Sterling ain't the one holding the leash tonight, suit," Grimjaw growls, then kicks the elf's legs out from under him.

I drive the frost-cobalt knife through the retainer's pinned hand, nailing it to the mahogany desk. Blood pools instantly, spreading crimson across expensive wood. And then something remarkable happens—it begins crystallizing at the edges, ice forming where the cold from my blade meets his warm flesh.

"Camera systems," I say flatly, yanking the mainframe tower open. Ripping out cables. "Hard drives."

My fingers find the array—they always build them in the same configuration, predictable bastards—and I wrench them free. Metal tears with satisfying screeching sounds. Sparks shower across my knuckles.

All four monitors flicker. Die. Plunge into static.

The manor is blind now.

Silence settles over the security room, broken only by the elf's panicked breathing and the smell of ozone from the burning electronics. There's another odor too—the copper tang of blood mixing with the acrid burn of melted plastic.

Then, faint as a ghost's whisper, drifting down from somewhere above:

A string quartet playing Vivaldi.

Champagne glasses clinking together.

The real party hasn't even noticed we're here yet.

The words click into place like gears I didn't know were there. Suddenly I understand why those wards feel so familiar—they're built from the same magic Mara used to save me at Kearny Marshes, back in... when was that? 2005?

My mind reels, trying to calculate the timeline.

"How long?" I croak. "How much time?"

Elena and Lysandra exchange a look—years of unspoken communication compressed into a single glance between women who have clearly worked side by side through things they'll never speak aloud.

"You've been out for three days," Elena says quietly. "It's January 17th, 2008. You've been missing since October 2006. We found you last week in what was left of Newark Bay. Voss had carved pieces out of you to study your frost-cobalt mutations."

The monitor beside my head spikes—a sharp peak that makes Lysandra adjust her wards slightly.

"He's remembering," she observes. Clinical. Measured.

"Give him a second," Elena snaps, then softens as she looks back down at me. Pride flickers across her tired features. "You did good work out there, Cole. Even if you were an idiot for going it alone."

The room smells wrong. Ozone and antiseptic fighting with something else—cedar, maybe? Fresh cut wood mixed with the metallic tang of blood and burning wards. I try to process it all but my thoughts keep slipping sideways, trying to reconcile everything I just learned.

Three years. They found me after three years. And somehow...

"How many are dead because I wasn't here?"

Lysandra's lips thin. Her wards pulse brighter—agitated or angry, hard to tell which.

"The numbers don't matter now." She steps closer to the table, tilting her head as she studies me like a specimen under glass. "What matters is whether you can fight. Whether you're willing to stop playing lone wolf and join the resistance we've built while you were frozen in ice water beneath County General."

Elena shoots her a warning glare, but doesn't contradict her.

I look between them—the surgeon covered in her son's blood, the High-Spire exile wreathed in protective fire—and finally understand what those three years really meant.

They didn't wait for me.

They built something without me.

And now they're offering me a place in it.

The machine beeps steadily behind my head. White-lilac wards intertwine with frost-cobalt sparks dancing across my skin. Somewhere far above us, 2008 New Jersey keeps spinning through its days while the Syndicate activates gods know what kind of weapon.

My mother waits for my answer, hand still on my shoulder.

Mara's mother watches with calculating eyes.

Both of them standing over the broken mechanic who thought he could win this war alone.

I swallow hard against my raw throat.

"Show me," I whisper.
