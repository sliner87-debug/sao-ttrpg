## CHAPTER 1: THE TWO MOTHERS

The first thing that cuts through the black is the smell—burnt sugar and ozone, like someone's trying to distill lightning into liquor. My eyes crack open to a ceiling of riveted stainless steel, six inches off my face. I'm horizontal. Restrained.

My body doesn't ask permission.

I surge upward hard enough to buck the gurney, every muscle fiber screaming as blood-iron hexes ignite beneath my skin. The IV line in my left arm shatters, plastic snapping like a gunshot. Frost explodes outward from my core—the room's temperature dropping thirty degrees in two seconds flat. Condensation crystals race up the medical monitors, ice crawling across their displays like hungry fingers.

"Shit!" Someone yelps.

I pivot on bare feet, expecting sterile BATC white. Expecting interrogation panels. Instead:

A closet-sized bunker. Low ceiling. Sodium-vapor lamps humming that sick orange glow, casting everything in jaundiced light. Exposed copper wiring snakes along the walls, sparking faintly. A battered Panasonic Toughbook sits wedged between two rust-streaked filing cabinets, running some kind of heart-rate monitor via serial cable, its screen already frosting over.

And hunched over a workbench littered with circuit boards and soldering irons is a Gnome. Grease-stained mechanic's jumpsuit, wild hair catching fire in shades of magenta under the sodium lights. He doesn't even look up, just keeps his magnifier down on whatever micro-transponder he's working, one hand steady as a surgeon while mine glow blue-white with residual frost-cobalt.

"You gonna freeze my whole setup or what?" He doesn't sound scared. Annoyed, maybe. "Yo, iceman, chill the hexes. You blow my motherboard, you're buying me a new Toughbook."

My breathing slows. I force the magic down—harder than it should be after what happened. The Morvain blast radius... there was supposed to be nothing left. No extraction team. No survivors.

"No." My voice comes out wrong, hoarse. I rip the second IV from my right arm, blood dripping onto concrete. Cold seeping up through my boots. "Who pulled me out?"

The Gnome finally glances up, pushing his magnifier back. His eyes are three different colors—genetic quirk or augmentation, can't tell which—and they track my movement like a predator sizing up prey. He sets down his soldering iron carefully.

"Lady, you got more questions coming, trust me. But first—" He gestures at the Toughbook. "-you need to see your vitals. Heart rate's jacked, electrolytes all fucked, and that frost-cobalt aura of yours? Burning through your reserves like cheap gas."

"I don't care about—"

"The hell you don't!" He slams a palm on the workbench, making tools jump. "Look at the screen! Look at it!"

I do. Reluctantly.

The heart-rate monitor shows a jagged line climbing toward critical levels. And beneath it, another readout: *Frost-Cobalt Index: 98% saturation. Cellular regeneration: 400%. Temporal displacement field: ACTIVE.*

"Temporal..." The word tastes like copper and old blood.

"Yeah." The Gnome—Fidget, based on the stitched name on his jumpsuit—leans back against the filing cabinet, arms crossed. "You've been displaced, sweetheart. Not dead. Pulled out of the blast radius six hours before impact. Time travel shit, real James Bond crap. You're currently in Sub-Station Seven, under the Kearny marshes, and—"

The pneumatic hiss cuts him off.

Heavy steel bulkhead door unlatching. Two figures stepping out from beyond, where shadows should have swallowed them whole.

My blood goes entirely cold. Not from magic this time.

From shock.

Because I recognize both faces. One I haven't seen since before the Breach. One who died in the Morvain explosion.

Both impossible.

Both standing there anyway.

* * *

The sodium lights flickered like dying stars as two figures materialized from the shadows at the far end of Sub-Station Seven. My breath caught—not from fear, but from recognition so sharp it felt like glass in my throat.

"Mara?" 

I heard Cole's voice crack before I saw him freeze, his MP5—its rune-etched barrel still glowing faintly cobalt—snapping up and tracking toward the newcomers. The weapon trembled in his hands, that distinct tremor of someone whose mind is trying to reconcile impossible data.

Behind me, I could feel it too—the sudden shift in atmospheric pressure, the way the air tasted different. Sweeter. Wrong.

Elena Reyes stood there in the sick yellow light, her dark hair streaked with premature silver, her face thinner than the last photograph I'd seen eight years ago. But it was her—unmistakably her, even with the changes time had carved into her features. Beside her, half-hidden in shadow, another figure emerged.

"Mom?"

The word left my lips before I could stop it. The woman stepped forward into the light, and my knees nearly buckled. Lysandra Vance. Alive. Impossible. Dead since 2000, supposedly burned in the clinic fire with Elena, with all those children we'd failed to save.

Cole made a sound—I don't know if it was a sob or a snarl—and lunged forward, but his mother was already moving, one hand raised palm-outward. Her arcane signature washed through the room like golden sunlight cutting through fog, and I felt the frost-cobalt panic radiating from Cole's direction neutralize instantly. Not by force, but by something deeper. Something that bypassed magic entirely and went straight for the heart.

"I buried an empty casket while Danny choked on black iron."

Cole's voice came out as a low rasp, dangerous and raw. He'd dropped his MP5—the heavy clatter of metal on concrete echoed through the bunker—and now he stood shaking, fists clenched at his sides. "Eight years, Mom. Eight fucking years I thought you were dead. I watched Danny turn to sludge while you were... what? Hiding?"

Elena didn't flinch. Didn't look away. She just stood there in her worn leather jacket, hands hanging loose at her sides, eyes never leaving her son's face.

"If BATC knew we survived," she said quietly, "they would have finished the job. We went underground to build the network. To build the Watching Party." Her dark eyes flicked to me then—just for a moment—and I saw something there that made ice form in my stomach. Knowledge. Warning. "We've been watching, Cole. Both of you. Every step you've taken since Newark."

The smell hit me then, cutting through the ozone and copper tang of the bunker. Fresh linen. White lilacs. My mother's scent, but cleaner somehow. Purified. Lysandra moved closer, and with each step, her arcane signature grew stronger—that same white lilac glow I'd channeled countless times, but brighter. Purer. Untainted by the Breach.

"Mara baby," she whispered, and hearing that old nickname made something crack inside my chest, "you don't remember me, do you?"

I did. Gods help me, I did—but those memories felt like they belonged to someone else. A child who'd lost her mother, who'd grown up believing in lies.

"Why?" The word scraped out of my throat. "Why fake your deaths?"

Lysandra reached into her pocket, pulled out an ancient Motorola Razr that looked laughably outdated next to modern tech. It cracked static as encrypted text bursts lit up its screen—messages scrolling too fast to read. "Because some truths are more dangerous than lies, sweetheart. And because we needed time. Time to prepare for what's coming."

Cole laughed—a sharp, bitter sound that didn't belong on his lips. "Preparation? Is that what you call eight years of silence while your sons fell apart?" He took a step forward, hands still clenched. "Danny turned himself inside-out trying to make sense of losing you. Of losing *everyone*."

Elena's jaw tightened. "I know."

"Do you? Do you really?" His voice rose, cracking at the edges. "He spent three years digging through every rumor, every whisper about that fire. Every corpse he could find. He thought... God, Cole, he thought maybe you were alive. That maybe he could save you somehow. But there was nothing. Just ash and bones and—"

"Stop." Lysandra's voice cut through Cole's words like a knife. She moved between them now, close enough to touch, though she didn't. "We didn't come here to argue about the past, or to apologize—though God knows you deserve better than the silence we gave you."

She turned to me then, and those eyes—my mother's eyes, brown and deep and infinite—locked onto mine with an intensity that made my breath catch.

"We brought you here," she said softly, "because Mara is blind, and she is about to walk into Elias Morvain's final trap."

The Razr in her hand crackled again. Another burst of encrypted text.

And somewhere deep in Sub-Station Seven, I heard something move in the darkness beyond the sodium lights. Something that had been listening all along.

* * *

The tactical room reeks of stale coffee and something worse—the acrid tang of ozone that clings to every surface down here in the Undercity. My fingers drum against the metal table, leaving faint impressions in the dust that's settled over blueprints we haven't updated in three weeks. Maybe longer. Time moves differently in the dark.

Cole stands across from me, shoulders squared, trying to look like he belongs at this table. He doesn't. None of us do, not really. But here we are.

"Show him," Lysandra says. Her voice cuts through the hum of ventilation shafts that never stop, never rest, just like us. She nods toward the IBM ThinkPad sitting between us, its screen glowing that sickly green phosphor that makes everyone look half-dead.

I tap the trackpad—click-clack-click—and satellite imagery floods the screen. Pulaski Skyway, but wrong somehow. Too clear. Enhanced. There's Pylon #4, the one Marcus Voss has been licking his lips over for months now, and there's movement. Corporate army movement. Black dots coalescing into black shapes, spreading like oil across the digital landscape.

"They've mobilized," I say, because someone needs to. "Full deployment. Voss isn't waiting anymore."

Cole leans closer, and I catch the way the green light reflects in his eyes. Good. Let him see it clearly. Let him understand what we're up against.

"The Telluric grid is bleeding," Lysandra continues, her clinical precision making it sound almost academic until you remember she's talking about the literal electricity keeping BATC alive. "Every micro-transponder the Gnomes planted is a leak point. The Watching Party has been siphoning power for years—not just stealing it, but learning it. Understanding how our systems think."

Fidget materializes at my elbow like he always does, appearing from nowhere with a rigged police scanner held out like an offering. His fingers dance across the buttons. Static crackles, then clarifies:

"...breach... Morvain Estate... High-Spire Elven IA..."

My heart stops. Just stops.

"Mara." Cole's hand shoots out, grabs my wrist. "That's—"

"I know what it is." My voice comes out sharp, harder than I mean it. But I can't soften it. Not when every fiber of my being wants to run, wants to grab my MP5 off this table and burn through every obstacle between here and—

"She's alone," Lysandra says quietly. "At Sub-Station Seven. Carrying everything. The weight of that tragedy." She pauses. "The ash-gray static that's trying to consume her from the inside out."

I feel it even now—the phantom scent of wet charred wool, the taste of copper on my tongue, the roar of that explosion that should have killed me but instead fused something dark into my soul. Something that whispers in the quiet moments, promises release if I'll only let go.

But there's another feeling too. Fainter, but warmer. A memory of heat against my chest, the sound of someone's heartbeat, steady and real. Cole's hearth-coal. It kept me anchored during the worst of it.

"It's all she has left," Lysandra continues, her mother's eyes meeting mine with an expression I can't quite read—pride? Sorrow? Both? "BATC turned her into a weapon. They didn't just train her; they rewired her. That ash-gray static... it's part of her now. But your ward, Cole..." Her gaze shifts to him. "...your hearth-coal. It’s the only thing keeping the ash from consuming her completely."

Cole's face goes tight. "Then we need to get back there. Now."

"We can't." I hate saying it, but facts are facts. "The Passaic River corridor is locked down. Every bridge between here and Kearny is crawling with Voss's people. We'd be walking into a kill zone."

"So what do you suggest?" His voice rises slightly—not angry at me, angry at the situation, which is reasonable considering he just found out his mentor is alone and hunted in enemy territory.

Fidget fidgets with the scanner, tuning through frequencies like he's looking for salvation in static. Not finding any.

Lysandra reaches across the table, fingers hovering over a folder I haven't opened yet. Hasn't been opened since before Sub-Station Seven went up in flames. Her daughter's file.

"Before we talk about rescue missions," she says carefully, "we need to understand what we're really dealing with. Who Mara Vance was before BATC claimed her."

I want to refuse. Want to stand up and walk out, go find Mara myself, consequences be damned. But that's exactly what the static wants—to make me reckless, impulsive, driven by emotion instead of strategy.

So I sit.

I breathe.

And I wait while Lysandra begins to decrypt the files that will tell us who my mother was, what made her tick, and why someone like Elias Morvain kept a vault full of information about a BATC Aegis Commander.

The ThinkPad's fan whirs louder as it processes.

Behind us, Fidget suddenly goes rigid.

"Got another ping," he squeaks. His voice cracks. "High-Spire District. Movement inside the Morvain Estate." He looks up at me, eyes wide. "They're hunting the Vance girl."

Cole doesn't hesitate. He grabs his MP5 off the tactical table with practiced ease.

"Start talking," he tells Lysandra, voice low and dangerous. "Tell me everything before I go get her."

I'm already moving toward the door, checking my weapon, loading magazines on muscle memory alone. The ash-gray static rises in my chest like bile, hungry for violence, eager for release.

But underneath it all—so faint I almost miss it—that warm coal-glow pulses once, twice.

A reminder.

Not just of Cole.

Of the woman I'm fighting to remain.

## CHAPTER 2: PROPHECY ON MINIDISC

The vault breathes smoke.

I push through hanging chains of melted copper, my boots crunching on shards of what used to be priceless crystal. Rain hammers through the collapse above—a cathedral-sized hole where a ceiling should be—turning everything below into a steaming hell-kitchen. The water hisses against slag so hot it still glows orange in the dark.

My left shoulder screams. Blood soaks through the bandage I fashioned from torn silk curtains. Doesn't matter. Nothing matters except finding what he hid.

*Elias Morvain. You built this tomb for yourself.*

White-lilac flames spring from my fingertips—not by choice anymore, just like breathing now. They cast everything in sickening gold as I move deeper into the ruins. The temperature drops. The stench changes from burnt wool and flesh to something older, colder. River mud. Rotting cedar. Decay that predates the estate itself.

Marble columns split clean down the middle, their halves leaning apart like ribcage bones. I weave between them, following a corridor of collapsed wealth—melted candlesticks fused to walls, portraits bubbled black, a grand piano twisted into a baroque sculpture of suffering.

There.

The safe room door hangs open on one hinge, its titanium frame peeled back like aluminum foil. Whoever blew this place apart didn't bother with finesse. Just raw power. The same kind of power that turned those commuters into ash last night. *314 souls. 314 faces I'll never forget. Never forgive myself for.*

I step inside.

The vault interior is surprisingly intact—a sphere of reinforced concrete studded with titanium plugs. Whatever destroyed the outer chambers stopped at this threshold. Or was meant to stop here. A message within a disaster.

Shelving units sag under scorched ledgers and data crystals. I start pulling files, flipping through carbon-scored pages. Corporate records. Shipping manifests. Offshore accounts. All damning, all useless. This isn't treasure. It's noise.

Then I see it: beneath a melted silver tray, half-buried in rubble, a pristine rectangle of brushed metal. A Sony MiniDisc player. Silver. Expensive. Untouched by fire or water.

My fingers close around it. Cold. Solid. Real.

*What are you?*

Behind the player, wedged in the gap between wall and a fallen safe deposit box, a single disc in a clear jewel case. No label. No writing. Nothing but my reflection staring back—a ghost woman holding fire in her hands.

I take both. Turn them over. Find nothing else.

The rain intensifies. Thunder rolls across the Passaic marshes somewhere beyond these walls. My flames gutter low—exhaustion bleeding through my control—but there's enough light to see the player has an AC adapter port. Battery dead, naturally.

I spot a power outlet still sparking behind a toppled filing cabinet. Three minutes of prying charred metal loose, and the cord plugs in. Green LED blinks. The screen flickers to life.

*Play.*

No hesitation. Can't afford it anymore.

I slot the disc. Wired earbuds pull free from a pocket sewn into my coat—the one that survived last night without burning. They taste like copper and ozone as I press them into my ears.

The player hums. Whirs. And then:

"—know you'll find this eventually, Detective Reyes."

The voice stops me mid-breath.

Older man. British accent cut with something American. Educated. Affable despite the context.

"I'm dead when you hear this, so indulge an old fraud his final monologue." A pause. Papers shuffling. "You're wondering why I kept certain ledgers pristine while letting others burn. Why I hid a recording device in my most secure vault rather than destroy it with everything else."

Another pause. Longer.

"Because some fires need witnesses, Cole. Some sins require documentation. I've spent forty years building networks that would make your skin crawl—not for money, never just for money. For survival. For leverage against things worse than corporations or crime syndicates."

My hands shake around the player. This is Elias Morvain speaking from beyond death, but his words sound rehearsed. Scripted. Like he knew exactly who'd be listening.

"The mini-disc contains coordinates to twelve locations across North Jersey. Sub-Station Seven. Kearny Marshes. Pulaski Skyway support pillars. Each marked on embedded maps. Below each location: caches of weapons-grade materials, experimental tech, and data cores containing evidence of—"

A crackle of interference.

"—don't have much time before the encryption fails completely. The first cache, beneath Sub-Station Seven in Newark's industrial sector, holds enough chemical precursors to render half the Passaic River system uninhabitable for a century. But that's not what matters."

The voice drops lower, conspiratorial now.

"What matters is the *second* set of coordinates. The ones buried three layers deep in those files. They point to where they're keeping her. Mara Kincaid. Your partner. The woman you burned alive in that train tunnel last night."

Blood drains from my face.

"I know about the prophecy, Detective. Know about the price your bloodline demands. But prophecies are just patterns until someone breaks them." Static crashes. Reforms. "She's not dead yet. Not technically. They're trying to weaponize what remains. If you move fast—if you trust the coordinates—I can give you a chance to fix what you were forced to do."

The recording ends.

I stand there in the smoking vault, rain pouring overhead, holding a dead man's confession like scripture.

Mara. Alive. Somewhere.

*No. I felt her die.*

But Elias Morvain knew about the prophecy. About me. Which means he knew more than he should have survived knowing.

Sub-Station Seven. Newark industrial sector.

My flames surge bright again—not ash-gray anymore but burning white-hot with purpose.

"Then let's see how far this rabbit hole goes," I whisper to the ruins.

I take both player and disc. Leave the ledgers to rot.

Outside, thunder promises storms worse than rain.

* * *

The MiniDisc player clicks and whirs to life in my pocket, that familiar mechanical sound cutting through the acrid stench of burnt insulation and corrosive battery fluid. Elias Morvain's voice emerges from the cheap earbuds—tinny, digitized, preserved like some artifact from before the Breach when men like him still thought they could play god with geometry and human blood.

"Prophecy One: The Gilded Cage."

His High-Spire accent drips with the kind of casual cruelty only old money can produce. Behind his words, I hear the vault's ruin settling around me—the hiss of ruptured arcane batteries spitting blue-black ichor across cracked marble, the groan of stressed support beams high above where the IA strike teams are already positioning themselves.

"The Telluric Grid was never about power distribution," Morvain says, his recorded voice carrying that same condescending tone he used at board meetings when explaining why another South Side neighborhood needed 'redevelopment.' "That was the public story. The convenient lie for the rubes and the politicians."

I crouch lower behind an overturned evidence locker, keeping my head below the level of the entrance. My hand finds the grip of my .45, weight solid and real in ways these corporate prophecies will never be.

"It's a containment circle. A magnificent, city-wide ward designed by the Architects themselves during the Great War. Five Pylons anchor it—Kearny, Newark, Secaucus, Paterson, and Sub-Station Seven. Between them runs currents of bound chaos, raw apotheosis held back by mathematical precision and sacrifice."

The MiniDisc player clicks to track two. Footsteps echo in the corridor outside. Heavy boots on metal grating. At least four sets.

"They say Apep sleeps beneath us. They're right, but sleeping is a generous term. It's more accurate to call it... waiting. And the grid keeps it waiting. For now."

Morvain takes what must have been a sip of expensive scotch before recording this section. I can almost smell the peat smoke through the compression artifacts.

"We've calculated the variables. Tested the bloodlines. Cross-referenced genetic markers with arcane aptitude scores and psychological profiles. The formula requires two catalysts: one vessel of pure elemental fire, untrained and uncorrupted—the Vance girl fits this parameter perfectly—and one anchor point. Someone who has already touched the grid, someone whose soul bears the scars of its geometry."

My knuckles whiten around the pistol grip.

"Cole Reyes. South Side machinist. Survivor of the 2003 substation incident at Pulaski. His resonance signature matches our requirements exactly. We'll position him as her guardian. Her mentor. He won't know he's part of the equation. Neither will she, initially."

The footsteps stop just outside the vault entrance. Someone whispers something I can't quite make out over Morvain's voice.

"The plan is elegant in its simplicity. We introduce Mara Vance to the grid's existence when she turns eighteen. Cole Reyes will be there to 'protect' her. We'll let them bond. Let them trust each other. Then we feed them pieces of the truth—controlled releases of information that build toward a single inevitable conclusion."

I pull the earbuds out for a second, cocking my head. The IA team hasn't moved yet. They're waiting for something. Reinforcements maybe, or they're trying to flush me out.

I jam the buds back in.

"And then comes the beautiful part," Morvain continues, his voice dropping into something close to genuine enthusiasm. "The grid is designed to prevent awakening. But it was built by human hands following Architect principles. It has a weakness. An exploit. When a being of sufficient power and emotional turmoil connects simultaneously with all five Pylons while channeling both fire and grounding currents..." He lets the sentence hang.

"You create a paradox. A mathematical impossibility that tears the containment circle apart from within. Not by destroying it, but by completing it so perfectly that its purpose inverts. The cage becomes a key. The seal becomes an invitation."

The MiniDisc clicks again. Track four now.

"We estimate a seventy-eight percent probability that both subjects will survive the initial breach. Long-term survival rates drop significantly once Apep begins feeding on the city's life-force, but that's acceptable collateral damage. The Board has already begun preparing evacuation protocols for essential personnel."

My jaw clenches so hard I can taste blood.

"Cole Reyes will likely die during the process itself. His role as anchor point means he'll absorb the majority of the feedback from the grid's inversion. Unfortunate, but necessary. The Vance girl should survive long enough to serve our primary objective: ensuring the Breach occurs before our competitors at Con Edison or the European Consortium can weaponize the technology first."

Outside, metal scrapes against concrete. They're setting up something. Maybe a flash-bang launcher, maybe worse.

"This recording is meant only for senior BATC Internal Affairs. Prophecy One is now active. Both subjects have been positioned and are proceeding according to schedule. Expect full manifestation by 2008. Record ends."

Silence except for my own breathing and the distant hum of the Telluric current running beneath this very building.

I pull out the earbuds and let them dangle from their cord. My hands aren't shaking. That should probably worry me more than it does.

"You miscalculated," I whisper into the ruined vault, voice barely audible over the sizzle of corrosive battery acid eating through marble flooring. "You calculated for a girl who'd break. For a machinist who'd die without question."

The red laser-tripwires sweep across the entrance, painting geometric patterns on shattered safety glass.

"I didn't break. Cole didn't die."

I stand up, .45 held in both hands.

"And when we come for you—when we burn Sub-Station Seven down around your precious Board—the only prophecy you'll be hearing is the one written in fire and screams."

* * *

The MiniDisc spins in my hand, its chrome surface catching the sickly yellow glow from the sodium-vapor lamps bleeding through the clinic's reinforced windows. Five-fifteen AM. I haven't slept. Haven't wanted to.

Mara's gone. Not dead—I'd know that in my bones—but *gone*. Vanished off the grid like smoke through a screen door.

I press play.

Static hisses, then her voice. Raw. Unfiltered.

*"Cole, if you're hearing this, I'm probably already—"*

A muffled crash. Suppressed gunfire. Her sharp intake of breath.

*"They came for me at the drop point. Corporate security. Not the usual muscle—these were Elven tacticals. IA Division. Someone tipped them off."*

Another sound. Wet pavement underfoot. Rain. The *thwip-thwip-thwip* of rounds tracing her path.

*"The MiniDisc... it's real, Cole. Everything Lysandra said is real. The prophecy, the Pylons, all of it. But there's more you need to know about your father. About what he really was."*

Her breathing gets heavier. Faster.

*"He wasn't just some machinist who stumbled onto something he shouldn't have. He *built* part of the system. And they killed him because he tried to dismantle it from inside."*

The recording cuts. Static fills the headphones.

I yank them off, fingers trembling. The frost-cobalt aura sparks around my knuckles—not from anger, but from the cold knot settling in my gut. All these years thinking my old man was just another victim caught between gears too big for him. Another South Side casualty.

But if what Mara's saying is true...

The clinic door opens behind me. Doc Maloney shuffles in, still pulling on his coat. His eyes are bloodshot, face gray with exhaustion. "You look like shit, kid."

"I need everything you've got on my father. Every schematic, every project file, every goddamn invoice."

He blinks slowly, processing. Then nods toward his workstation. "It's all there. Been meaning to give it to you anyway. Found it in a hidden partition when I was restoring his old terminal." He pauses, studying my face. "What'd you find?"

I hold up the MiniDisc. "My mother."

That stops him cold. "Thought she died in—"

"She did. Twenty-two years ago." I move past him to the terminal, sliding the disc into the drive. Files unfurl across the screen. Video logs. Audio transcripts. Schematics for something called *Project Chimera*.

And a photograph.

Me. Younger, maybe seven or eight. Standing beside a woman with Mara's eyes and my father's stubborn jawline. She has her arm around a man who looks exactly like me—but older, harder, with the same frost-cobalt burning around his fingertips.

My father. Alive.

Doc comes up behind me, breathing sharp. "Jesus Christ, Cole..."

The next file pulses red. A timestamp: 3:47 AM. Forty-three minutes ago.

A video chat request from an encrypted source.

I accept.

Lysandra appears on screen. Unchanged by time—the same sharp features, same calculating intelligence in her gaze. The same woman from my nightmares and my half-remembered dreams.

"Hello, Sliver," she says softly. "Did Mara survive?"

My jaw tightens. "Where is she?"

"You're both alive. That's more than I hoped for." She leans back in her chair—some sterile white room that could be anywhere in the tri-state area. Corporate. Clean. Dangerous. "We have about thirty hours before IA Division traces your location. You need to listen carefully."

"I'm listening."

"The Pylons aren't just power stations, Cole. They're anchors. Seven of them, spread across North Jersey, holding open rifts between dimensions." Her expression hardens. "Your father discovered how to close them. Permanently. But doing so requires someone with a direct bloodline to manipulate the resonance fields—someone like you."

"You want me to destroy them."

"No." A pause. "I want you to *reclaim* them. The Millennium Breach wasn't an accident, Sliver. It was engineered by Pylon Corp as a control mechanism. Your father built the master kill-switch, but they buried it inside Sub-Station Seven. To access it, you'll need both of us."

The screen flickers. Another window opens, showing a live feed of the Kearny marshes. And there, standing waist-deep in the polluted water beneath the Pulaski Skyway, is Mara. Alive. Whole. Rain streaming down her face.

She looks directly into the camera.

"Cole," she breathes. "Mom's telling the truth. About everything. But we can't do this alone. You know who else we need."

Behind her, emerging from the neon-soaked shadows beneath the bridge, comes a figure I haven't seen since before the Breach tore reality apart.

Gunner.

Still wearing his security guard uniform, still carrying that .357 like it weighs nothing, still looking like he'd rather be anywhere else.

"We've got a team," Lysandra continues. "We've got motive. What we don't have is time. IA Division has already locked down three of the Pylons. They're hunting for anyone connected to your father's research."

I look at Doc Maloney. He meets my eyes, gives one curt nod.

"When?" I ask.

"Dawn. We hit Pylon #4 first—it's the weakest and controls the western approaches to Neon Hollow." She smiles grimly. "Be ready to burn bright, Sliver. This ends with either all of us free or none of us breathing."

The call disconnects.

Outside, the sky begins to bleed gray. The city wakes up, oblivious as always to the gears turning beneath its feet. To the rifts bleeding between worlds. To the machinist's son planning to tear it all down.

My hands crackle with frost-cobalt fire.

Thirty hours until war.

Time to get to work.

## CHAPTER 3: THE SLIVER OF JUSTICE

The IBM ThinkPad's screen casts a sickly green glow across Lysandra's face as she scrolls through the November 7th, 2005 casualty report. Each line of text is another weight on my shoulders, and I'm already drowning.

"Three hundred fourteen," she says, her voice cracking in a way I've never heard before. The Stoic Grandmother, unraveling. "The Pulaski Skyway commuter train, 6:47 PM. Twenty-three humans. Forty-seven Elves—mostly Woodlanders from the Meadowlands enclave. Thirty-nine Dwarves from the Lincoln Tunnel mines. Twenty-five Orcs who'd been working construction on Route 1-9."

She stops scrolling. The cursor blinks like a damned metronome counting down my life.

"The BATC boardroom was three miles away," Lysandra continues, turning to face me fully now. Her amber eyes reflect that green monitor light, making her look demonic in the Under-City gloom. "Vane Thorne and Elias Morvain were having scotch. Watching the telemetry from their private screens while you stood at Sub-Station Seven with thirty-eight Orphans behind wards that had been deliberately compromised."

## CHAPTER 5: THE LOCAL 405 ALLIANCE

## CHAPTER 15: CHAPTER 15

The tunnel reeks of ozone and something worse—something biological that makes my stomach clench even behind the filtration. I lean against Cole's shoulder, and he doesn't shake me off this time. Good. Because if I let go now, I think I'll dissolve into the darkness around us like sugar in water.

"Easy there, Commander," Cole murmurs, one arm steadying my waist while his other keeps his MP5 low and ready. "We're almost to the sanctuaries."

Almost. That word tastes like rust.

The splice-tunnel spreads before us like a mechanical nervous system—thousands upon thousands of fiber-optic cables stolen from BATC mainlines, bundled together in jury-rigged conduits that hum with barely-contained data streams. Some glow faintly blue in the dark, pulsing like bioluminescent veins. Others are dead black, severed and weeping drops of resin that hit the concrete floor with wet *plops*.

"We need to move faster," I whisper, but even saying it exhausts me. The wards are screaming. They've been screaming since we left Sub-Station Seven, a constant high-pitched whine at the edge of my consciousness that makes my teeth ache. Every step feels like wading through honey made of broken glass.

"That way," Cole points with his chin toward a branching corridor where the cables split into different directions. Water drips somewhere in the distance—steady, rhythmic. The clicking sounds start up again. Multiple sources. Close.

Then they emerge from the shadows, and I have to fight not to trigger every defensive ward I've got loaded.

Goblins. Wire-runners. Six of them, maybe seven—I can't quite focus enough to count properly. Short, wiry metahumans with skin the color of old parchment and eyes that catch what little light there is and reflect it back in harsh yellow glints. They move with an unsettling economy of motion, each one holding electrified crowbars that crackle with contained lightning.

"Hold it right there!" The lead Goblin steps forward, scarred face twisted into something between a sneer and suspicion. His crowbar arcs between us like a miniature sun. "Corp-sec crawling all over the upper pipes, mechanic. You kicked the hornet's nest."

Cole's hand doesn't go for his weapon. Instead, he reaches into his pocket with deliberate slowness and produces a heavy iron coin, the Watching Party insignia etched so deep into its surface you could read it by touch alone.

The Goblin's eyes widen slightly. The others shift their weight but don't lower their weapons yet.

"I need passage to the clinic," Cole says, voice flat and certain. "Shadow Mothers owe me a favor. This woman needs medical attention that can't be traced."

There's a long moment where time seems to stretch. My wards are flickering now—not from any external threat but from my own fraying concentration. A pulse of cold blue light washes across our small group, and I hear one of the Goblins hiss.

Rix—at least, I think that's what Cole called him—examines the coin more closely, then looks up at me. Those yellow eyes bore into mine, and I see intelligence there, sharp and calculating. He's cataloguing every detail: my BATC uniform, the way my hands shake, the faint glow around my fingers where the wards are deteriorating.

"Payment first." His voice is guttural, accented in ways I can't quite parse. "Telluric copper. Pure shavings. Enough to grease three sensor grids."

Cole produces another item from his coat—a small leather pouch that he tosses to Rix with a practiced flick. The Goblin catches it, weighs it in his palm, then tears it open with his teeth. He sniffs the contents, nods once.

"Through the BATC thermal scanners won't pick us up," Rix says, already turning away. "But stay close. Step wrong, they'll have your heat signatures in seconds."

We move. Fast now. Following the wire-runners through passages that seem to shift and breathe around us. The cables overhead create patterns I almost recognize—they're routed according to some logic, some structure—but exhaustion makes it impossible to focus on.

My foot catches on an uneven section of concrete, and I go down hard. Pain lances up my ankle. My wards flare violently, not from any conscious command but from pure trauma response, flooding the tunnel with cascading barriers of cold blue light that reflect off the dripping walls like a thunderstorm trapped underground.

"Commander!" Cole drops to his knees beside me, one hand steadying my shoulder while the other keeps his weapon trained on the darkness ahead.

Rix stops, turns back with obvious irritation. But there's something else in his expression too—concern? Pity?

"We don't have time for this," the Goblin says, but he doesn't keep walking either. Instead, he crouches beside my other side without asking permission and looks into my eyes with an intensity that feels invasive. "You're bleeding out. Not physical. Wards are eating you alive."

I want to tell him he's wrong, that I'm fine, that we need to keep moving. But the words dissolve before they reach my tongue.

The ash-gray static is waiting at the edges of my vision again. Closer now. Hungry.

* * *

The tunnel floor rushes up to meet me, but it never connects. Arms catch me—strong, solid arms that know exactly how much pressure to use without hurting. Cole's grip is firm as iron around my waist, his chest against mine as he arrests my fall with effortless control.

My vision swims. Everything bleeds together—wet concrete walls, dripping stalactites of rust-streaked condensation, the faces of the Goblin crew watching with those unsettling amber eyes. Then there's something else. Something wrong.

*Smoke.*

I can't see it, but I *smell* it—that acrid burn of wet charred wool mixed with ash-gray static, like old newspapers catching fire in damp basement air. My nostrils flare involuntarily. Sub-Station Seven. That godforsaken place trying to drag me back down through time, through failure, through the memory of screaming commuters and burning flesh.

"No," I hear myself say, voice thin and distant. "No, no, no—"

Cole's hand cups the back of my head, pressing my face against his shoulder. The smell of him cuts through everything else—clean sweat, leather, and underneath it all that particular warmth of hearth-coal magic, like standing too close to a bonfire on a winter night. It's solid. Real. *Present tense.*

"Stay with me, Mara." His voice rumbles low against my ear, each word deliberate and measured. "You left the ash behind. You're here. You're breathing. We won."

His forehead presses to mine. Heat floods through the contact point—not just warmth but *certainty*, that frost-cobalt resolve that makes Cole who he is. It pours into me like water finding cracks in stone, forcing its way into every chaotic, tangled thread of my Weave where the static wants to take root.

My fingers clutch at his tactical vest, gripping the fabric hard enough to wrinkle it. The texture is real—I can feel every seam, every reinforced panel. This isn't memory or psychic echo. This is *now*.

"I'm here, Cole." My voice finds strength, pushes past the trembling. "I'm here."

The ash-gray recedes like fog burned away by morning sun. In its place blooms that pure white-lilac luminescence, brilliant and clean, filling my vision until there's nothing else. Cole's magic intertwines with mine—his blood-iron crimson threading through my violet like iron filings drawn to magnetism. Together they create something stronger than either alone.

Grimjaw watches from several feet away, arms crossed over his barrel chest. The Goblin chief's expression is unreadable—whether it's respect or concern or some combination I can't parse. Behind him, the rest of the crew maintains distance, their clicking conversation dropping to near-silence as they observe.

"You good?" Grimjaw asks finally, his gravel-and-sandpaper voice cutting through the tunnel's ambient drips.

I nod against Cole's shoulder before pulling back just enough to meet his eyes. His gaze holds mine steady, searching for any sign of regression. Finding none, the tension leaves his shoulders incrementally.

"Yeah," I say, straightening but keeping one hand braced against Cole's arm. "Just... needed a minute."

"Understood." Grimjaw jerks his chin toward the tunnel ahead. "Bunker's three hundred meters. Keep moving, keep quiet. Gnome security doesn't take kindly to surprise visitors."

We start forward again, Cole matching my pace without needing instruction. The dripping grows louder as we walk, water finding every crack in the aging infrastructure above us. Somewhere in the darkness ahead, light begins to glow—not the sickly yellow of sodium lamps but something cleaner, brighter.

The tunnel opens suddenly into vastness.

The bunker spreads out before us like a cathedral carved from concrete and steel. Overhead lights—fluorescent arrays mounted on industrial scaffolding—bathe everything in clinical white brightness that makes me squint after the tunnels' gloom. The air here smells different: sharp alchemical solvents cutting through antiseptic sterilization fluid, and underneath it all something almost comforting—fresh coffee brewing somewhere beyond our line of sight.

The floor is pristine polished concrete marked with yellow safety lines and caution tape. Workstations line the walls, most currently dark but clearly functional. In the center, raised platforms hold what look like research equipment covered in protective sheets.

"Gnomish design," Grimjaw mutters with something approaching pride. "Built this place themselves, before the Breach. Hasn't been touched since."

"Where are they?" I ask, scanning for signs of occupation. "Your people?"

"They come at night mostly. Daytime brings too many human patrols overhead." He gestures vaguely upward. "This deep, they can't hear us. But we don't push it."

Cole's hand finds mine, fingers lacing together briefly before releasing. Message received: *I've got your back.*

"Alright," I say, voice returning to its command tone now that the static has cleared completely. "We need to establish contact with whoever's running this operation. And then we need to figure out how the hell we're going to get to Sub-Station Seven without Marcus Voss's goons intercepting us."

"One problem at a time, Commander." Cole's smile is slight but present. "You just gave him terminal case of ego-death. Pretty sure he won't be coordinating anything for the next few hours while his consciousness reassembles itself."

"Good," I say flatly. "Because I'm not done with him yet."

* * *

The door hisses open.

I don't remember walking through it. The corridor behind us dissolves into static—white noise bleeding between dimensions—and then we're standing in a cathedral of technology that shouldn't exist beneath Neon Hollow's rust-slicked streets.

Banks of IBM ThinkPads glow like tombstones in a graveyard of old gods. Sony monitors line every wall, their green phosphorescent displays tracking BATC movements across the city in real-time. I can see our own heat signatures crawling through the network like parasites on silk. The hum of server racks fills my skull, vibrating through bone and filling the spaces where rational thought used to live.

"Commander Vance." Elena Reyes steps forward, her tactical gear stained with blood I recognize as her own. Behind her, kneeling beside what looks like an ancient mainframe jury-rigged with alchemical condensers, is a woman who makes me forget how to breathe.

Silver-streaked hair catches the blue-white light from the monitors. Eyes—violet eyes exactly like mine, but older, harder, carrying the weight of secrets that could crack continents—lift from a scrolling terminal display.

Mom.

My mother.

Lysandra Vance.

Alive.

The PalmPilot slips from my fingers. It clatters against concrete, and somewhere in the back of my mind I register the sound of plastic shattering, but I can't look away from her face. Can't process this as anything other than hallucination born from too many nights haunting the Kearny marshes, searching for ghosts in the fog.

She stands slowly, deliberately, and I'm moving before my brain catches up to intention. Three steps. Five. Seven. My legs are shaking so badly I barely make it across the room before my knees buckle.

Her arms catch me.

I bury my face in her shoulder, and the sobs that tear out of my chest sound like they're coming from someone else's throat—raw and desperate and six years late. She smells like ozone and old books and the particular scent of rain-soaked asphalt after a thunderstorm, and it's all I can do not to dissolve into nothing just to stay here, pressed against her, feeling her hand stroke my hair.

"Beautiful girl," she whispers, her voice thick with emotion but carrying that steel I remember from briefings when I was eight, watching her coordinate shadow-war operations through cracked satellite feeds. "You survived. You did so well."

I can't speak. Can only cling to her like she's the only solid thing in a universe gone liquid around us.

Over my shoulder, I feel her gaze shift. Feel the temperature drop by ten degrees as her attention focuses on Cole.

"You brought him." Her voice changes. The warmth bleeds out, replaced by tactical certainty that could carve through dimensional barriers. "Cole Brennan. The Cord-Bearer. You have the Toughbook?"

I force myself to lift my head, blinking tears away. Cole stands frozen near the doorway, his face pale beneath his tan, the IBM ThinkPad held like a holy relic he's afraid might shatter if he breathes wrong on it.

He nods jerkily, holding up the device.

Lysandra's expression doesn't soften. If anything, it hardens further as she gently extracts herself from my grip—I want to protest, to hold on tighter—but she guides me to stand beside Elena instead. Her violet eyes lock onto Cole with predatory focus.

"The coordinates for Pylon Five. The Crown Spire." It's not a question. "But to breach it..." She pauses, and I see something flicker across her face—regret? fear? Something ancient and terrible. "...we have to awaken something worse than Apep."

The server racks hum their hypnotic dirge.

Somewhere above us, three floors up, BATC patrols continue their endless rounds through Sub-Station Seven's corridors, oblivious to the abyss we're standing at the edge of.

And I realize with bone-deep certainty that whatever comes next will break us all.
