## CHAPTER 1: THE CRUCIBLE OF THE SERPENT (DUAL POV

The catwalk groans beneath my weight as I crouch low, sodium vapor lamps throwing sick orange across rusted steel. Below us, eight hundred thousand gallons of Apep's Venom churn like a dying god.

"Scale of operation," Cole whispers beside me—dead silent, no inflection, just statement of fact. His frost-cobalt aura bleeds cold across the metal railing, ice crystals spreading from where his fingers grip white-knuckled. The temperature drops ten degrees instantly. Condensation on the pipes freezes mid-drip.

I don't answer immediately. My eyes track the movement below—the reinforced glass platform suspended over the crucible, Elias Morvain standing there with Lord Malvourne like they're conducting some demonic symphony. They're orchestrating the flow now, directing massive turbine pipes to pour the black sludge at precise intervals against the Fifth Nail.

The industrial roar is deafening. Eight hundred thousand gallons doesn't move quietly. It *screams* through the facility's veins.

"The High-Spire guards down there," I finally say, keeping my voice low enough that it barely carries past Cole's ear. "Heavily augmented. Twin-rifles, telluric armor plating." I shift my weight, feeling the BATC combat rig against my spine. "We go in hard or we go in smart."

Cole's head turns fractionally toward me, but he doesn't look away from the pit. "Danny's still locked in that Arcane Iron-Lung. Across the city. Running out of air with every minute we waste watching this." His jaw tightens. "Smart gets people killed."

The Venom slaps against one of the massive steel spikes holding the Fifth Nail together. The impact reverberates up through the catwalk into my knees. The entire Pylon shudders—structural groan, metal fatigue. My telluric senses pick up pressure building in the chamber, a mounting headache behind my eyes as ambient energy density climbs.

"Malvourne's pushing something," I mutter, fingers finding the safety on my sidearm. "The sludge level just hit critical marker three. See those stress fractures spreading along the support trusses?"

Another shudder runs through the facility. Harder this time. The sodium lamps flicker—orange shadows dancing across Morvain's smile as he watches his creation build toward some terrible crescendo.

Cole's aura flares brighter. Ice spreads faster now, encasing half the railing in crystalline armor. "I can drop us down. Thirty-foot fall, maybe twenty if I slow it enough." He glances at me for the first time since we positioned ourselves. "You'll need to be ready the second our boots touch glass."

Below, Malvourne raises one hand. The turbines surge—a collective gasp from hundreds of thousands of gallons being forced through narrower channels. The crucible begins to rise.

"The Fifth Nail is groaning under pressure," I say, urgency bleeding into my voice despite years of training designed to strip emotion from tactical assessment. "When that thing gives way—"

It groans again. Louder. Metal screaming against metal.

"Now," Cole says, and his frost-cobalt aura explodes outward like a supernova of cold.

* * *

I snap my PalmPilot open with one hand, the other still gripping the crucible's obsidian rim. The green phosphorescence washes across my face, illuminating the sweat beads tracking down my temples. My fingers fly across the miniature keyboard—hardwired commands shooting through the rubber-jacketed cable threaded under my tactical vest into the Panasonic Toughbook slung against my spine.

The diagnostic floods the screen in a cascade of red.

"Cole," I say, voice flat despite the ice forming in my gut. "Tell me you see this."

The containment fields are hemorrhaging. Not degrading—they're *bleeding* magical energy at rates that defy thermodynamics. The weave patterns show Morvain's signature all over it: those jagged, serpentine threads that twist probability itself into knots. But there's something else underneath, something I didn't anticipate.

"Siphoning telluric pressure," I mutter, pulling up layer after layer of arcane telemetry. "He's not just trying to break the seal. He's compressing the crucible's ambient magic into a singular point-state." My thumb hovers over a calculation matrix. When I run it, the result makes my stomach drop. "Target: structural integrity failure of Pylon Five. ETA approximately twelve minutes based on current bleed rate."

The air around the crucible pulses with malevolent heat. Where my white-lilac flame meets the obsidian, the stone smokes and drips like wax. I can feel Cole's hearth-coal warmth pressing against the back of my mind—that constant anchor keeping the ash-gray static of full-blown panic from consuming my focus. Without him, I'd already be lost to the crucible's hunger.

My earpiece crackles with interference, then a Gnome voice cuts through—high-pitched, urgent, speaking in rapid-fire technical argot that means they've cracked the Nextel network frequencies again.

"—structural integrity Fifth Nail falling below threshold—less than fifteen minutes to catastrophic failure—repeat, less than fifteen minutes—"

I key my mic. "Confirm breach containment. Isolate the vector."

The Gnome's response is drowned by another wave of static, but the meaning comes through clear enough. They're seeing what I'm seeing: Morvain's gambit. He doesn't need to escape the crucible if he can turn it into a bomb that takes half the Pylon with it.

"Mara—" Cole's voice tightens. Something in his tone makes me glance up from the PalmPilot's hellish readouts.

That's when I see her.

Topside, maybe forty yards along the adjacent gantry, an Elven blood-sniper in tactical black has her rifle raised. The weapon's barrel gleams with etched runes—woven steel designed to punch through magical shielding like paper. She hasn't fired yet, but she's tracking something. When I follow her line of sight, I realize the glow from my Toughbook screen is painting a nice bright target on my position.

The sniper's eyes lock onto mine. Even at this distance, I can see the predatory focus there, the cold calculation of a High-Spire operator who knows exactly how much damage her rune-bullets will do to someone standing in front of an active crucible.

My white-lilac flame flares instinctively, responding to the spike of adrenaline. The air around us shimmers, heat and magic warring for dominance. Behind me, the crucible *screams*—that sound beyond hearing that tears at your bones and sanity alike.

Thirteen minutes now.

I snap the PalmPilot shut, green light dying on my face. My hand drops to my sidearm as I scan for cover that isn't behind us—the only direction that matters anymore.

"Sniper, twelve o'clock high," I tell Cole, keeping my voice low enough that the wind might carry it away from listening ears. "Rune-weapon. They want to take out the Aegis commander before we can stop Morvain's countdown."

The blood-sniper shifts her weight, settling into her aim. Through the scope I can see glinting even from here—I'd bet my commission she's got some kind of targeting enhancement charm woven into that rifle.

She doesn't fire yet though. Waiting for orders maybe, or just enjoying the moment when prey realizes the hunter has them dead-to-rights.

Either way, we're running out of time in more ways than one.

* * *

The shot comes from my left—high caliber, suppressed. I don't think; frost-cobalt reflexes take over and I'm slamming Commander Vance down onto the steel grating as a depleted-bismuth slug screams through the space her head occupied a microsecond before.

"Contact!" I bark, already rolling us behind a rusted support beam as another round spangs off the metal framework above us. The sniper's positioned somewhere in the skeletal superstructure of the gantry crane, maybe thirty meters out, elevated position giving them the advantage.

Stealth protocol is blown. Doesn't matter now.

I draw my sidearm—a rune-etched Glock modified for kinetic amplification—and check on Vance. Her eyes are sharp, calculating. She nods once, understanding. We move as a coordinated unit now, not infiltrators but assault team.

"Fanning right," she whispers, her voice barely audible over the industrial din below. "You cover high ground."

I rack the bolt on my MP5—*clack*—the sound deafeningly loud in this confined space. The weapon hums with residual arcano-sigils, glowing faintly blue-white along its frame. Good. We'll need every edge.

Vance moves first, her form dissolving into wisps of white-lilac energy as she engages her Aegis Commander aspect. Three shots, precise and measured, echo from her direction. The sounds of bodies hitting the catwalk follow immediately.

"Clear," she reports. "Four tangos down."

But those weren't our only problems. Heavy boots thunder across metal grating—reinforcements pouring up the service ladder. At least six corporate security personnel in tactical gear, weapons drawn and ready. They fan out methodically, professionals covering each other's approach.

No time for subtlety.

I slam my palm against the steel floor and feel the blood-iron metallurgy surge through my system. The metal screams—literally *screams*—as it tears itself free from the structural integrity of the gantry. The entire section of grating beneath three advancing guards buckles and rips away, their eyes widening in shock as they plummet into the black industrial sludge twenty feet below.

Splash. Thud. Silence.

Two more guards open fire. I duck behind an I-beam as rounds chew through the air where I stood. Vance returns fire, her kinetic blasts striking true—their armor sparking and sizzling as white-lilac energy punches through reinforced plating. Cordite fills the air, mixing with the acrid smell of burning metal.

We're making too much noise now. Too much heat. But we can't stop—not when we're this close to the primary causeway doors.

"Move!" Vance shouts, and we sprint toward the massive blast doors marking the entrance to Pylon #5 proper. One guard makes a desperate lunge at her from behind a generator housing, but she doesn't even look back—just flicks her wrist and sends him flying backward with a pulse of raw Aegis authority that cracks his ribs audibly.

The door keypad glows orange in the darkness ahead. We need access codes. Problem is, every BATC asset we've encountered so far went down before they could talk.

Except one.

A wounded Elven guard drags himself along the grating, crimson blood trailing from a gut wound that's clearly fatal. His blue skin has gone pale gray, eyes glassy with pain and fear. He clutches his side weakly as I approach, my weapon trained on his head.

"I don't have time for this," I tell him flatly, my voice stripped of any warmth or humanity. The Threefold Cord demands results, not mercy. "The override code. Now."

He spits at me—a gesture of defiance born from terror rather than courage.

Fine.

I aim for his knee instead. The shot echoes like thunder in the enclosed space. He screams, high and thin, clutching at his shattered leg.

"Last chance," I say, stepping closer until my boot is inches from his face. "Code. Or you die slow."

Through gritted teeth, barely coherent: "F-Four-seven... eight-three..." Another gasping breath. "Two-one-niner..."

"That's it?" I press.

"...Alpha... Echo... Seven..."

His body goes slack after that—death claiming him instantly. No more information. No confirmation if he gave us everything or held something back out of spite.

Vance is already at the keypad, her fingers flying across the numeric interface. "Entering sequence."

*Beep-beep-beep.*

The massive blast doors shudder—hydraulics groaning to life after years of dormancy. They grind open slowly, agonizingly slowly, revealing what lies beyond.

And oh fuck no.

At least twenty BATC security forces in full tactical armor, weapons locked onto our position. Behind them, the central platform looms—a cathedral of industrial horror where something ancient and terrible awaits.

"Contact!" Vance yells unnecessarily—I can see them clear as day.

"We go through hard!" I shout back, chambering a round with a decisive *click*.

## CHAPTER 2: THE CAUSEWAY SYNDICATE (COLE POV

The muzzles of three assault rifles tracked me like tombstones marking my grave. Behind them stood men in black tactical gear—the kind that costs more than what I made in six months at the machine shop. Their fingers tightened on triggers as Mara's blood pooled between us, spreading across cracked concrete stained with decades of industrial runoff.

Then the world exploded.

The loading bay doors—twisted steel monsters held together by rust and prayer—detonated outward in a shower of shrapnel and sparks. The concussive blast hit me like God's own fist, slamming me backward into the chain-link fence. My ears rang. My vision swam with white static punctuated by dancing red afterimages.

V8 engines screamed. Not the clean, polished sound of new cars, but the guttural roar of heavily modified 1999 Crown Vics pushed well past their redline. Three of them came through the breach like bullets fired from hell itself, their reinforced bumpers crumpling the security posts guarding the entrance. Behind them rolled converted delivery trucks, their sides studded with mismatched armor plates welded together in desperate haste.

Grimjaw emerged from the lead car before it stopped moving, his massive frame hunched low behind a sawed-off shotgun bigger than most people's arms. His green skin caught the emergency lights strobing across the facility, making him look like some nightmare demon ripped straight from the darkest corners of Jersey mythology. He fired twice—*BOOM-BOOM*—and two corporate shooters became sacks of meat hurled against far walls.

"HOLD THE LINE!" His voice cut through the chaos like a chainsaw through bone. Thick South Side accent, every word sharp-edged and mean. "DON'T LET THESE SUIT-WEARING LEECHES PAST THE BARRICADE!"

More of his cavalry poured out. Orc enforcers with crude armor fashioned from scrap metal and pure spite. Dwarves from Local 405, their bodies compact and brutal, wielding makeshift shotguns loaded with buckshot that would punch holes through brick. Razor-Wire Street-Elves moved like phantoms between cover positions, their blades glinting under the harsh fluorescent lights that still somehow flickered above us.

The Goblin splice-techs scrambled into position behind the vehicles, hooking up jury-rigged power cells to portable laser arrays. The devices whined to life, painting red targeting beams across the chests of BATC mercenaries who were only now recovering from their shock.

The shooting started then. Really started.

Machine gun fire transformed night into day. Muzzle flashes lit up the causeway in rapid stuttering bursts, each one accompanied by the deafening crack-crack-crack of rounds traveling faster than sound. Sparks flew where bullets struck steel. Bullet casings rained down like metallic snow, clattering against the concrete in a steady percussion that merged with the gunfire into something primal and terrible.

I dragged myself toward Mara, my shoulder screaming where I'd slammed into the fence. Blood—hers or mine, I couldn't tell anymore—coated my hands as I grabbed her arm.

"Up! Get up, damn it!" I hauled her behind a stack of shipping containers, ignoring the way my muscles protested. The air tasted like copper and burning rubber from the Crown Vics' tires, which had been shredded by return fire but kept spinning anyway, driven purely by momentum and hate.

An Orc the size of a refrigerator took a burst to the chest at point-blank range. His body jerked like a puppet on strings, blood spraying across his brothers-in-arms before he fell. Another was decapitated by a sniper's round, his head tumbling end over end through the smoke-choked air.

Grimjaw fired his shotgun again, then again, the weapon bucking in his massive hands like an angry beast. "KILL THE SUITS! KILL THEM ALL!"

Behind them—the corporate forces—that massive shape moved into view. The BATC riot-mech stood fifteen feet tall, its armor plating gleaming under the emergency lights. Rotary cannons mounted on both arms began to spin up, the whine building from mechanical groan to furious howl.

The thing raised one arm, targeting systems painting our positions in cold blue light.

"This is bad," I muttered to Mara.

She nodded, eyes wide with fear but also something else. Determination maybe. Or just stubborn refusal to die here on this forgotten stretch of Jersey concrete.

The mech fired.

Bullets tore through the causeway like a storm of metal rage, turning cover into confetti and flesh into red mist.

* * *

The gunfire sounds like hail on tin, each round smacking into the reinforced concrete barrier we're huddled behind. I can smell ozone and motor oil, that particular stench of dwarven engineering that clings to everything in the South Side yards.

"Busbar's hot, Cole! Give us three mikes before the grid strokes out!"

Torque Kaelen's voice cuts through the chaos like a blade. He's hunched over a tangle of copper conductors, his massive hands moving with surprising delicacy as he strips insulation with a combat knife. Behind him, Bram Kovacs is sweating bullets—literally—and sweat. The master machinist has his coveralls rolled up past his elbows, forearms corded with muscle as he torque-wrenches connections tighter than a virgin's asshole at a brothel.

I peek around our barrier. The riot-mech looms thirty feet away, its arcane shielding flickering like heat shimmer above cracked asphalt. Those blue-white distortions are all that keep us from turning into pink mist. The thing's servos whine as it tracks movement, that targeting array sweeping methodically across the Crown Vault's parking structure.

"They're jamming the frequencies!" Some gnome I don't recognize, bald head gleaming under the sodium lights, is crouched behind a beat-to-shit Crown Vic. His IBM ThinkPad sits open on the hood, cooling fans whining like angry hornets. Twin Motorola Razrs cradle in his lap, those prehistoric bricks somehow still doing the job when it counts.

The busbar between Torque and Bram begins to hum. Not just hum—the fucking thing starts to *sing*, a high-frequency keening that sets my teeth on edge. Electricity crawls along its surface like living things, blue arcs leaping from terminal to terminal in rhythmic pulses.

"Two minutes!" Bram slams a red button on the stolen generator, black grease smeared across his knuckles. "Tell your boy toys to make it count!"

I take the magazine he thrusts at me. Cold metal, warm wood—the weight of it feels right even though everything else has gone sideways tonight. I check the loads: bismuth slugs, each one machined with precision that would make a gunsmith weep. These'll punch through armor plating if God smiles.

"We need that platform door open," I shout back. "Once those shields drop, we got maybe ten seconds before the whole ritual space seals itself!"

Torque doesn't look up from his work, but his hands speed up, fingers flying over connections. "Can't control what we can't hack! The gnomes are working on it!"

Another burst of gunfire. Something ricochets off the concrete above our heads, sending chunks of aggregate pattering down onto Bram's coveralls. He doesn't flinch. Dwarves were forged in furnaces hotter than this shit anyway.

The busbar is practically screaming now, electricity cascading in sheets of blue-white fire. Sparks rain down onto the oil-stained pavement, sizzling where they hit puddles of leaked hydraulic fluid. The smell intensifies—burnt electronics and hot copper.

"Sixty seconds!"

My heart hammers against my ribs like it wants out. I rack the slide on the modified rifle, chambering one of Bram's special rounds. The click echoes in my ears. Outside our barrier, shadows dance as the riot-mech pivots, its targeting system finally locking onto the gnome's position behind the Crown Vic.

"No no no—"

The gnome dives as three micro-missiles launch from the mech's shoulder pods. They streak across the parking structure in trailing plumes of smoke, impacting against the Crown Vic with explosions that lift the sedan six inches off the ground. Fireballs roll across the hood, licking at the ThinkPad's cooling fans which keep whining even as plastic melts around them.

"Forty-five seconds and counting!" Torque yells, though his voice sounds distant, muffled by the ringing in my ears.

* * *

The doors were dying.

I heard their death rattle—a grinding scream of tortured metal that cut straight through the gunfire behind us. My boots hit the rust-streaked concrete of the causeway at full sprint, Mara's hand gripping my wrist like a vise. The gap was closing. I could see it narrowing with each heartbeat, a mouth of steel teeth chewing shut.

"FASTER!" Grimjaw's roar split the air. Two of his ogre-kin breachers had stepped into the threshold, their massive frames silhouetted against the orange hell-fire of the battlefield. They moved like industrial machinery given flesh—all hydraulic hiss and piston-snap precision. Their hydraulic rams slammed into the door panels with impacts that sent shock waves through the floor beneath my feet.

The sound was deafening. Metal shrieked. Gears ground in protest. And those two brutes pushed back against tons of closing weight, their muscles bulging grotesquely under mottled gray-green skin, veins standing out like steel cables. Sweat poured down their faces as they braced themselves against the inevitable.

"MOVE IT, REYES! WE CAN'T HOLD THIS!"

Gunfire stitched across the space behind us—Mara's rifle barking in reply, suppressing whatever had followed us through the breach. But I didn't look back. Couldn't afford to. The gap between those closing doors was maybe four feet wide now. Three and a half. My lungs burned. My legs screamed. I hauled Mara forward by pure momentum.

We slid through sideways, our bodies compressed in that narrowing space. My shoulder scraped against cold steel. Mara's knee caught the other side. Then we were tumbling onto the central platform floor, the impact driving the air from my lungs.

*SLAM.*

The doors sealed with a finality that felt cosmic. That grinding metal scream cut off instantly, replaced by an oppressive silence thick enough to taste. Dust settled around us in slow motion, suspended in the dead air. I could feel it—the pressure difference, the absolute cutoff from everything outside this chamber.

The platform stretched before us, lit by sickly green bioluminescence emanating from some kind of crucible at its center. A massive cauldron filled with roiling sludge, chunky and alive with movement. And beside it...

Lord Malvourne turned away from his work.

His eyes weren't human anymore—not entirely. They glowed with a wet crimson light, like infected wounds given sentience, pulsing with their own malignant heartbeat. Rotting flesh peeling from bone. Decay made conscious.

"And here," his voice dripped with ancient poison, "are the final obstacles."

My hand went for my pistol. Beside me, Mara's bio-wards flared brilliant white-lilac, her cedar-scent burning away the oppressive stench of blood-magic that rolled off Malvourne in waves. Her past trauma—all that ash-gray static clawing at her mind—seemed to shrink back from the warmth radiating between us.

Cole Reyes, South Side machinist and whatever-the-fuck-else I'd become tonight.

I leveled my weapon at those glowing corpse-eyes.

## CHAPTER 3: THE BLOOD-CURSE OF MALVOURNE (MARA POV

The air turns viscous before I see it.

A wrongness creeps across the industrial floor like oil spreading backward—blue-black and shimmering, eating through the steel plating between us and Malvourne with a sound like wet tissue paper being torn apart at high speed. The smell hits next: rusted iron mixed with something worse, something biological. Coagulated blood. Old wounds reopened.

"Commander Vance." Malvourne's voice carries across the distance, smooth as polished granite. His smile is all teeth. "How quaint. The BATC sends its gutter-born magician to face the House of Malvourne."

I don't answer. Waste of oxygen. Instead, I step forward, positioning myself squarely in front of Cole's position. My hands come up instinctively, palms out, fingers spread. The weave responds before conscious thought—white-lilac flame erupts from my fingertips, dense and crackling, condensing into a translucent barrier three feet across.

The ancestral rot hits my ward like a physical impact.

Light explodes between us—a blinding concussion of pure force that throws my hair back and makes my teeth ache. Through the glare, I can see the blue-black residue recoiling, then advancing again, persistent as a living thing. It's *hungry*. The magic has weight, texture—it presses against my barrier like cold fingers probing for entry points.

Malvourne laughs, the sound echoing off the vault walls. "Your grandmother's tricks won't—"

I raise one hand without looking away from the curse, jabbing two fingers toward Cole in rapid succession. *Flank left.* He doesn't need words. Professional muscle, trained for exactly this kind of kinetic chaos. While I burn focus maintaining the ward, he'll get angle on the bastard.

The pain starts as a distant humming in my veins.

This is different from normal resistance work. Malvourne's blood-curse isn't just trying to overwhelm my barrier—it's *probing*, invasive, clinical. Threads of corrupted magic worm through microscopic gaps in my concentration, seeking the warmth of my pulse points, looking for a way into my circulatory system. I feel them like ice water injected beneath my skin.

*Triage protocol: Compartmentalize.*

The training kicks in automatically. Pain is information. This level of discomfort is manageable. My barrier stays solid, white-lilac flames licking higher, eating oxygen, burning brighter. The collision point between our magics pulses with an almost organic rhythm—expand, contract, expand.

"Did you know," Malvourne continues conversationally, hands tucked casually in his pockets now, "that your mentor's lighter found its way into my collection? Sub-Station Seven burned so *prettily* that night."

My world narrows to three things: the barrier, the pain, and those last words.

Cole has moved—I see it peripherally. Good. Let him handle the physical approach while I burn through this curse. The iron-gall residue sizzles where it contacts the floor grating beneath my boots. One spot gives way with a metallic shriek, dissolving completely through.

"Your grandmother knew how to weave," Malvourne says, pulling something from his pocket. Silver catches the light. A Zippo. Battered. Familiar. "But she also knew when to run. You lack that particular survival instinct, Commander."

The ward flickers at my momentary distraction.

Blue-black tendrils whip through the gap, wrapping around my left forearm. Cold burns through flesh. I scream—a sharp intake of breath more than anything else—but my hands stay raised. Can't let it break through. If this stuff gets into my bloodstream...

Cole is moving fast now. I can hear his boots on metal, heavy and purposeful.

Malvourne clicks the Zippo open. Spins it. Watches me like he's inspecting livestock.

"The Vance bloodline ends here." He flicks the wheel. Sparks ignite. "In Pylon #5, under the weight of what came before."

* * *

The Zippo flips open with a metallic *clink* that echoes through the suffocating air. Malvourne's face catches the flame—not reflected, but *projected*, his features twisting across the dancing light like something demonic born from smoke.

"Sub-Station Seven," he says, and the words drip with satisfaction. "2005. You were so young then, Mara. So full of idealism."

My blood turns to ice. The ash-gray static doesn't just surge—it *violently* lurches forward, pressing against my white-lilac wards like a living thing trying to claw its way into my skull. I taste copper. I smell it.

*Wet charred wool.*

The stench hits me like a physical assault, transporting me back to that day. Back to the screaming. Back to finding her—a woman who'd taken me in when the streets would've swallowed me whole—crumpled among the wreckage, her hands still outstretched toward where the children had been sleeping. Thirty-eight orphaned souls, snuffed out in seconds by what we'd called an accident. What we'd called a structural failure.

"What?" The word tears from my throat raw and broken.

Malvourne smiles, and it's the most terrible thing I've ever seen. "I made sure every single one of those kids ended up exactly where they needed to be. Positioned for maximum resonance when Apep returned." He takes a drag from his cigarette, exhales smoke that coils around Cole's form. "Your mentor? She was the only one who might have put it together. Had to silence her first."

The platform beneath us vibrates harder now, the sludge below boiling hotter. I can feel it through my boots—the entire structure groaning under pressure that has nothing to do with engineering and everything to do with the reality-warping bullshit currently poisoning this space.

"Liar," I spit the word out like venom. But even as I say it, pieces click into place. The way the investigation never went anywhere. How records were sealed. How fast the BATC moved to bury it all under bureaucratic paperwork and promises of enhanced safety protocols.

How no one came forward after. No witnesses. No survivors except me.

"She died reaching for them," I hear myself saying, voice hollow. Empty. "Tried to save them."

"And failed," Malvourne says simply. "Just like you'll fail tonight, Mara Vance. You're so predictable. So driven by your trauma." His projected face grins wider. "Did you really think this was about stopping Apep? You're just another sacrifice. Another piece in the pattern."

The static is screaming now, a high-pitched whine that threatens to crack my wards wide open. My knees want to buckle. Every instinct screams at me to run, to survive, to *breathe*—

Then I feel him.

Cole.

That steady presence, frost-cobalt and utterly devoid of remorse. He doesn't speak—doesn't need to—but his essence cuts through the psychic storm like a blade through silk. The static shatters against him, dissipating into nothingness.

I look at Malvourne through new eyes.

*He just admitted to murdering thirty-eight children.*

*And her.*

My mentor. The woman who taught me to read when I was seven years old on a cardboard bed in Newark. Who showed me magic existed outside street-level conjuring. Who gave me everything before some monster decided she was inconvenient.

The grief has nowhere left to go.

It weaponizes.

Purity of rage replaces every other emotion in an instant—a cold, crystalline transformation that feels almost holy in its completeness. The white-lilac scent becomes overwhelming, fresh cedar joining it, the ozone sharp enough to cut. My wards don't just hold—they become offensive platforms, crackling with barely-contained violence.

Malvourne notices. His smile falters.

"I signed whose death warrant?" My voice drops an octave, shedding all hesitation, all doubt. Pure South Side North Jersey clip—flat, deadpan, utterly certain. "You're a fucking corpse already, Malvourne. Just haven't stopped moving yet."

Before he can react, I drop my defensive barrier entirely.

The kinetic energy builds around my fists—not gentle, not controlled, but raw and hungry and screaming for release. Every ounce of pain, every moment of loss, every second spent wondering if I could have saved them funnels into this single instant.

I launch forward.

The strike is pure velocity and malice—a kinetic lance that punches through the air directly toward Malvourne's chest, fast enough to shatter bone and rupture organs simultaneously. No finesse. No technique.

Just rage made manifest.

* * *

I hit the ground hard, my knees slamming into concrete that still radiates residual heat from Malvourne's arcane circle. The world tilts sideways for a microsecond—the afterimage of that fucking kinetic strike still burned behind my retinas like a solar flare.

"Reyes!" Cole's voice cracks through the ringing in my ears. Not pain. Fear.

But I don't have time to process it because my heart just decided to try and claw its way out of my ribcage. A metallic *screech* tears through my chest cavity—that's not a sound, that's a *feeling*. Like someone jammed a rusted wire garrote around my aorta and started pulling.

The Sterling-Voss Lead-Bismuth Geas.

Fuck.

My fingers claw at my combat vest as I gasp, air refusing to move past my constricted throat. The implant—corporate insurance, they called it. Standard issue for field operatives. Two grams of lead-bismuth alloy coiled around my heart like a chrome serpent, dormant until...

"You've got exactly one hundred and twenty seconds, Commander."

Morvain's voice booms across the platform, distorted through PA speakers. I can see him through the gaps between shipping containers—a silhouette backlit by crackling energy from the ritual controls. His hand is still pressed against some kind of terminal, glowing symbols cascading down the screen like digital dominos.

Her white-lilac magic flickered faintly in the afterglow of whatever the hell just happened—a harmonic inversion so precise it had turned the city's Telluric Core Compressor inside-out rather than blowing it apart.

*Trust Bram's welding.*

Except nothing about this felt like trust. It felt like we'd duct-taped a wound while cancer ate the rest of the body.

"Help me," I grunted, bracing against a chunk of foundation. Together, we clawed our way out of the wreckage, emerging into rain that fell sideways, thick as motor oil. The downpour turned the entire industrial park into a black mirror reflecting nothing but our own ghostly shapes.

My phone chirped. That harsh synthetic *BEEP-BEEP-BEEP* that meant emergency protocols.

Elena's code. Three beeps: *medical*, *structural breach*, *containment failure*.

"The clinic." My voice came out wrong—stripped bare, no machinist's calm to hide behind. "That pulse hit the South Side."

Mara was already moving, pulling herself up onto the hood of a crushed BATC sedan. Her eyes scanned the chaos of overturned vehicles and collapsed scaffolding. "There. 1999 Crown Vic. Interceptor model. Keys are probably still in the ignition."

We ran.

The Crown Vic sat tilted on its passenger side, engine ticking as it cooled. I yanked open the driver's door—miracle of miracles, the keys dangled from the ignition. Mara threw herself into the passenger seat as I twisted the key.

The V8 roared to life.

"Buckle up," I said, though she already had. I slammed the gearshift into reverse, spun us around with tires screaming, then punched the accelerator toward the exit gates. The windshield wipers engaged with a rhythmic *thwack-thwack*, clearing away the black sludge that wasn't quite water anymore.

"Cole." Mara's hand found my arm. "If that pulse overloaded the Telluric Core Compressor... if it breached the glass..."

"It didn't." The words sounded like prayer and threat combined. "Bram sealed it. We closed the gate."

*Trust the math.*

But the pager kept beeping.

I pushed the Crown Vic harder through Neon Hollow's rain-slicked streets. Abandoned cars lined both sides—their occupants either evacuated or turned to salt by whatever backlash had rippled through the city. The wipers beat their steady rhythm while I white-knuckled the steering wheel until my palms burned.

Through my peripheral vision, I caught Mara watching me. Her magic was working overtime now, sealing micro-fractures in my ribs without me asking. Healing what I couldn't afford to feel yet.

"You need to breathe," she said quietly.

"I'm breathing fine."

"No. You're holding everything inside. Your heart rate is 140 BPM. That's not survival mode—that's panic."

She was right. Every muscle in my body was locked tighter than a machinist's torque wrench. My jaw ached from clenching. The rain outside seemed to press against the windows like hands trying to drag us back into the abyss we'd just crawled out of.

The South Side Clinic came into view—a squat concrete building reinforced with arcane-iron plating. Through its front windows, I saw something that made my stomach drop.

Blue-black light. Erratic, violent flashes erupting from the basement level where Danny's Arcane Iron-Lung was housed.

"Faster," I snarled, stomping on the accelerator.

The Crown Vic lurched forward.

* * *

The basement door explodes inward under our combined weight.

Wood splinters blast across the concrete floor like buckshot. Mara's combat boots hit first—heavy, military-issue, followed by my work boots still caked with marsh mud from the Kearny Marshes. The impact sends the rusted metal frame swinging wide on damaged hinges that scream like something dying.

The scene inside stops me mid-stride.

The Arcane Iron-Lung dominates the space—a monstrous brass-and-glass contraption that shouldn't exist outside a fever dream. Eight feet tall, maybe ten, its primary chamber is a cylindrical tube of reinforced glass thick enough to withstand artillery fire, mounted vertically in an intricate framework of red-hot copper piping and whining mechanical joints. Steam hisses from a dozen relief valves. The smell hits me next: ozone burning so sharp it makes my eyes water, mixed with the acrid stench of heated brass and something sterile underneath—medical alcohol, probably.

Danny's inside that glass prison.

Or what used to be Danny. Now it's just a human-shaped silhouette surrounded by swirling black sludge that catches the emergency lighting and throws back reflections that look almost alive. Tubes snake into and out of him at various points—I don't want to know where they connect. An IBM ThinkPad perched precariously on a stack of crates runs diagnostic readouts, each line cascading into fatal red error codes faster than I can process them.

"Cole!"

My mother's voice cuts through the industrial cacophony. She's pressed against the far wall behind a massive figure I recognize immediately—Grimjaw, the Syndicate Orc squad leader, seven feet of muscle and tusks and bad intentions. His gray hide is matted with sweat under the harsh lights. He's using his own body as a shield between Mom and whatever hell is about to break loose in here.

*"Boss!"* Grimjaw's shout overpowers even the deafening whine of the Telluric Core Compressor—the machine's spinning past the redline, screaming metal-on-metal like a jet engine being torn apart from the inside. *"Pressure valves are blown! The whole rig is gonna pop!"*

I'm already moving before my brain fully processes what he's saying. My frost-cobalt magic flares up my arms—not the careful control I usually maintain, but raw panic-fueled power that makes my fingers ache and my vision blur at the edges. The air around me crystallizes into hanging shards of ice that tinkle like broken wind chimes.

The brass dials on the Iron-Lung's control panel are spinning wildly. I slam my freezing hands onto them, feeling the metal bite into my palms hard enough to draw blood. Ice spreads across the surface instantly, crawling along the pipes like some kind of mechanical frostbite. It won't stop anything—that's the problem with tech this advanced—but maybe it'll slow down the chain reaction enough.

*"Hold on, Danny,"* I mutter through gritted teeth, watching frost creep across the overheating components. The cold burns my skin where it touches the scalding hot machinery. *"Just hold on a little longer."*

The ThinkPad screen goes haywire—a cascade failure of every vital sign imaginable. Heart rate flatlining. Brain activity... zero. Oxygen saturation... negative values that shouldn't be possible.

Then the glass starts singing.

Not metaphorically. Actually *singing*—a high-pitched harmonic resonance as stress fractures spider-web across its surface in branching patterns of pure structural failure. Hairline cracks spread outward from a central point somewhere near where Danny's chest would be if he were standing instead of suspended in black iron sludge inside a machine designed by people who should've been locked away decades ago.

*CRACK.*

The sound echoes through the basement like a gunshot magnified a thousand times.

The primary cylinder of the Arcane Iron-Lung—eight feet of reinforced safety glass meant to contain things that kill anyone who breathes too close—explodes outward in a shower of razor-sharp shrapnel and pressurized fluid.

Time dilates. I see Mara diving sideways, his coat billowing. See Grimjaw throwing his arms up to protect Mom, his muscular frame tensing for impact. See the black sludge erupting from the shattered chamber in an expanding sphere of corrosive nightmare that catches the red emergency lighting and turns it into something that looks like blood.

My hands are still frozen to those brass dials.

And then everything moves at normal speed again, all at once, and there's no more time to think about consequences or second-guessing or any of the smart choices I should've made back when this whole mess started with a dead kid in the marshlands and my brother disappearing into worse than death itself.

* * *

I fall to my knees before I realize I'm falling.

The concrete is cold against my skin where my jeans have worn thin at the joints. My hands plant themselves on either side of me, palms flat, fingers splayed. Somewhere behind me, I hear Elena's voice crack—a sound like porcelain shattering—but I can't look away from what's standing in the ruins of that machine.

Danny.

My brother.

He's taller than I remember. Taller than he should be, because eight years have passed outside while he sat frozen in that hell-forged chrysalis. His hair is longer, hanging past his shoulders in dark tangles, and his skin has the pale quality of something kept too long out of natural light. But his chest—that goddamn chest—rises and falls with the simple, miraculous mechanics of breathing.

Actual breathing.

Not the wet, mechanical hiss of that fucking Iron-Lung. Not air forced through tubes and valves and pumps designed by people who thought human lives were just raw materials for their experiments. Just lungs. Just a boy taking oxygen into his body and releasing it again.

The sound fills my ears like cathedral bells.

His bare feet touch the floor—actual flesh against actual concrete—and I notice they're dirty, but alive. No more metal encasement. No more crystalline prison. He takes another breath, deeper this time, and coughs. It's a horrible sound, all phlegm and eight years of disuse, but it's *human*. He doubles forward, bracing himself against the edge of the broken machinery, and when he straightens up, he spits something onto the floor.

Black iron crystals.

They hit the ground with tiny *tink* sounds, rolling across the concrete in lazy, harmless arcs. Within seconds, they begin to crumble, turning to gray ash, dissolving like snowflakes in summer heat. The air around them shimmers once—just once—with residual magic before going completely still.

Dead.

Whatever made those things function inside Danny's lungs, whatever gave Apep his power over my brother, it's gone now. Exhausted. Spent. Finished.

"Cole...?" 

Danny's voice cuts through the silence.

It's hoarse. Raw. Completely lacking the metallic reverb that colored every word he spoke while trapped in the Iron-Lung's vocoder system. This is just his voice—young, uncertain, scared—but *his*. My throat closes around nothing as I try to answer, fail, try again.

"I'm here," I manage finally, my own voice cracking around words I never thought I'd say again. "I'm right here."

He looks around the chamber—really looks—for the first time since emerging from that nightmare machine. His eyes pass over Elena, over Mara standing beside me, over Grimjaw's massive frame still poised in a defensive crouch near the entrance. They don't linger on anything except faces, trying to find anchors in a world he hasn't seen in eight years.

Then they come back to me.

"Mom?"

Elena makes a sound I've never heard her make before—a broken thing between sob and laugh—and then she's moving, rushing forward past me, her arms outstretched. Danny meets her halfway, collapsing into her embrace like he's been falling toward this moment for all of eternity.

I should stand up. Should go to them. But my legs won't cooperate.

My frost-cobalt magic is receding now—not the dramatic explosion of power from earlier, but a slow, gentle withdrawal, like tide pulling away from shore. The cold leaves my veins, returns to wherever it lives inside me when I'm not actively channeling it. My fingers lose their crystalline hardness, becoming merely flesh and bone again. The Threefold Cord doctrine closes, each strand dissolving back into the whole until there's only... me. Just Cole Reyes. Just a man in a leather jacket who's covered head-to-toe in grease and soot and blood that isn't his own.

Mara kneels beside me, her white-lilac flame guttering low around us both—soft, warm, carrying that scent of cedar and home-fire smoke. Her hands find my shoulders, steadying me even though I'm already on the ground. She doesn't say anything; she doesn't need to. There are no words adequate for what we're witnessing anyway.

I look up at her—really look—and see tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face.

"They're gone," I whisper, closing the last threads of my doctrine completely. "The crystals. The Iron-Lung. All of it."

She nods, her thumb brushing across my jawline to catch one of my own tears before it can fall.

"The sliver..." Mara says softly, almost reverently, "it was enough."

And it was.

Not enough to bring back everyone we've lost over the years. Not enough to undo every wrong that's ever been committed against people who couldn't defend themselves. Not enough to fix all the broken things in this twisted city we call Neon Hollow. But it was enough to save Danny.

That has to be enough.

It *is* enough.

My brother is breathing on his own air for the first time in eight years, and he's alive, and he's with our mother, and nothing else matters except this moment right here.

Dawn light begins bleeding through the high, barred windows of the basement—the kind installed decades ago when this place served some other purpose, some other function now forgotten beneath layers of industrial neglect. It's pale gray at first, barely distinguishable from the darkness still clinging to corners and shadows, but growing brighter by seconds.

Morning sunlight.

After everything—the corruption, the betrayals, the bloodshed, the sacrifices—I get to see morning sunlight again while my family lives.

Mara pulls me closer as Elena and Danny hold each other tighter, both of them crying now, making sounds between laughter and grief because they don't know how else to process what just happened. Grimjaw stands guard at the entrance—still vigilant despite the obvious resolution, still protecting us even now—and I realize he's nodding slowly, approvingly.

He saw it too.

The sliver worked.

Justice, however small, came true.

And maybe—that's enough.

Maybe that's always been enough.

I close my eyes as dawn bleeds fully into the chamber around us, feeling Mara's arms wrap completely around me now, holding me upright when gravity tries to pull me down further. The last thing I register before exhaustion finally claims me is her whisper against my ear:

"We did good work today."

Yeah.

Yeah we did.

## CHAPTER 13: MORNING SUNLIGHT

I pushed through the clinic's automatic doors into the pre-dawn chill, my white-lilac magic still humming faintly across my fingertips. The rain had stopped sometime during the night—left everything slick and greasy under the dying sodium-vapor lamps that were just now beginning their automated shutdown sequence. Each one flickered out with a soft electronic whine, plunging another section of the South Side into natural light.

Morning. The first real morning in three days that didn't start with gunfire.

The streets were cluttered with the aftermath. Overturned trauma-ambulances, mostly 1980s Ford models that had seen better decades. Goblin splice-techs were pinned inside some of them, unconscious, still wearing their cracked tactical gear. I could hear the hiss-pop of cutting torches already—the Dwarven welders from Local 405 working methodically to free them without further injury.

Bram Kovacs was barking orders at his crew, his voice gravel-scraped but steady. He caught sight of me and spat a stream of tobacco juice onto the wet asphalt. "About time you showed your face out here, Commander."

"Some of us were busy keeping people alive," I shot back, but there was no heat in it.

He grunted, which for Bram passed as laughter, then returned to directing his blowtorches. The acrid smell of burning metal mixed with the wet stone scent of the street and something else—spent brass casings, still warm enough to tingle against my magic sense.

Further down, an unexpected scene: Grimjaw's Orcs huddled around a makeshift fire someone'd started in a dumpster, passing cigarettes between massive hands. And there, smoking with them, stood Vane Thorne. The BATC Inquisitor looked like he'd been dragged through hell and half of New Jersey—his bespoke suit was torn, bloodstained, one sleeve completely burned away. But he was there, sharing smoke with creatures he would've arrested on sight six months ago.

Thorne saw me approaching and straightened slightly, wincing. When he spoke, all corporate superiority had drained from his voice, leaving just exhaustion and something that might have been respect. "The Sterling-Voss board is in shambles, Vance. Morvain is gone." He took another drag, exhaled slowly. "The contracts are void."

I felt something unknot in my chest. Years of work—the alliance building, the political maneuvering, all the bodies we'd buried trying to keep this fragile peace—it hadn't been for nothing.

Nearby, a Gnome sat curled on a curb, her fingers moving across a battered Sony MiniDisc player. She was playing some victory tune I didn't recognize, exhausted but grinning like she'd won the lottery. Her cyberware was exposed, sparking occasionally as it short-circuited in the dampness. I let my magic drift over her—sealed the leaks, soothed the neural feedback headache she was probably getting.

"South Side belongs to the union again," Bram muttered, watching an ambulance being righted by three Orcs working in perfect coordination.

"Not just your union, Bram." I smiled—actually smiled, felt my face muscles move in ways they hadn't since before the Breach. "Belongs to everyone who bled for it tonight."

He considered that, then spat again. "Fair enough, Commander."

The sodium-vapor lamp above us flickered one last time before dying completely. Real sunlight was breaking through the industrial haze now, painting everything in shades of grey and gold. It looked wrong somehow—too beautiful after what we'd survived. But also necessary. The world needed to remember what daylight looked like.

Then came the sound: heavy engines, multiple vehicles approaching from the Pulaski Skyway entrance. Reinforced BATC transport trucks, their armor scarred but intact, pulling into our intersection. And leading them...

Lysandra Vance stepped down from the lead vehicle, her tactical gear pristine despite everything, her staff crackling with residual energy even at rest.

The rear doors of the transports were beginning to unlock with a series of hydraulic hisses.

* * *

The transport doors hiss open like a wound sealing shut in reverse.

Morning sunlight floods in—not the sickly amber of the Pylon-dreams, but honest gold that makes my eyes water. I've been staring at gray concrete for so long that natural light feels violent, invasive. Beautiful.

Lysandra steps out first, and she looks *destroyed*. Not physically—her armor is still intact, that ridiculous blue cape somehow unscathed—but there's something hollow behind her eyes now. Something permanent. She nods at me. One sharp nod. That's all we need.

Behind her, they come.

Thirty-eight children stumble into dawn like prisoners released from a tomb they'd forgotten existed. Some are clutching blankets, others holding hands with fingers twisted by malnourishment. Their clothes hang on them like costumes on coat hangers—they've grown during their imprisonment while their wardrobes stayed small, frozen. Bright reds against gray skin. Yellow polka dots on sallow human faces. The absurdity of children's patterns against the backdrop of South Side devastation makes my chest tight.

Elena Reyes emerges from the clinic like she owns it, which considering everything she's done to keep this place running, maybe she does. She's got stacks of styrofoam cups balanced in her arms, steam rising from what must be coffee so hot it'll blister tongues. Doesn't matter. The kids need heat more than sense right now.

"Form up!" Elena barks, but there's warmth underneath the rough edges. "Eat first. Then we get you processed."

The children spread out across cracked pavement, some sitting, some standing, all of them staring upward at sky they'd been denied. An Elven boy can't be more than six, his ears crooked from malnutrition, tilts his head back until I'm afraid he'll fall over just watching clouds drift past. A Human girl around ten has tears streaming down her face, but she's smiling too—mouth open, catching sunlight like she's tasting it.

Then one of them walks toward me.

Ogre-kin. Female. Can't be more than eight years old, but already showing the heavy bone structure that'll make her imposing as an adult if she survives long enough. Her eyes are wrong-colored—one brown, one milky white—and her skin has that sickly green undertone common to her kind when stressed. But she's *walking* toward me with purpose, no hesitation despite the crowd, and I realize every muscle in my body is locked tight.

"I know you," she says.

Her voice hits like a punch to the solar plexus. Heavy. Guttural. South Side accent thick as tar.

"You're the glowing lady."

* * *

The concrete beneath my boots feels different this morning. Cleaner somehow, though I know that can't be right. Neon Hollow doesn't get clean—it just gets less filthy between catastrophes.

I stand in the center of the intersection where everything ended. Where Apep's psychic rot had seeped deepest into the fabric of this broken city. My hands hang at my sides, palms open. No staff. No anchor stone. Just me and what I've become after carrying too much weight for too long.

The fused anchor burns in my chest—not painfully now, but like a second sun. Warm. Mine.

I close my eyes and breathe in the cold December air. It tastes wrong. Too fresh. There should still be ash on my tongue from the fires that burned through Pulaski Skyway last month. Should be the metallic tang of blood and ozone from when the Crown Vault cracked open and bled its secrets across Kearny Marshes.

But there's nothing.

I drop to one knee on the cold asphalt and press my palm flat against the ground. Concrete. Just concrete. Not the psychic scar tissue of a million forgotten horrors. Not the residue of despair that made people tear their own skin off trying to escape their heads.

The anchor pulse once, twice, three times. A question I don't need words for.

*Yes,* something answers back. *You can do this.*

Of course I can. What choice is there?

I think back to the spell I used to burn away the Last Court's heart—a spell of pure destructive force, forged from desperation and rage and the kind of hate that eats you hollow if you're not careful. That magic was fire. Was teeth. Was the scream you make when someone takes everything you love just to watch you bleed out on broken glass.

This needs to be different.

My other hand finds purchase beside the first. Both palms down. Grounding myself in dirt and rust and all the ugly reality of where I am. This isn't some ethereal plane or sanctified temple. This is Neon Hollow. South Side. Where the people who couldn't afford anywhere better came to die slow deaths between factory shifts and bottle bottoms.

They deserve better than to have their graves violated by cosmic horror.

I reach deep into the fused anchor—not just my will anymore, but Cole's too. Lysandra's. Even that piece of Danny I managed to pull back from whatever void had been trying to keep him. All of us together, making this work.

Hearth-Coal Warmth. White-Lilac Healing.

It should be impossible. You don't scrub away years of accumulated misery with comfort spells. That's not how trauma works—you don't just wash it off like motor oil under a car's chassis.

But as the magic begins to gather in my core, I understand why it has to be this way. Because destruction leaves emptiness, and emptiness gets filled again by whatever darkness happens to be passing through. Warding keeps things out but locks the rot already inside.

Healing? Healing means changing what's there at the cellular level. Means replacing poison with blood cells that remember sunlight instead of abyss.

The spell rises up through me like warm breath. I stand slowly, arms still spread, and let it flow outward.

For a heartbeat, nothing happens.

Then—

Cedar. God, so much cedar, sweet and sharp and clean-burning. It fills my lungs until I'm coughing, laughing, crying all at once because when was the last time anything in this city smelled like living wood instead of burning plastic and chemical runoff?

The wave expands outward from where I stand. Silent. Visceral. A pressure against my skin that isn't quite touch, pushing through brick and mortar and the souls of people sleeping three blocks over who'll wake up wondering if they died and went somewhere better without realizing they're still in bed.

I feel it working into the pavement beneath me, dissolving the psychic stains like acid on rust. Feel it seeping into walls where families huddled during air raids or just trying to survive one more night between their landlord's eviction notices. Feel it washing through minds tangled in addiction or grief or simple exhaustion from being poor in a world that doesn't care whether you make rent or eat.

Not destroying. Not protecting.

*Healing.*

The smog lifts. Not gone—you don't fix decades of industrial abuse in ten minutes—but parted enough for morning light to hit the buildings and turn them gold instead of gray. The sky beyond is blue. Blue. I'd forgotten what real blue looked like past the perpetual haze that sat over everything like a sick blanket.

My knees buckle but Cole's there before I fall, his hand under my elbow steady as steel. "Easy, Commander," he says, voice soft. "You're allowed to stop now."

I look up at him—really *look*—and see how tired he is. How much weight he's been carrying himself while I was off playing savior for an entire borough. "Is it done?"

"Yeah." He helps me sit on the curb, keeping one hand on my shoulder like I might float away. "It's done. You did it."

Lysandra materializes out of nowhere with a cigarette already lit, exhaling smoke that smells wrong against all this cedar-heaven. She leans against the clinic doorframe, watching me with something approaching approval. "Christ, Vance. That was actually beautiful. Makes me want to puke."

"That means you liked it," Cole tells her without looking away from me.

"I hate you both." But she's smiling. Barely.

## CHAPTER 14: THE BLOOD-IRON RING

The December wind cuts through the open bay doors like a blade, carrying snow that settles on everything inside my shop—the workbenches, the Crown Vic's glossy black hood, the scorch marks I still haven't sanded off the concrete floor. I don't bother closing them. Let the cold in. Let the world breathe here.

*tink-tink-tink*

My jeweler's hammer strikes the anvil with surgical precision. Each tap is measured, deliberate. The iron rod beneath the blows glows faint orange at its center, just enough heat to work without burning. No frost-cobalt tonight—not much anyway. Just enough to whisper along the grain boundaries, sealing the metal against time itself.

Penance served. Revenge crystallized in the deep with Elias and Malvourne, their screams trapped forever in Pylon #5's crown vault. And Danny—my boy Danny—breathing easy upstairs, lungs clean as spring water after the Surgeon's poison worked its way clear.

Three cords. One ledger.

*tink-tink-tink*

"Hey, boss!" Grimjaw's voice booms from the loft. "The kid says a Goblin splice-deck can out-process a Gnome rig! Tell him he's fragging delusional!"

I don't look up. Keep working. The glow pulses in rhythm with my hammer.

"Tell him to check the busbar latency, Grim," I call back. "Then take his money."

Laughter from upstairs. Danny's higher-pitched giggle mixing with the bigger man's rumble. Normal sounds. Mundane arguments about nothing important. It’s exactly what I fought for when I went down into that dark, when I stood against the Breach and came back changed.

The iron begins to shape. I'm not making a weapon. Not runic armor or blades or anything that kills. Just a simple ring band, maybe eight millimeters wide. Unadorned. Clean lines.

*clang-clang-clang*

The Toughbook on the bench plays some acoustic rock track—I don't know who it is, just found it in a folder labeled "Safe Listening." No screaming, no distortion, no rage. The song's about cold winters and warm fires, something like that. It catches in the silence between hammer strikes.

I heat the rod again, just slightly, watching the color shift through the spectrum. Blue. Yellow. Orange. Red.

Mara Vance is coming. I hear her footsteps before she appears—confident stride, heels clicking purposefully across pavement now concrete. She doesn't knock anymore. Just walks in like she owns half this place.

"Cole." Her voice carries authority wrapped in velvet. "We need to talk about the Pulaski Skyway contracts."

I set down the hammer. Wipe black flux from my hands with a rag that smells like cutting oil and yesterday's work.

"Talk," I say without turning around first. Then I do, meeting those sharp eyes of hers as snow swirls past the bay doors behind her, painting everything white.

She looks different somehow. Softer around the edges maybe. Or maybe I'm just seeing clearer these days.

What do you want to know?

* * *

The shop door groans open, letting in a blade of winter light that cuts across the concrete floor. Snow dusts off Mara's leather jacket as she steps inside, shaking crystals from her hair like some kind of urban snow fairy. She kicks the door shut behind her—thump—and suddenly the only sounds are the hiss of the forge and the drip-drip of melting slush on the floorboards.

"Smells like you've been cooking up trouble again," she says, her voice warm but carrying that edge of concern it always does when she finds me hunched over the anvil after dark.

I don't look up immediately. Can't. The piece I'm working on needs this moment—this perfect hammer-strike timing that disappears if you break concentration for even a heartbeat. The ring sits on its bed of charcoal, glowing red-orange in the low light, and I bring the hammer down one more time. *CLANG.*

Steam rises. The metal cools with that particular whistle it makes when it's satisfied with its shape.

Only then do I straighten, rolling my shoulders against the ache, turning to face her properly. She's leaning against the workbench now, arms crossed, that knowing half-smile playing at her lips. The shop's radiant heat catches in her eyes—the white-lilac glow she can't quite control, mixing with the firelight until everything feels warmer than it should be in January.

"You know," she starts, pulling out her Motorola Razr and setting it on the bench where it immediately begins buzzing, "Vane Thorne called."

The name makes my jaw tighten involuntarily. Vane Thorne. High-Spire. Everything about that world I walked away from.

"What'd he want?" I ask, keeping my tone neutral as I wipe my hands on a rag that's seen better days.

Mara grins wider. "Offered me a consultant gig. High-Spire corner office." She pauses, watching my reaction with those calculating eyes that miss nothing. "Told him my rates tripled and I only work South Side hours."

I turn fully now, letting the hammer rest against my hip. "You tell him to slot it?"

"In so many words." Her smile turns sharp, predatory. "He sighed so hard I think his pointy ears drooped."

A laugh escapes me before I can stop it—genuine, unexpected. I pull her close by the waist, forehead meeting hers, breathing in cedar and ozone and whatever magic keeps her warm even when winter tries to claim everything.

"Good," I say simply.

We stand there for a moment, just existing together in this space that's ours and only ours. No Geas collars. No corporate hitmen. No looming apocalypses waiting to swallow the city whole. Just the shop, the heat, and each other.

"It's weird," Mara says eventually, pulling back enough to look at me properly. The lilac glow intensifies slightly, casting dancing shadows across the walls covered in blueprints and schematics. "The rebuilding efforts are actually... working? People are showing up. Volunteers. Not just meta-humans either. Regular folk too. Like they remember what community feels like."

"The BATC trials helping?"

She nods slowly, absently reaching for one of Danny's half-finished circuit boards scattered across the bench. "And Danny's lost in Gnome hardware hacking again. Found some kind of pre-Collapse server farm under Secaucus. He won't shut up about 'legacy protocols' or whatever nerd-speak he's picked up from watching those old tech manuals."

I chuckle, imagining our kid bent over glowing motherboards late into the night, muttering code like prayer. "He get that from you or me?"

"That's an offensive question." But she's smiling as she says it, fingers tracing idle patterns on my oil-stained shirt. "Though honestly? Probably both. We're terrible influences."

The forge pops, sending a shower of sparks spiraling upward where they die against the dark ceiling beams.

My hand moves to my jacket pocket without conscious thought—has been doing that all evening—and I feel the weight of the ring inside. The piece I've been working on through two nights and most of today. The metal that cost me more than it should have, forged with intention every bit as careful as any spell.

Mara notices immediately. She always does.

"Cole?" Her voice shifts register, going soft in that way that means she sees something on my face I didn't mean to show. "What is it?"

My throat goes dry suddenly. The shop feels smaller, the heat more intense, my heart hammering against ribs that still ache from last week's fight at the Pulaski Skyway.

"I need to tell you something," I start, pulling away just enough to reach into that pocket.

* * *

The ring sits on my palm like a piece of captured midnight—gunmetal gray, unremarkable to anyone who doesn't know better. But I know. Every molecule of this thing has been forged in blood-iron and frost-cobalt, two elements that should hate each other but somehow don't when they're held together by intention and heat.

My hands aren't shaking. Not much, anyway.

Outside the shop windows, snow falls in heavy sheets across Neon Hollow, turning the industrial wasteland into something almost beautiful. Almost. The Kearny Marshes are buried under white now, all those twisted things lurking beneath the surface tucked away for winter. Doesn't mean they've stopped moving.

Mara stands three feet away, wrapped in her leather jacket, arms crossed tight over her chest. Her eyes—that impossible white-lilac color—haven't left mine since I pulled the ring box from my coat pocket five minutes ago.

"You gonna stand there gawking all night, Reyes?" Her voice cuts through the workshop silence, sharp as one of her blades. But there's something underneath it. Something soft.

I close my fingers around the ring, feel its weight settle against my skin. This isn't Newark romance bullshit. No diamond rings, no down-on-one-knee speeches about forever. That's not us. Never was.

"Got something to say," I tell her, voice rougher than I meant. "Been thinking about it since the Crown Vault."

"Oh you have." She steps closer, boots crunching on salt-strewn concrete. "Do tell."

The ring opens between my fingers like a mechanical flower. Blood-iron catches the overhead fluorescents, throws back dull red. Frost-cobalt veins run through it like frozen lightning. And woven through both—absorbing her warmth without burning, reflecting her light without shattering—I can see traces of that white-lilac glow she doesn't even realize she's projecting.

"I'm done being just mine," I say. "That sliver of justice I carry around? The thing that made me go after Pylon #5, made me crawl through the Abyss looking for vengeance?" My throat tightens. "It belongs to us now. Not the past. Our future."

Her breath catches. Just barely, but I hear it.

"This is it, Vance. No take-backs." I hold up the ring. "You're stuck with me. South Side mechanic who can't let things go. Guy who'll probably still wake up in cold sweats from Pulaski Skyway nights. You getting into this knowing exactly what you're signing up for."

A tear slips down her cheek—just one, tracking silver in the harsh shop lighting. Her magic flares behind it, brilliant white-lilac cascading through the air like aurora borealis trapped indoors.

"You son of a bitch," she whispers.

Then she's moving, closing the distance between us in three strides. Her left hand extends, palm up, fingers trembling just slightly. The other hand grabs my collar, yanking me down as she rises on her toes.

"Cole Reyes." My name on her tongue sounds like a vow and a curse intertwined. "If you think you can weld yourself into my life and then fuck it up later, you've got another thing coming."

I slide the ring onto her finger. It fits perfectly—not because I measured or guessed, but because some things just know where they belong. The blood-iron feels right against her skin. The frost-cobalt pulses once, twice, settling into place beside that constant white-lilac radiance.

But I'm not done.

Reaching for my workbench without letting go of her gaze, I grab the micro-arc welder—the one I use for precision joints when standard welding would crack the metal. The kind of tool that makes beautiful seams invisible to the naked eye.

"This part's permanent," I tell her, voice thick now, raw. "No taking it off. No losing it. No mistakes."

She nods, watching as I ignite the torch. A sharp *bzzzt* fills the workshop, ozone smell hitting immediately—that clean electricity scent mixed with burnt flux from the rod. I guide the tiny spark toward the seam where ring meets knuckle, feeling magic thread through my fingertips alongside the electrical current.

The fusion is instantaneous. White-hot blue light erupts at the point of contact, merging with her natural glow until both colors swirl together—frost-cobalt and white-lilac creating something new entirely before settling back down into dull gunmetal gray. The seam disappears completely, leaving only smooth metal that will never separate.

"Welded," I breathe out.

Mara crashes into me, lips finding mine hard and desperate and real. Her magic floods through the connection like a promise made manifest, white-lilac warmth spreading across my chest where she presses against me. I taste salt and sweat and something sweet underneath it all.

"No take-backs," she murmurs against my mouth.

"I wouldn't have it any other way, Vance."

## CHAPTER 15: OPTION C EPILOGUE — THE SHEARED FANG & THE CRACKED SISTER SEALS

The elevator descends into the abyss beneath Pylon #5.

I stand at the center of the steel car, flanked by my BATC security detail—two grizzled Aegis Commanders whose names I've already forgotten. They don't matter. What matters is what waits below us in Vault 74. The thing that's been eating itself out of its tomb for six months straight.

The elevator lurches. We're thirty feet down now, maybe forty. Through the reinforced mesh floor panels, I can see the sodium-vapor lights receding above us like dying stars. The air grows cold. Not just cold—the kind of cold that seepes into your bones and makes you remember what it felt like to be small and afraid in the dark.

When the doors finally slide open, it's worse than I imagined.

Vault 74 should look like every other containment facility I've visited in my twelve years as Commander. Sterile. Controlled. Military precision incarnate. Instead, this place looks like something tried to claw its way out of a nightmare and succeeded halfway.

The stasis field surrounding the fang is failing. I can see it in the way the air shimmers around the massive obsidian tooth—the way the light bends wrong, fracturing across angles that shouldn't exist in three-dimensional space. Thirty feet long, eight feet at its widest point, still dripping with blue-black venom that hisses where it touches the reinforced concrete floor.

"Commander Vance." The High-Spire Elf guarding the eastern catwalk has perfect posture, perfect uniform, perfectly manicured nails holding an MP5 with rune-etched magazines. His name tag reads Captain Lyr'vannil. He sounds like he's reading from a script. "All personnel accounted for. Containment intact."

"Your thermometer's lying to you," I say flatly.

He doesn't respond. Doesn't even blink. But I notice his fingers tightening on the weapon.

Down below, past the safety rails, I watch a Dwarf engineer—his Local 405 patch visible even from here—tap frantically at his PalmPilot. The screen glitches. Again. A third time before he curses in Khuzdul, loud enough that I catch it even over the humming of the failing stasis generators.

"The grid's failing!" he shouts up at us, his voice cracking with panic. "The venom is highly conductive! It's shorting the busbars!"

Captain Lyr'vannil finally moves. His MP5 comes up to his shoulder with practiced ease. "Containment team, we have a breach! I repeat, the artifact is—"

The lights die.

Not fade. Not flicker. Just *die*, all at once, like someone flipped a switch on reality itself.

For one heartbeat, there's nothing but darkness and the sound of our breathing echoing off concrete walls that suddenly feel far too close. Then tactical flashlights snap on around me, their beams cutting through the blackness like surgical instruments probing for weakness.

In the strobing light, I see it.

The fang isn't just sitting there anymore. It's *pulsing*. Black ichor oozes from cracks in its surface—not the slow drip of dead matter, but an active, purposeful flow, like oil pumping from a deep well. And beneath that sound, if you listen closely enough, there's something else.

Hissing.

Breathing.

*Thinking*.

"Oh fuck," whispers one of my detail. "Commander, it's alive."

"No," I say quietly, watching as more of those sickening veins spread across the vault floor. "It was always alive. We just weren't ready to admit what we'd locked down here."

The Dwarf engineer screams as the telluric drain beside him sizzles and goes critical. The concrete beneath it crumbles away, revealing a deep pit filled with corrosive residue. He stumbles backward, boots scraping against wet stone—

—and then the fang *cracks*.

Not a hairline fracture. A violent, catastrophic explosion that sends obsidian shards screeching through the air like bullets. The stasis field collapses entirely, releasing a geyser of that blue-black venom directly upward.

Captain Lyr'vannil fires his MP5 into the mass without hesitation. Rune-bullets punch through the viscous fluid, each impact creating a shower of sparks—but underneath, something worse is happening.

The venom isn't just splattering. It's *clinging*. Coalescing. Forming shapes.

A hand emerges first—a grotesque limb made of solidified ichor and jagged tooth fragments. Then another. Shoulders that shift between serpentine and humanoid forms before settling on neither. The thing rising from the fang's broken carcass defies description, its body constantly reforming in ways that hurt to watch.

"Contact!" someone shouts. But we're already running. Already scrambling for the elevator as behind us, that Splinter Avatar of Apep—the fragment of his consciousness we thought was dead—unfurls itself fully in the darkness of Vault 74.

Its roar shakes loose chunks of ceiling concrete.

And I realize with cold certainty: this isn't containment anymore.

This is ground zero.

* * *

I hit the ground hard as the first salvo of bullets ripped through the air where my head had been moments before. Concrete dust exploded around me, peppering my tactical suit with microscopic fragments that felt like needles against my skin.

"CONTACT!" someone screamed.

The Splinter Avatar moved like lightning made solid—if lightning could dissolve steel beams and shatter bones with a single touch. I'd seen its larger cousin once, back in 2008 when we thought we'd won. This was different. Smaller, yes, but faster. Hungrier. Its body rippled with an oily sheen that caught the emergency lights, casting sickly shadows across the vault floor.

Three BATC guards opened up simultaneously, their MP5s spitting silver-tipped rounds etched with preservation runes. Elite equipment for elite problems. But this wasn't just any problem.

The bullets passed through the Avatar like it wasn't there at all.

Then it struck.

The nearest guard—the only human in our detail—had time for one scream before the creature's segmented tail whipped around, catching him across the midsection. His torso simply... separated. No blood. No mess beyond the thermal burn marks left on his uniform where the venom had touched him. He collapsed into two pieces, each half still twitching independently.

"Fall back to—"

The second guard's words dissolved into wet choking sounds as the Avatar's jaws unhinged impossibly wide, swallowing him whole. His body bulged down the serpentine throat, limbs flailing against something that couldn't be pierced, couldn't be broken.

I rolled behind a support column, drawing my sidearm—a .45 ACP loaded with blessed ammo that cost more per round than most people's monthly rent. The weight felt meaningless in my hands.

"Vance! Move!" That was Korin, one of the Elven operatives. She fired controlled bursts from her position near the blast doors, each shot designed to herd rather than kill. Her tactical training showed, even as panic crept into her voice.

The Avatar didn't care about her tactics. It lunged.

She dove sideways, and I saw its head crash against the reinforced steel door we'd sealed just minutes ago. The impact should have dented titanium. Should have cracked reinforced concrete. Instead, the door peeled open like aluminum foil, twisted metal shrieking in protest before shearing completely off its hinges.

Through the opening, I glimpsed the dark throat of the maintenance tunnels—a place even BATC hadn't fully mapped. The Great Serpent's domain.

"No!" Korin scrambled forward, throwing some kind of glowing talisman that exploded in violet light on contact with the Avatar's hide. For a heartbeat, it seemed to slow. To solidify. But then the creature absorbed the magic itself, its body growing denser, more opaque. More real.

It turned toward us both, eyes—if they could be called eyes—burning with cold blue flame that reflected off every surface, multiplying until the entire vault seemed filled with hungry gazes.

Korin kept firing. The MP5's report echoed deafeningly in the enclosed space, spent casings clattering across the floor where they immediately began dissolving in pools of black venom that seeped from wherever the creature touched down.

"Radio!" I shouted. "Tell them it's loose!"

But she was already there, her hands shaking as she lifted the comm unit to her lips. "It's loose! The Fang is alive! It's heading for the aqueducts! For God's sake, seal the—"

The Avatar struck faster than thought. Its jaws snapped shut around Korin's upper body, cutting off her transmission mid-sentence. The radio hit the ground, static hissing from its speaker as teeth crunched through bone and flesh with wet, organic sounds that would haunt my nightmares forever.

I fired six rounds into its skull.

All six passed harmlessly through, leaving only temporary dimples in its translucent form before the wounds sealed behind me like ripples in water.

The thing looked at me directly then—a predatory intelligence older than human civilization given terrible new form—and I saw something in those burning eyes that made my blood run cold. Hunger. Not just for flesh or energy or even arcane power.

It wanted the world itself.

When it lunged this time, I didn't try to shoot. Didn't try to fight. Just ran.

My boots slammed against concrete as I sprinted toward the emergency stairwell, heard the Avatar's body scraping along walls—the sound of glass being rubbed against metal multiplied by a thousand. Behind me came the wet thump-thump of something massive moving with unnatural speed.

I burst through the stairwell door just as the Avatar crashed into it, the reinforced steel buckling inward but holding long enough for me to slam the door shut behind me. My hand found the emergency lock—an arcane sealing rune we'd installed specifically for containment situations—

And traced it with shaking fingers.

Light blazed blue-white hot across every surface. The air crackled with binding force. For one precious moment, I thought maybe, just maybe—

The door exploded outward in a shower of molten metal fragments.

The Splinter Avatar poured through the gap, its smaller size letting it squeeze through spaces it shouldn't have been able to navigate. It hit the stairs and kept coming, unstoppable momentum carrying it down into darkness even as I fled upward toward ground level.

"Vance!" That was someone on comms. "Status report! What's your status?"

My lungs burned. Every breath tasted like copper and ozone and ancient decay—the smell of whatever dimension had spawned this thing now bleeding through into ours.

"Splinter manifest," I gasped between strides. "Full containment breach. It's in the sub-level tunnels. Locking down everything from—"

A deafening roar cut me off—not from below where the Avatar had gone, but from somewhere deeper still. Like stone screaming. Like reality tearing at seams that should never have been opened.

Then silence.

And in that silence, the faintest sound: a rhythmic pulsing that seemed to resonate in my bones themselves. Blue-black residue marked where the Avatar had touched surfaces, and within those stains, something glowed with steady cadence.

Heartbeat frequency.

Transmission signal.

I keyed my radio again, voice raw with exhaustion and terror. "It's not just here. Whoever gets this signal—I don't care what department or command structure—they need to know."

Pause.

"The Great Serpent is broadcasting."

* * *

The Mothers' Bunker smells like burnt electronics and stale energy drinks. Rows upon rows of black server racks hum their monotonous song while cooling fans work overtime against the heat. My fingers dance across the mechanical keyboard—old-school Cherry switches that click-clack with each keystroke. Real hardware for real frequencies.

"Oi, watch this shit," I mutter to myself, pulling up the raw seismograph data from Pylon #5's aftermath. Three months since Commander Vance unleashed that Splinter Avatar, and we thought we'd contained the fallout. Thought wrong.

The screens flicker.

Every Panasonic Toughbook in my station—six of them running custom-leased software through Panasonic's enterprise division—glitches simultaneously. The green phosphor text scrambles into digital snow. My heart drops.

Then the frequency hits.

Not an earthquake. Never an earthquake in these readings. This is different. Pure arcane resonance, traveling along the leylines like lightning through copper wire. The seismographs spike upward in perfect synchronization, all six monitors showing the same impossible pattern: blood-red spikes erupting across the graphs like a cardiac arrest monitor in a horror movie.

"Yo!" I scream across the bunker. "WAKE UP THE SWITCHBOARD! WE GOT MASSIVE TELLURIC FEEDBACK!"

My colleague, Shortstack—real name's Marcus, but who uses those anymore?—slides over in his rolling chair so fast the wheels squeal. His oversized glasses reflect the harsh red glow from the screens as he leans in close. "Where? Neon Hollow?"

I shake my head, my hand trembling as I point at the readings with my index finger. "No. You're not seeing it." I cycle through the satellite overlays, cross-referencing the seismic data with known leyline intersections. "The shockwave from November... it didn't just bounce. It traveled."

"To where?" Shortstack asks, his voice dropping to something between a whisper and a prayer.

"Everywhere."

I pull up two separate satellite maps side by side. Cairo first—the Nile Duat, the ancient Egyptian underworld. The sensors embedded in the Sister Obelisk-Seals there show hairline fractures spreading across their surfaces like spiderwebs on glass. Then the second map: Xibalba, deep beneath the Yucatan Peninsula. Same thing. Identical cracks radiating outward from Maya seals that haven't seen the light of day—or any other kind of light—in three thousand years.

"The seals," I breathe, feeling ice form in my stomach despite the bunker's oppressive heat. "They're cracking."

A Nokia brick phone—I keep one for emergencies because cell signals are garbage this deep underground—begins ringing on my desk. The old-fashioned mechanical bell rings insistently, shattering the stunned silence. Nobody moves to answer it.

Because we all see what's coming next.

The screens flash. All six monitors go white for a microsecond, then display the same message in stark black letters:

**GLOBAL BREACH IMMINENT**

"That wasn't just Apep," Shortstack whispers. He's right. That cage in Neon Hollow? Just the appetizer. Commander Vance didn't open one door. She kicked down every last goddamn gate at once.

The ancient gods of the underworld are waking up. And not just here. Across the entire fucking planet.

My hands shake as I reach for the phone. One ring. Two rings. Three.

Somewhere in Neon Hollow, Mara Vance is probably still thinking she won.

She doesn't know yet that her victory was just the beginning.
