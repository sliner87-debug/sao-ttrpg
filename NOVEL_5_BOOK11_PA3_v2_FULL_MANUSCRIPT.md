## CHAPTER 1: CHAPTER 1

The air down here tastes like burning copper and diesel fuel. I'm crouched in the access duct three levels up, pressed so tight against the rust-coated pipes that I can feel every vibration through my ribs. Through the perforated metal grating above me, I catch flashes of movement below—shadows moving through sodium vapor light that makes everything look diseased.

"Commander Vance." The whisper comes from my left, barely audible over the hum of transformers. Special Agent Torres. His breath fogs the cold metal inches from my face. "They're running another check on the main conduits. Looks like they're prepping for transport."

I don't move. Don't even blink. Below, the black-ops crew is working fast now, their movements efficient and brutal. The clank of chains echoes off concrete walls slick with iron-gall residue—some kind of industrial byproduct that drips in thick, sizzling streams when it hits the floor. It smells sharp and acrid, cutting through the ozone stench of raw magic.

Through gaps in the grating, I see him. Marcus Voss. Trench coat hanging open despite the heat radiating from the nearby busbars. He's got a clove cigarette wedged between his lips, the cherry glowing orange-red in the half-light as he leans over what looks like four massive tanks. Lead-lined. Heavy enough that it takes three Razor-Wire Orcs to haul them into position with industrial chains.

"They're using Liquid Isfet," Torres whispers again. "Unstable batch. Those valves won't hold past maximum pressure."

I know. I can taste it from here—that particular wrongness in the air that means someone's weaponizing pure corruption. The Isfet sloshes inside its containment vessels, dark and viscous as motor oil mixed with nightmare fuel.

Voss straightens up, brushing ash from his lapel. His voice carries down through the ductwork, flat and matter-of-fact: "Pressure readings?"

One of the Orcs—a scarred brute with tusks filed to points—consults a gauge mounted to one of the tanks. "Holding at eighty percent, Mr. Voss. Gauges are rigged to spike past critical threshold on remote trigger."

"Good." Voss pulls the cigarette from his mouth, studying the glowing tip like he's reading tea leaves. "How long until commuter traffic resumes?"

"Ten minutes," another Orc replies. This one's the foreman by the look of his faded tattoos. South Side accent thick enough to spread on bread: "Valves are stripped, boss. This shit blows, it ain't leaving ash, it's leaving shadows."

Voss doesn't even glance at him. Just flicks cigarette ash onto concrete that's already stained black with burnt residue. "Shadows work too."

The foreman grunts acknowledgment and barks orders to his crew in gutter-Orcish. Chains rattle. Metal groans under impossible weight as they position the final tank directly beneath ventilation grates that feed fresh air to the lower platform.

That's when I see him.

Elias Morvain.

He descends from a catwalk above, immaculate in a bespoke suit that somehow remains pristine despite the grime and filth coating everything else in these tunnels. The fabric is dark charcoal, tailored to razor-sharp precision. His shoes—hand-stitched Italian leather—don't make a sound on concrete slick with iron-gall residue.

Corporate. High-Spire. The kind of elf who calculates collateral damage with spreadsheet precision while never getting his hands dirty himself.

Morvain stops beside Voss, adjusting his cufflinks with methodical care. His voice carries down crisp and clear, each word articulated with boardroom coldness:

"The blast radius needs recalibration. Current parameters will compromise the upper pylons by point-zero-three percent. Unacceptable structural failure probability."

Voss shrugs. "We'll adjust. Target's priority one anyway."

"I'm not concerned with priorities." Morvain's gaze sweeps across the assembled tanks, calculating trajectories like a chess master planning fifteen moves ahead. "I'm concerned with asset preservation. Leave the pylons intact. Incinerate everything below deck level."

The Orc foreman spits into the shadows. "That's gonna take more punch than these babies can manage, boss-man. Need double the payload to guarantee platform-level incineration without touching the supports."

Morvain finally looks at him, expression unreadable behind eyes that reflect sodium light like polished glass. "Then acquire additional resources. This facility operates on a contingency budget. Adapt accordingly."

He turns away before anyone can respond, already climbing back toward the catwalk, leaving Voss to oversee the final preparations alone.

Below, the crew shifts into high gear. Industrial wrenches clanging against metal. The sharp hiss of pressure valves being manually adjusted. Voss circles the rigging setup like a shark, checking connections with practiced efficiency.

Then he pulls out a phone—heavily modified Motorola Razr, all brushed steel and red LED indicators. Flips it open with one hand while tightening a valve with the other.

"Payload is primed," he says into the device. Pause. Listening. Then: "Just waiting on the target to buy his ticket."

The line goes dead.

Torres's grip tightens on my shoulder—barely perceptible through body armor—but I feel it. Feel the weight of what just happened settling over us both.

Someone's coming down to this platform.

And four tons of Liquid Isfet are waiting to turn them into shadows.

* * *

The Toughbook's screen glows that sickly green, washing out Morvain's face as he leans closer. Static crackles through the stolen audio feed—a river of white noise punctuated by the mechanical *thump-thump* of turnstiles somewhere in the depths below. Newark Penn Station breathes around us like a living thing.

"Got him," I mutter, fingers already moving across the touchpad.

Voss doesn't look up from his own terminal, a second Panasonic perched precariously on a stack of evidence folders. His reflection stares back from the dark screen, hollow-eyed and sharp-featured in the ambient glow. "Camera three, mezzanine level. Last visual was security checkpoint two."

I pull up the feed. Grainy. Green-tinted. The kind of footage they show in detective shows when they want you to know something important is happening but can't quite afford decent equipment. Except this isn't television—this is real, and those aren't actors filtering through the turnstiles below.

A Gnome. Hard to miss him even in the crowd. He's shorter than most, stockier, clutching a briefcase against his chest like it contains his soul. Which, in a way, it does. Kix—or whatever his real name is; we've got him filed under forty-seven different aliases—is sweating. You can see it even through the shitty resolution, little beads catching the fluorescent lights above.

"Bingo," Voss says quietly.

Kix's head swivels left, then right. Paranoid. Good. Should be paranoid. The BATC doesn't take kindly to accountants who suddenly develop consciences. Especially ones who've decrypted their slush-fund ledgers and are trying to walk away with the proof.

He checks his pager. One of those old Nokia models, extended antenna stabbing upward like an accusation. A relic. Analog. Untraceable.

Except we traced it anyway.

"Marcus." I keep my voice low. We're not supposed to be here, technically speaking. Not without a warrant, which we don't have, because officially speaking, the slush fund doesn't exist and neither do the decrypted ledgers.

Morvain shifts beside me, leather jacket creaking. "Yeah?"

"Once he hits the lower platform, seal the gates."

The question hangs unspoken between us. Collateral damage. Three hundred fourteen metahumans caught underground while Kix makes his play. Local 405 Dwarven machinists heading home after a double shift at the shipyards. Office workers. Students. All of them just trying to get somewhere else before midnight.

Voss finally looks up from his terminal, meeting my eyes for half a second. He knows what I'm asking. Whether we're really going to trap all those people just to catch one rat.

His gaze drops back to the screen. Doesn't answer.

Below us, Kix descends the escalator. Each step takes him deeper into the station's throat. He disappears briefly behind a pillar, then emerges on the lower platform—platform seven, the dead-end track that only sees service during rush hour. The crowd swells around him: a sea of blue work jackets and business casual, bodies pressing together in the confined space.

"He's at the bottom," Voss says.

I watch Kix merge into the mass of commuters. Almost invisible now. Just another body waiting for a train that won't come.

My hand hovers over the keyboard. One command. That's all it takes.

*Thump-thump. Thump-thump.*

The turnstiles keep spinning, feeding more people into the trap.

"You sure about this?" Morvain asks, though he's already reaching for his sidearm, checking the magazine out of habit.

"Positive." Not entirely true. But necessary? Absolutely.

Voss types something into his terminal. The mainframe connection lights up green. His fingers find the execute key.

The steel quarantine gates slam shut with a hydraulic *bang* that echoes through every speaker in the system.

On camera three, the crowd below doesn't immediately react. They're still moving, still talking, oblivious. Then someone notices—the Dwarf near the front, looking back over his shoulder. Another person catches it. Then another.

Panic starts as a ripple. Becomes a wave.

Kix spins around in the middle of the platform, briefcase still clutched to his chest, eyes wide and white in his dark face.

He just realized he's trapped.

And so are 314 other souls.

"Now we wait," I say, killing the audio feed because I don't want to hear what happens next.

* * *

I'm slamming through the gates of the Pulaski Skyway when the call comes through on my helmet comms. The Nextel crackles—Command, but wrong. Urgent. Wrong.

"Code Black. Sub-Station Seven." The voice is Morvain's handler, but there's something underneath it. Fear? Excitement? Can't tell anymore after twelve years in BATC Aegis. "Confirmed Class-5 Necro-Plague aerosol release on the lower concourse. Seal the grid. Scramble Aegis immediately. We need thermal purge protocols."

My hands tighten on the wheel of the Interceptor. Class-5. That's not just containment—that's scorched earth protocol. Burn everything within a half-mile radius. Turn the whole sector into glass if we have to.

The Skyway stretches out ahead, rust-streaked concrete pillars disappearing into November fog rolling off Newark Bay. Behind me, the rest of my squad peels off from HQ—Jackson on his bike, Chen and Rodriguez in the second vehicle. We're a six-person rapid response team designed for exactly this kind of shit.

But something doesn't sit right.

"The hell is a Class-5 doing at Sub-Station Seven?" I key back, my voice sharp even through the helmet compression. "That's a civilian transit hub. Where'd the threat intel come from?"

Silence. Then: "Direct report from on-site security. Multiple casualties already. They're venting the station now—you'll see the signature from three blocks out."

White-lilac magic flares hot around my knuckles where they grip the steering wheel. My bio-wards are clean. Pure. Telling me nothing about what's waiting down there except that my instincts are screaming wrong.

I floor the accelerator anyway.

The Interceptor whips off the Skyway exit toward Kearny, tires screeching onto surface streets. The city bleeds past—industrial parks, rusted warehouses, the skeletal remains of the old Passaic River waterfront. Four miles. Three and a half.

"Command, what assets are we talking about?" I demand. "How many tangos on site?"

More silence. This time longer.

"They're gone," comes the reply. "Security footage shows three individuals deploying aerosol canisters from a service elevator. Lower platform. Then they vanished into the maintenance tunnels. Vance, you have eyes-on command once you arrive. Full tactical authority to execute purge protocol if necessary."

My jaw clenches so hard it aches. Tactical authority means they're betting my life that I know what I'm walking into. That my training will override whatever bullshit is waiting.

Two miles. The smell hits first—that acrid chemical burn that means Isfet contamination. But underneath it, something else. Not death exactly. Something colder.

Something planned.

"I want thermal scanners running full spectrum the second we breach," I order the squad. "Chen, Rodriguez—environmental sampling kits ready. Jackson, you're with me on initial entry. We secure the perimeter, then we clear platform by platform. No heroes, no assumptions. This could be a trap, or it could be legitimate, but either way, we do this right or not at all."

A chorus of acknowledgments crackles back through the comms. Good soldiers. Smart enough to know when their commander's worried.

Half a mile out, I kill the lights and sirens. We roll in quiet mode, engines idling rough and hungry as we approach Sub-Station Seven's surface entrance. The whole structure looks wrong somehow—inert, like someone hit pause on reality itself.

I park parallel to the station entrance, blocking it. Chen and Rodriguez immediately deploy environmental sensors while Jackson covers our six with his rifle up and trained on the dark windows above us.

"Signatures are... fuck." Chen's voice goes tight. "Commander, you need to see this readout."

I pull up short of the entrance doors, my boots crunching on shattered glass from some long-ago vandalism. The sensor display illuminates my HUD:

NECRO-PLAGUE: NEGATIVE
ISFET CONTAMINATION: MINIMAL, VENTED
THERMAL SIGNATURES: ZERO ACTIVE SOURCES

"What the hell does that mean?" Jackson mutters behind me. "If there was an aerosol release..."

"Exactly." I stare at those readings, white-lilac magic crawling up my spine now, protective and hot. "Either we got lucky as hell, or someone wanted us to think there was an attack."

The entrance doors hang open ahead. Darkness beyond them. And underneath everything—so faint I almost miss it—that distinctive mechanical hiss of pressurized vapor systems shutting down.

Someone just turned off whatever they were using to sell this lie.

"Stay sharp," I whisper into the comms. "We're going in."

My hand finds the grip of my sidearm as I cross the threshold. The station concourse yawns before us—tiled walls, flickering fluorescent lights, empty benches. No bodies. No signs of struggle. Just... nothing.

Except for the smell. That chemical burn still clinging to the air like a ghost.

And the distant sound of footsteps echoing up from below.

"Contact!" Rodriguez shouts through the comms. "Multiple heat signatures, lower platform! They're moving fast!"

My magic flares full spectrum, washing the darkness in revealing light. White-lilac fire spills from my palms, illuminating every surface as I surge forward into the station's depths.

They want a fight? Fine.

Let's give them one.

## CHAPTER 2: CHAPTER 2

The locker room reeks of disinfectant and old sweat, fluorescent tubes buzzing overhead like trapped insects. I'm standing at my locker, palms extended, watching the arcane current flow through my hands. Pure flame—that's what the instructors called it during basic training. White-lilac and clean, smelling of cedar shavings from some pristine forest that never existed outside my mind's eye.

It dances across my fingers without burning. Not yet. Not today.

"Still showing off for the new recruits?" Saris leans against the bench behind me, her Street-Elf features sharp in the harsh lighting. She's already geared up—light tactical armor, her modified Barrett M82 resting against the wall like an old friend.

I let the flame gutter out with a thought. "Just keeping the edge sharp."

"That flame gonna save you when we're knee-deep in Splicers?" Gart shuffles over, his Ogre-Kin frame making the doorway look smaller. He slams a depleted-bismuth magazine into his MP5 with enough force to crack bone. The metallic clatter echoes off the concrete walls. "Heard rumors about Sub-Station Seven. Whole sector went dark three hours ago."

"Rumors don't kill us," I say, closing my locker with a soft click. My hand finds the Nextel pager clipped to my tactical belt. Still silent. Still calm.

The silence doesn't last.

Klaxons split the air like angry gods. Red emergency lights strobe across every surface, turning blood into crimson waterfalls on the white tile floor. My pager vibrates violently against my hip—an aggressive buzzing that feels like insects crawling under my skin.

I yank it free, thumb pressing the LCD screen awake. Green backlit text bleeds across the display:

*CODE RED. CLASS-5 NECRO-PLAGUE. SUB-STATION SEVEN.*

My stomach drops. Class-5. Flesh-eater status. That's not just dead bodies shambling through the streets—that's people melting from the inside out, their flesh turning to black ichor while they're still screaming. That's entire blocks becoming quarantine zones overnight.

"What is it?" Saris is already moving to her gear.

I hold up the pager, watch the emergency alert scroll past again. "Sub-Seven. They've got a plague outbreak."

Gart's laugh is dry as corpse dust. "You gotta be shitting me. That station's been running cold for months. What are the odds?"

"Not our job to calculate probabilities." I'm already pulling on my MOPP-suit, the chemical-resistant fabric cool against my skin. The zipper teeth catch and pull. "Full containment protocols. Rebreathers mandatory. This isn't a reconnaissance mission."

My hands move automatically—check the seals on the suit, verify the rebreather cartridge, mag-check my sidearm. The routine calms something deep in my chest even as adrenaline sharpens my vision to razor edges.

"Sixty seconds," I say, holstering the weapon with practiced efficiency. "Garage. Now."

Saris grabs her rifle, slinging it across her shoulder. Gart's already at the door, his massive frame blocking what little light filters into the corridor beyond.

The dispatch radio mounted on the wall squawks to life before we can make it out: "...multiple casualties reported... Sub-Station Seven command center is... repeat, command center is compromised..." The voice cuts off mid-word, replaced by static that sounds like a thousand flies dying at once.

I don't wait to hear more.

My boots hit the corridor hard, sprinting toward the garage where our transport sits waiting. Behind me, I hear Saris and Gart following close—their footsteps echoing mine in perfect rhythm.

Class-5 necro-plague. Sub-Station Seven.

The words taste like copper and fear on my tongue.

* * *

The rain hits the windshield like buckshot, each drop exploding into starbursts under the strobing amber lights of the city. I'm pressed against the passenger door of our lead Crown Vic, watching Neon Hollow blur past—sodium-vapor lamps bleeding through the sheets of water, turning everything the color of old bruises. The wipers slap their relentless rhythm: thump-thump, thump-thump, thump-thump.

"Twenty seconds," growls Martinez from behind the wheel. He doesn't look at me. His knuckles are white on the steering column, veins standing out like cables on his forearms. The radio crackles with cross-talk from the three other vehicles trailing us through the downpour—a convoy of dark sedans that look like they belong to repo men instead of BATC Aegis Command.

I turn my attention to the IBM ThinkPad bolted to the dashboard, its screen glowing that sickly green that always reminds me of emergency room lighting. My fingers move across the clunky keyboard, pulling up the telemetry feed Vane Thorne's team managed to scrape together before the comms went dark at Sub-Station Seven. The numbers scroll by, clinical and terrifying:

*Bio-hazard bloom detected. Class-5 pathogen. Containment breach imminent.*

Class-5. Jesus Christ.

Saris shifts in the back seat, her optical-camo scope clicking softly as she cycles through magnification settings. She's a Street-Elf, which means she grew up navigating these neon-soaked streets before we ever recruited her. Means she knows what happens when something goes wrong in the tunnels beneath Newark Bay. "Boss," she says, her voice cutting through the wail of our sirens, "a Class-5 means a 90% mortality rate in ten minutes. If it's already in the ventilation system, we're just showing up to bag bodies."

The Crown Vic hits a pothole hard enough to rattle my teeth. I grip the edge of the ThinkPad's mounting bracket and stare at the data streaming past. The bio-signatures aren't moving. They're stationary. That's either good news or the worst news possible—depending on whether containment is holding or if everything below ground is already dead and the sensors just haven't registered it yet.

"Command says it's contained to the lower platform." My own voice sounds hollow in the cramped space. "We hold the line at the mezzanine. Thermal purge protocol is authorized if we can't secure the ventilation manifold."

Martinez finally glances at me, his reflection ghostly in the side mirror. "You believe that?"

I don't answer. Don't need to. We both know Command has been wrong before. We all do.

The other cars peel off as we approach Sub-Station Seven—their headlights sweeping across the entrance plaza like search beams at a crime scene. Which, I suppose, is exactly what this is. The rain hammers down harder now, or maybe it's just gotten louder because we've stopped moving. The sirens cut out with a mechanical whine, leaving an eerie silence broken only by water drumming against metal.

Then I see them: plumes of black smoke rising from the subway grates nearest the station entrance. Not steam—smoke. And through our windshield, barely visible through the curtain of rain, civilians are pouring out of the main doors. Their mouths are open. Screaming about gas attacks. Chemical weapons. Something worse.

Saris is already out of the car, her scope raised, scanning for hostiles. Martinez follows, his service pistol drawn but held low. Our team spreads out across the plaza in practiced formation—four more tactical units emerging from the other Crown Vics, securing perimeters, establishing a containment zone.

I step into the rain myself, the cold immediately soaking through my tactical jacket. The smell hits me next—not gas, not chemicals. Something organic and rotten, like meat left too long in summer heat. My stomach clenches.

"Vance to Command." My radio crackles. "We have visual confirmation of civilian exodus. Smoke plumes emerging from ventilation grates. Requesting immediate clarification on biological agent characteristics."

Static answers me. Then Thorne's voice, tinny and distant: "Unclear. Sensors indicate possible mutation. Proceed with extreme caution."

Possible mutation.

The words hang in the air as I watch another figure stumble from the station entrance—a man in a business suit, his face streaming with something that isn't rain. Blood? Sweat? He collapses onto the wet pavement five feet away, convulsing.

My hand moves instinctively to my sidearm.

Behind me, one of the newer recruits vomits into the gutter.

* * *

The overhead fluorescents flickered once, twice, then died completely as we hit the upper concourse of Sub-Station Seven. Emergency reds kicked on immediately, bathing everything in arterial crimson that made my squad look like demons emerging from hell itself. Which, I suppose, wasn't far from the truth.

"Commander! North side's a shitshow!" Rodriguez's voice crackled in my ear as we pushed through the first wave of commuters. The woman was human—a rare choice for Aegis work, but she'd proven herself during the Kearny Marshes incident three years back. She knew how to hold ground when the world went sideways.

I didn't answer. Couldn't waste breath. My lungs were already burning from the sprint up the emergency stairs, but there was no time for anything less than maximum momentum. The station smelled wrong—metal and ozone and something else underneath it all, something sweet-rotten that made my pure weave crawl along my spine like ice water.

The crowd thinned ahead where a handful of Newark PD officers had managed to string up yellow tape across one of the main thoroughfares. An Orc beat cop, easily seven feet tall with tusks that gleamed in the red lighting, was trying to reason with a woman clutching two kids. His voice rumbled so deep it vibrated in my sternum even from twenty feet away.

"Not safe down there, ma'am. Station's locked down."

"I need to get to Elizabeth!" She was screaming now, her face contorted. "My daughter's at daycare in Union County!"

I flared my ward.

The white-lilac light exploded outward from my chest in a perfect sphere, sixty feet across, pure and brilliant enough to make everyone within its radius cry out and throw hands over their eyes. For just one crystalline second, every eye in the concourse was on me—on the glowing sigil burning on my uniform, on the way my hair lifted in a wind that wasn't there, on the absolute certainty that whatever was happening, I was in charge of it now.

The silence lasted exactly that long.

Then I spoke, my voice carrying that particular edge that came with channeling pure weave authority: "PD! Get that tape up properly. No one crosses until I say otherwise." I turned my head slightly toward Rodriguez without breaking eye contact with the nearest officer. "Status report."

"North escalator secured," she said, already moving to enforce my order. "South still open but PD's setting up barricades."

"Captain!" I called out, spotting the human lieutenant coordinating the police effort. He looked exhausted, his weathered face tight with the kind of tension that came from watching chaos unfold and knowing you couldn't stop it all no matter how hard you tried. "Vance, Aegis Command. Brief me."

He jogged over, Motorola crackling with static from his shoulder mic. "Lower platform sealed about five minutes ago. Transit Authority shut down the mechanical gates and locked them remotely. But people are trapped between here and there—maybe three hundred souls caught in the middle sections when the alarm went off." His jaw worked. "We're getting reports of... disturbances. From below."

Before he could elaborate, my earpiece lit up with Vane Thorne's distinctive voice—that High-Spire Elven cadence that always made him sound like he was talking down to you even when he wasn't trying to. Which, given his position as BATC Internal Affairs Inquisitor, meant he pretty much always was.

*"Commander Vance."* The connection was crystal clear despite the ambient noise around us. Thorne had access to tech that made standard military comms look like tin cans on a string. *"Hold your current position at the upper mezzanine. Do not—repeat, do NOT—attempt entry into the lower platform. The necro-pathogen is demonstrating volatile expansion characteristics."*

I started walking toward the security overlook without responding. Behind me, I heard Rodriguez shouting orders, her boots heavy on the concrete as she positioned my squad to control access points. The glass barrier of the observation deck gleamed ahead, reinforced acrylic thick enough to stop rifle fire, designed so station masters could watch the trains come and go without risking getting hit by debris or derailment.

*"Commander."* Thorne's tone sharpened. *"Did you copy? We're still establishing containment protocols. Your primary objective is perimeter enforcement."*

"Copy that, Command," I said evenly, my hand already reaching for the railing leading up to the overlook. The metal felt cold through my tactical gloves. "I'm approaching the overlook now to assess the situation from elevated position."

The stairs were narrow, steep enough that someone in full gear would have trouble if they rushed them. I took them two at a time anyway. My heart hammered against my ribs—not from exertion but from the same primal instinct that had kept humanity alive when everything else tried to kill them. Something bad was waiting downstairs. Something that made even Thorne nervous, which was saying something given his usual attitude of superior indifference.

I reached the top step.

And looked down.

My breath caught.

Through the reinforced glass, the entire lower platform stretched out below like a diorama of damnation. Emergency lighting cast everything in shades of red and shadow, turning ordinary commuters into silhouettes frozen mid-action. Some were running. Others stood stock-still, staring at something out of frame. A few were on their knees, hands pressed to their faces.

All three hundred of them trapped behind the locked mechanical gates.

And between where I stood and where they waited, in the gaps between the support pillars and beneath the departures board, things moved in ways that shouldn't be possible. Shapes that were wrong—too tall, too angular, leaving trails of darkness that seemed to eat the light around them.

One of them turned toward the upper mezzanine.

Toward me.

Its face was human-shaped but all the proportions were stretched like taffy pulled too thin. Eyes like holes burned through paper stared directly upward with an intelligence that predated anything biological. When it opened its mouth, I heard nothing through the thick glass—but I saw teeth that weren't meant for chewing food, only tearing flesh and bone.

"Oh fuck," Rodriguez whispered in my ear. "Commander, you seeing what I'm seeing?"

The thing smiled.

And began climbing.

Not up the stairs or escalators—directly up the wall, fingers digging into concrete as if it were butter. Behind it, others stirred. The plague vector wasn't expanding anymore.

It had already arrived.

"The lower level is compromised," I said into my mic, voice steady even as my pure weave flared instinctively around me like a shield. "I have visual confirmation. We need evac protocols now."

*"Negative."* Thorne again, but his usual smugness was gone replaced by something closer to fear. *"Standby for extraction, Commander. Do NOT engage."*

Below me, the first creature reached the top of the wall and stepped onto the reinforced glass. ## CHAPTER 3: CHAPTER 3

The security booth reeks of stale coffee and fear. My hand trembles as I press it against the reinforced glass, feeling the vibrations of 314 desperate souls pounding from below.

"Three-one-four," I whisper, my voice cracking. "Christ."

Through the double-paned safety glass, the lower platform of Sub-Station Seven sprawls like a hell-bound waiting room. The fluorescent lights flicker overhead—one, two, three remaining bulbs sputtering their last breath before a transformer explodes somewhere deep in the station's guts. Darkness eats at the edges of the platform, creeping inward like oil spreading across water.

But even in the dying light, I can see them. All of them.

Local 405 Dwarves cluster near the locked gates, their calloused hands still hammering against the steel bars with that rhythmic, desperate percussion. Their Carhartt jackets, once the color of rust and forest green, now look gray in the emergency lighting. Somewhere in that mass, I recognize Big Jax Morrison—his wife was pregnant when I pulled him out of a collapsed building last October. His fists beat faster than the others, his mouth moving in silent screams I can't hear through the glass.

Street-Elves weave between the humans, clutching grocery bags to their narrow chests. One of them—a woman with braided silver hair—keeps looking back toward the far end of the platform where her children must be. Her pointed ears are flat against her skull, a sign of pure terror I learned to read during the Kearny Marshes incident. She knows something. They all know something, and it's turning their blood to ice.

The humans are worst of all. A teenager drops his Sony MiniDisc player, the plastic case clattering across the tile floor as he scrambles backward. It spins twice, its LCD screen still displaying the track list of some pop-punk album that will never finish playing. The kid who dropped it has his arm around a smaller figure—a sister? Little brother? They're crying now, both of them, pressed together like drowning swimmers.

Orc laborers stand at the periphery, their massive frames creating a perimeter they didn't choose. Their green skin has gone pale, almost jaundiced under the failing lights. One of them—the one with the faded prison tattoos running up his neck—has his eyes closed. He's praying. I've seen enough Orcs to know when they've accepted death.

My magic flares without conscious thought. White-lilac energy coils around my wrist, tendrils of clean cedar scent filling the cramped booth. It wants out. It *needs* to reach down there, to wrap itself around those 314 souls and yank them through the glass, through reality itself if necessary.

But magic doesn't work like that. Not even mine.

"Commander." Gart's voice rumbles from beside me, low and dangerous. His Orcish features have gone hard, all soft tissue retracted, leaving nothing but bone and muscle. "Boss."

I don't look at him. Can't tear my eyes away from the platform.

"There's kids down there," he continues, each word clipped, controlled. "Half the South Side shift is on that platform. The night crews. The ones pulling double shifts at County General because we're short-staffed."

"I see them." My knuckles are white against the glass. Blood and magic pulse in tandem through my veins.

Gart steps closer, his bulk filling half my field of vision. "The ventilation system runs underneath Platform Seven. If whatever's coming—"

"It's already here," I cut him off.

The goblin whimpers somewhere behind me.

"Zip him," I tell Martinez, who's moved in to assist. "Tag everything. We're done here."

My hand drops to my side, resting on the grip of my sidearm. The weight is familiar now. Comfortable, even. Two years ago, this would have felt wrong. Two years ago, there was something inside me that recoiled at the idea of pointing a gun at someone who wasn't actively trying to kill me.

That something is gone.

Martinez pulls the zip-cuffs from his belt with practiced efficiency. They snap shut around the Elf's wrists with a sound like breaking bones. He doesn't struggle anymore. Just stares up at me with those wounded animal eyes while rain continues its relentless assault on the roof above us.

Outside, November wind howls through gaps in the warehouse walls. Neon light from the street bleeds through in sickly greens and pinks, painting everything in colors that don't exist in nature. My shift ends in fifteen minutes. Then I can go home to my apartment in the Sterling Heights complex—one of the perks of command—and sit in the dark for six hours before it starts all over again.

The smell of wet charred wool intensifies as I exhale slowly.

"Commander?" Martinez's voice breaks through my thoughts. "We're clear. Ready to move out?"

I nod once, sharp. Professional.

"Let's go."

* * *

The rain falls in sheets across Neon Hollow, turning the cracked pavement into rivers of oil-slick darkness. I pull my collar higher, feeling the wool scrape against the scarred skin of my throat. Three years since the breach. Three years since County General burned.

My shoes slap against the wet concrete as I walk. Each step sends small waves rippling outward, disturbing the reflection of the sodium-vapor lights above. They cast everything in that sickly orange glow, making the abandoned storefronts look like something from another world—one where hope still existed.

I pass a row of parked cars, their chrome catching and fragmenting the light. An old Buick with missing hubcaps. A sedan someone's painted to match their gang colors—the faded blue and gold of the Diablos. Past them all until I reach it: my assigned vehicle. A black 1999 Crown Victoria, unmarked, nondescript. Just another government car in a city full of them.

I lean against the hood, letting the freezing rain soak through my hair. The metal is cold even through the fabric of my coat, that penetrating chill that seems to come from deeper than the surface. I close my eyes and do my count.

*Three hundred and fourteen. Thirty-eight.*

Numbers. Always numbers. They're the only things keeping me tethered anymore, the only threads connecting me to whatever passes for reality these days. Three hundred and fourteen days since I woke up in Sub-Station Seven with no memory of how I got there. Thirty-eight souls lost during the Kearny Marshes incident—an incident I can't remember being part of but somehow commanded.

I reach into my pocket and pull out my Nokia pager. The green backlight illuminates my face as I check the time: 11:42 PM.

A group of Orc dockworkers cross on the opposite sidewalk, their laughter cutting through the sound of the rain. One of them uncaps a flask, takes a long drink, then passes it around while they share some joke I'm too far away to hear. Their voices are rough, genuine—alive in a way I haven't been since the ash-gray static settled into my bones.

I watch them like a ghost watching the living. When they pass, I whisper my count into the falling water.

*"Three hundred and fourteen."*

The words dissolve instantly, washed away by the downpour.

* * *

The pager vibrates against my hip like a dying insect. I reach for it without thinking, muscle memory older than thought itself. Two years of carrying this thing around, and still the sudden buzz makes my teeth grind.

I unclip it from my belt. The green LCD screen bleeds light across my knuckles, casting everything in that sickly emergency room glow. Priority One dispatch. Direct from Command. From *his* office.

My thumb hovers over the message button. The ash-gray static in my blood hums its familiar warning, but there's no point in delaying. There never is.

`UNSANCTIONED ARCANE DETONATION. WAREHOUSE DISTRICT. SUSPECT ARMED WITH ILLEGAL TELLURIC HARDWARE. DEADLY FORCE AUTHORIZED.`

Of course they are. They always are.

I stare at those words until the letters blur. Another night. Another mess. Another body count that won't appear in any official report because some things are better left buried under layers of classification so thick they might as well be tombstones.

The rain hasn't let up since midnight. It drums against the Crown Vic's roof in a rhythm that sounds almost mocking—*thwack-thwack-thwack*—like someone trying to get my attention. Like the universe wants me to notice how perfectly it all fits together. The storm, the killing, the orders that come down from on high wrapped in bureaucratic chains.

I unlock the car door. The hinges groan, protesting the weight of water clinging to every surface. The interior smells like stale coffee and leather, and underneath that, something else. Something metallic and sharp that reminds me of gunpowder and old blood.

I slide into the driver's seat. The cold leather sticks to my damp clothes for just a second before releasing. My fingers find the key in the ignition. I turn it.

The V8 engine rumbles to life beneath me—a deep, guttural sound that vibrates through the chassis and into my bones. For a moment, just one heartbeat, I can pretend this is something other than what it is. Just a woman in a car, driving through the rain toward somewhere that isn't here.

But that's a lie we both know.

I put the car in gear. The windshield wipers slap away sheets of water, clearing just enough space to see forward. Forward into the dark. Forward into whatever fresh nightmare BATC Command has cooked up for me tonight.

I reach for the radio mic. The cord is heavy, weighted, pulling at my hand like it's connected to lead ballast instead of plastic and wire. I press the button.

"My name is Vance," I say, and my voice comes out flat, dead, hollowed out by two years of carrying ghosts that won't let go. "I have the page. I'm en route to the Warehouse District."

There's a crackle of static on the other end, then nothing but silence. They heard me. They always do.

I release the button. The mic clicks back into place with a sound that feels final, absolute. Like there's no going back from this. Not that there ever was.

The Crown Vic accelerates into Neon Hollow's rain-soaked streets. The headlights cut through torrential downpour, twin cones of light pushing back against an darkness that wants nothing more than to swallow everything whole.

Warehouse District. Telluric hardware. Deadly force authorized.

Those words sit in my chest like shrapnel, sharp and unignorable. The Lead-Bismuth Geas coils tighter around my heart—a familiar weight, a familiar cage. Two years and it still hasn't gotten any lighter.

I drive deeper into the night.

Somewhere ahead, in the gutters of the Warehouse District, someone's already dead. And I'm on my way to make sure they stay that way.

The rain keeps falling.

The engine keeps rumbling.

And somewhere between here and there—between now and whatever fresh horror waits—I feel something cold settle over me like ash.

The halo of it all.
