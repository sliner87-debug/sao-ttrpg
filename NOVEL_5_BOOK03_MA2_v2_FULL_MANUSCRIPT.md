## CHAPTER 1: THE DEAD ELVEN SNIPER

The crack was deafening—a thunderclap that ripped through my ribcage and left my ears screaming with white noise.

I flinched hard, my Aegis shields snapping up in a burst of white-lilac light, bracing for the impact, for the hot lead to tear through flesh and bone. My heart hammered against my sternum. One second. Two seconds. Nothing.

The silence that followed was worse than the gunshot.

My breath fogged in the freezing air as I turned slowly, the Motorola Razr clutched tight in my palm. Its flashlight beam swept across rust-streaked concrete, over tangled conduit cables dripping with condensation, up toward the gantry thirty feet behind me where Kaelen "Glass-Eye" had been perched like some Gothic gargoyle, his rifle trained on my spine.

He wasn't there anymore.

What remained slumped against the metal railing was something else entirely—the shell of what had been an Elven sniper. Half his skull was gone, collapsed inward into a crater of pulped tissue and bone fragments. The exit wound was massive, fist-sized, the edges cauterized black around a dark crater. Behind him, the concrete wall wept gray dust from where the round had punched straight through reinforced barrier.

My stomach lurched.

Not from the gore—though God knows there was enough to make even seasoned Aegis grimace—but from the implications tearing through my mind like shrapnel. That shot hadn't come from Kaelen's position. It had come from somewhere *behind* that concrete pillar. From where the Butcher of Sterling Manor was supposed to be hiding in the shadows, watching, waiting.

Cole Reyes just saved my life.

I moved closer, boots crunching on shattered glass and grit. The stench hit me next—wet charred wool mixed with iron-gall residue sizzling from the Elf's optical-camo suit, leaking blue-black fluid onto the floor below. My white-lilac ward flickered, fighting against an ash-gray static that made my teeth ache.

The dead Elf's modifications were all wrong for High-Spire work. Sub-dermal wire-jacks at his temples, cheap optical camo stitched into what looked like a 2004 North Face jacket, probably stolen from some dead backpacker up in the Passaic River woods. No elegance. No refinement. Just desperate Razor-Wire street-magic cobled together by someone who'd never seen real Aegis work.

He'd been cold-blooded about it too—no hesitation when he drew down on me. The kind of merc who'd put two rounds in your head without a second thought.

And Cole had stopped him anyway.

I crouched beside the corpse, ignoring how the temperature kept dropping around me—that telltale frost-cobalt signature Cole left behind when he worked up close. My fingers found something embedded in the steel grating near the wall: a slug, still humming with residual energy. I pried it loose carefully, holding it up to the sodium-vapor glare.

It was huge—not standard BATC issue. Some kind of hand-loaded round, the metal frosted white with that same unnatural cold radiating off it. Depleted bismuth maybe, or something worse. Something that could punch through concrete and bone alike without slowing down.

In the distance, sirens began wailing.

BATC response teams, finally getting their shit together enough to follow my trail into the sub-basements. They'd find this mess soon enough—the dead sniper, the exit wound through solid concrete, the slug that shouldn't exist according to any ordnance database.

They'd ask questions I couldn't answer.

I pocketed the round anyway, standing slowly as my joints protested the cold seeping into my bones. The tinnitus in my ears was fading, replaced by the rhythmic thump-thump of blood rushing back to my head. Alive. Somehow still alive.

Cole Reyes saved you.

The thought sat bitter on my tongue as I moved toward the far end of the corridor, leaving Glass-Eye's body slumping against his gantry like discarded meat. Behind me, the sirens grew louder, echoing through the transit tunnels like hunting dogs closing in for the kill.

I needed to move faster.

Needed to figure out what the hell had just happened before someone decided I was worth more dead than alive—which, given my current track record, wouldn't take long at all.

The ash-gray static recedes like fog burned away by noon sun, though it leaves its signature—a bitter taste of copper behind my teeth, the phantom stench of wet charred wool clinging to the back of my throat from November 2005. I force my breath to slow. Four counts in. Hold. Four counts out. The tactical training kicks in before the trauma can fully manifest.

My hands stop shaking.

I crouch beside what remains of Kaelen—or whoever he was pretending to be. The corpse slumps against the reinforced concrete pillar, dark ichor pooling beneath his skull where Cole Reyes' slug punched through three feet of engineered stone and bone alike. The entry wound is small, nearly surgical despite the violence of impact. Exit wound... well, there isn't one. Not anymore. What came out the other side vaporized into copper-tinged mist that still hangs in the air like morning fog over the Passaic River.

The corpse wears military-grade optical camouflage—expensive tech, the kind that runs off portable battery packs and leaves distinctive thermal signatures when it fails. This one is failing. I trace the rune workings etched into the suppressor housing. Standard activation glyphs, but there's something else underneath. A serial number, partially obscured by scoring from repeated firing cycles. I lean closer, squinting against the Toughbook's backlight as I enhance the image.

Or maybe both.

He wears dark tactical gear over what looks like civilian clothes underneath—jeans, leather jacket, nothing that identifies him beyond the BATC-issued radio clipping to his collar.

I pull up the Bureau database on my Toughbook. Cross-reference the serial number with active personnel files. The search engine whirs.

Which means either it's a fake—elaborately constructed to look legitimate—or someone has scrubbed the records already. Cover-up in progress.

Movement catches my peripheral vision. There—partially concealed behind an exposed water pipe near the gantry access ladder. A secondary modification on Kaelen's rifle. Some kind of transmitter unit, still blinking its status light red.

Active transmission.

My heart rate spikes as I lean closer, careful not to disturb the body. The device is embedded in the weapon's stock, custom-fitted. Professional work. Not something you slap together in a garage between shifts at the McCarter Highway dockyards.

I snap another photo. Then another. Multiple angles because this—this is important somehow. I can feel it in my bones.

The Panasonic Toughbook saves each image automatically, timestamped and geo-tagged to Pylon Two sub-basement coordinates. Chain-of-custody protocols engage. Digital signatures lock into place.

"Commander Vance, team is approaching your position. ETA thirty seconds."

Thorne again. Closer now. They're taking the stairs instead of waiting for the transit car.

"Copy." I stand slowly, knees protesting from crouching beside a corpse that shouldn't exist. From surviving what should have been fatal.

Kaelen's eyes are open. Still open. Staring at nothing. Waiting for answers that won't come from a dead man.

Why did Cole spare me?

The question gnaws at me like rats in the walls of some abandoned warehouse along the Newark Bay shoreline. He had the shot. Had already killed once tonight—the sniper who'd been targeting me through the reinforced concrete pillar. Could have added me to his scorecard without breaking stride.

But he didn't.

Which means either: one, he needs me alive for something; two, there's a line even someone like him won't cross; or three, I'm useful to him in ways I haven't figured out yet.

Or maybe all three.

The flashing red-and-blue lights paint everything in strobing crimson and ice-white. I can hear them now—the distant squeal of tires on wet asphalt above ground, the rhythmic thump-thump-thump of helicopter rotors cutting through the November rain. They're closing in.

Sixty seconds. Maybe less.

My white-lilac ward flares weakly around my fingertips, casting sickly purple shadows across the concrete. It tastes like copper pennies and fresh-cut cedar, but underneath—that phantom stench rises again. Wet charred wool. 2005. This isn't just ammunition. This is Cole Reyes's signature, his ghost-mark burned into every molecule. If BATC finds it here, if they run the trace...

He'll never make it to Pylon #3.

I don't know why I care. The Butcher killed seventeen agents last month—including Orcish heavies twice my size who actually knew what they were doing. He's responsible for three breaches in as many weeks. By protocol, by duty, by the oath I took when they pinned these bars on my collar, I should be bagging this evidence right now, calling it in.

But I'm standing here instead, staring at my reflection in the polished metal.

My nose starts bleeding first. The white-lilac weave protests violently as I force it open wider than recommended, pushing bio-light through my circulatory system until my veins glow faintly beneath my skin. The feedback hits like an ice-pick behind my eyes. My knees want to buckle, but I lock them straight.

*Just scrub. Just clean. Just erase.*

The light pours into the slug, and I feel Cole's signature fighting back—frost-cobalt resistance burning against my palms. There's intention in this ammunition. Purpose. Someone loaded this round expecting to kill someone important, and they didn't give a shit about leaving tracks.

I push harder.

Blood drips onto the concrete. My vision tunnels, the strobing lights becoming a single nauseating pulse of red-white-red-white. The metallic taste floods my mouth—copper and something worse, like licking an old battery. My fingers cramp around the slug.

And then it gives.

The frost-cobalt goes sterile. Ash-gray. Unlinked. Untraceable. Just another piece of metal that happened to be lying around Pylon #2 sub-basement where someone fired it through a concrete pillar during a breach situation which is frankly not even that unusual given current conditions and—

I stuff it in my pocket.

The rifle scope comes off next, IA-chip still embedded in the mount. I can feel its ghost-marks too—the way Cole looked through it, the targets he acquired, the breath he held before squeezing down on the trigger. All of it washing over me in waves that make my stomach turn.

But I take it anyway.

My white-lilac ward flares once, brilliant and desperate, as I wrap the scope in bio-light and tuck it against the small of my back. It burns cold against my spine, and I know I should feel guilty but mostly I just feel tired.

The doors explode inward.

"BATC! Clear! Weapons down!" The Orcish heavy doesn't wait for compliance—his flashlight beam sweeps across my face, blinding me momentarily, and I hear the rack-chamber of three shotguns at least. Human tacticals behind him, younger agents with wide eyes and tight lips who've probably never seen an actual combat scene up close.

I raise my hands slowly, blood dripping from my nose onto my uniform.

"Stand down, breach-team," I say, keeping my voice level even as my knees threaten to give out. "Threat is neutralized."

"Commander Vance?" The lead tactical steps forward, his Maglite still trained on my face like I'm the enemy here. Which, technically, I guess I am now.

"Confirmed dead," I tell them, gesturing toward the body without looking at it again. Can't afford the hesitation, not when they're watching me this closely. "Single sniper, frost-cobalt ammunition. Caught a round through the throat. He's been neutralized."

"Where's your backup?"

"En route." Not strictly true, but they won't question it. I'm Aegis Commander. My word carries weight even when it's technically a lie.

They're going to search anyway. They always do. Protocol demands it. But they'll find nothing—just concrete dust and blood spatter and the faint smell of burnt cedar that means someone used heavy weave here and didn't bother cleaning up after themselves which frankly is sloppy workmanship but whatever.

My hand brushes against something hard and metallic sliding down the inside of my tactical belt.

I don't react. Don't flinch. Just keep my hands raised as the tacticals spread out, their boots crunching over broken glass, weapons sweeping across corners and shadows and every goddamn hiding spot in this sub-basement corridor.

Someone just planted evidence on me.

And I have absolutely no idea if it's Cole trying to frame me or BATC already knowing what I did or something worse entirely.

The Orcish heavy approaches, sniffing the air with his nostrils flared. They can smell weave-use better than humans. He'll know I just scrubbed something. He has to know.

But he just grunts and waves his team forward.

"Clear!"

"All clear!"

"Moving to perimeter sweep!"

As they turn away, still searching, I feel the weight at my spine. The scope. The evidence. The proof that I chose to protect the Butcher over protocol, duty, everything I swore to uphold.

And beneath all that, pressing cold against my lower back: whatever the hell just got dropped onto me while I wasn't looking.

I wipe the blood from my lip with the back of my hand and force myself to stand straighter.

"Secure the area," I say. "I'm calling this in."

My hand moves to my comm-piece, and I wonder if I should be scared right now.

Wonder when exactly I became someone who makes choices like this.

Wonder if there's any going back from here.

## CHAPTER 2: THE INQUISITOR & THE TRANSPONDER

The November rain tastes like rust and diesel fuel.

I'm sitting on the bumper of an ambulance, watching my breath fog into the freezing night air while some EMT checks my vitals with practiced indifference. Her name tag says RODRIGUEZ in block letters. She's human, which means she doesn't give a damn about the faint purple burn-scorch on my left forearm where the sniper's round clipped me.

Pylon #2 is still smoking three hundred yards behind us, a skeletal tombstone for two dead men and whatever secrets they were carrying.

"Blood pressure's elevated," Rodriguez mutters, wrapping the cuff around my bicep. "You want to talk about it?"

What I want to do is scream that something is wrong—that I can feel it crawling up my spine like ice and static—but instead I watch the sodium-vapor lights flicker across the Passaic River. Somewhere past those chemical-plant smokestacks, Newark Bay churns black against the horizon.

A set of headlights cuts through the curtain of rain.

Black. Pristine. A Lincoln Town Car that costs more than Rodriguez makes in three years.

The car stops. The door opens.

Vane Thorne steps out wearing a charcoal suit that somehow looks pressed despite the weather, despite the hour, despite the fact that we're standing in a crime zone that smells like charred electronics and gunpowder. He closes the door with a soft *thunk* and stands there for a moment, letting the rain decide whether it wants to touch him or not.

It doesn't.

"Rodriguez." His voice has that smooth quality that comes from expensive vocal coaching—no regional accent, no emotional inflection, just pure corporate precision. "Step away from Commander Vance."

She looks at me. I give her a slight shake of my head. She knows what Thorne is: BATC Internal Affairs. High-Spire. Elf. The kind of metahuman who eats field commanders for breakfast and uses their bones as dental floss.

Rodriguez unbuckles the blood pressure cuff wordlessly and retreats into the ambulance, pulling the doors shut behind her with a sound like finality.

Thorne approaches slowly, his feline tapetum lucidum eyes catching the yellow streetlight in a way that makes them glow faintly, hypnotically. It's a predator's adaptation—the kind of genetic tweak that screams *I see better than you in the dark*.

"I have questions about the sniper," he says when he's close enough that I can smell his cologne cutting through the industrial smog. Something expensive. Italian. Masculine without trying too hard.

"A lot of dead people tonight." I slide off the bumper, feeling the cold seep through my tactical boots. "What's your angle?"

His smile doesn't reach those glowing eyes. "The angle is logistics, Commander. Specifically: how a Razor-Wire junkie manages to drop dead from a trajectory my forensic-mages can't even triangulate."

My hand moves automatically toward where my sidearm should be before I remember—it's in evidence. All our weapons are. Standard protocol after an officer-involved shooting.

"Sloppy work," Thorne continues, circling me like a shark testing water. "*Thwack-thwack* goes the windshield wipers. *Bang* goes the sniper. But here's what bothers me: no weave-signature on the fatal slug. None. That takes Aegis-level bio-wards. That takes resources. That takes intent."

I meet his gaze levelly, fighting the urge to touch my chest where Cole slipped that transponder during the chaos. The device feels like a living thing against my ribcage—a promise and a threat rolled into one.

"Maybe the junkie bought a defective hex-round on the South Side," I say evenly. "I don't micromanage Syndicate suicide runs."

"And maybe," Thorne counters smoothly, producing a silver warrant-stylus from his inner jacket pocket, "you're lying to a BATC Inquisitor. Level-3 IA Audit, Commander Vance. Effective immediately. Your unit's armories are restricted pending review."

He holds up the stylus. It glows faintly with corporate magic—legal paperwork woven into reality itself.

"The warrant's already filed," he adds conversationally. "Your access codes will deactivate at 0400 hours. You have approximately fifty-five minutes to brief your replacement."

Behind him, Pylon #2 groans as emergency crews begin their work, the sound echoing across the Kearny Marshes like something dying.

I want to reach for my sidearm. I want to tell him about the runner in the warehouse, about Cole, about the things I'm starting to remember that I shouldn't be able to remember because they haven't happened yet—but all I can do is stand there in the rain while Internal Affairs dismantles my command structure piece by piece.

"Anything else?" My voice comes out harder than intended.

Thorne's smile widens fractionally. "No, Commander. That covers it." He pockets the warrant-stylus with a gesture that looks almost casual but isn't. "Oh—and Vance?"

"Yes?"

His eyes catch the light again, that feline glow intensifying just slightly. "You smell like charred wool. Interesting choice for cover scent on a night like this."

Then he turns and walks back to his Town Car without waiting for an answer, sliding into the passenger seat with fluid grace while some driver I never even noticed pulls away into the darkness toward McCarter Highway.

I watch the taillights disappear around a bend in the road before allowing myself to breathe properly again.

My chest feels tight where the transponder pulses against bone.

Fifty-five minutes until my armories lock down. Fifty-five minutes until Thorne starts digging through every log file, every expenditure report, every goddamn coffee cup left on my desk.

And somewhere in this nightmare, Cole is still out there with whatever the hell he stole from Pylon #2 while I was bleeding on concrete and Vane Thorne was writing my termination papers in corporate heaven.

The EMT—Rodriguez—cracks open her door again. "You need transport?"

"Not yet," I say quietly, pulling out my burner phone from my tactical vest. The screen lights up: 0317 AM. Forty-three minutes now.

Someone needs to warn the others. Someone needs to figure out what Cole actually took and whether it's worth dying for.

But first, I pull up my contacts list and scroll past names that mean nothing anymore.

Forty-three minutes until IA locks everything down.

Forty-three minutes to save what remains of Aegis Command.

I press dial on the only number that matters right now—the one that leads deeper into whatever conspiracy just tried to kill me twice in one night—and listen to the phone ring once, twice, three times while rain runs cold down my collar and the ghost of Thorne's yellow eyes watches me from everywhere and nowhere at once.

The line clicks open.

"Vance." I keep my voice low. Urgent. "Cole made contact. We have problems."

The rain hammers down harder now, each drop striking my tactical vest like tiny needles. I can feel the weight of the transponder against my ribs where it nestles in the hollow beneath my sternum—a foreign object embedded in flesh, a ghost Cole left behind without me ever feeling its insertion.

Thorne stands between me and the Crown Vic, water streaming off his black Inquisitor's coat. He holds out one gloved hand, palm up. "Your laptop, Commander."

I don't move immediately. The Panasonic Toughbook sits in the passenger seat, its screen dark, its hard drive holding secrets that would burn Cole and the entire rogue operation to ash if discovered. My fingerprints are on every key. My suit's telemetry logs everything—biometrics, tactical positioning, weapon discharge patterns. And buried in there, compressed and hidden in what should be innocuous maintenance files, is the laser-mapping reconstruction of that impossible shot from Pylon #2.

"Commander." Thorne's voice cuts through the rain's percussion. His silver stylus taps against his other palm, waiting.

My weave tastes of charred wool as I reach into the car. The suppression is working—barely. My corrupted bio-ward spirals around my neural pathways like smoke seeking ventilation, but it's enough. Enough to keep my heart rate steady, my microexpressions locked down tight.

I lift the Toughbook carefully, hands visible, no sudden movements. As I pass it over, our fingers don't touch. His eyes never leave mine.

The moment the laptop leaves my possession, I channel the pulse.

It's subtle—I've practiced this exact maneuver in the simulations after 2005. A concentrated burst of ash-gray static, localized, precise. My corrupted weave makes the signature untraceable; even I'm not entirely sure how I'm doing it anymore, just that I can. The pulse lances through the motherboard's sensitive components.

Thorne's expression flickers—just for a fraction of a second—when he tries to connect his stylus to the USB port. There's a sharp crackle of electricity, a blue spark jumping between metal and plastic. The Toughbook's power indicator blinks once, then dies completely.

"Damn." He turns the dead machine over in his hands, examining it with narrowed eyes. "What did you do?"

"Nothing," I say flatly. "Equipment failure. You see what happens out here—the moisture, the proximity to the Pylon collapse. Electrical interference." The lie rolls off my tongue, smooth and cold. My weave burns with the effort of maintaining it, but I don't break eye contact.

"File a requisition form for a new one." I turn toward my mobile command post. "I'm going back to my vehicle. I have orphans to check on."

His hand shoots out, grabbing my arm above the elbow. Not hard enough to hurt, but firm enough to stop me. "You're not done talking yet."

I look down at where he touches me, then slowly raise my eyes to meet his gaze. When I speak, there's no inflection, no emotion—just dead air. "Remove your hand."

For a moment, tension crackles between us sharper than any static discharge. His jaw works. Then he lets go.

"I'll be monitoring your movements closely, Commander." He pulls out a Nextel, thumbnails already flying across its screen. "Thorne here. Put a tail on Vance. She's hiding something from the Pylon."

I don't wait to hear more. The rain soaks through my vest as I walk to my Crown Vic, each step deliberate, controlled. Behind me, I can feel Thorne's eyes boring into my back like drill bits seeking purchase in bone.

Inside the vehicle, I slam the door hard enough to rattle the frame. My hands are shaking—not from fear, but from the adrenaline dump of holding that lie together while my corrupted weave screamed warnings at every level of my nervous system.

The transponder pulses once against my ribs.

*He knows something is wrong,* I think, gripping the steering wheel. *He just doesn't know what.*

And if Cole's still alive somewhere in this soaked, corrupt landscape, Thorne hunting for evidence means we're running out of time to bury whatever secrets that damaged Pylon was meant to protect.

The interior of my mobile command post smells like stale coffee and damp upholstery—a familiar scent that should be comforting but only makes my stomach turn harder.

Three hours. That's how long I sat in that interrogation room while Chen picked through my report with surgical precision, his questions cutting deeper than any blade. Where were you when Pylon Two went down? Can anyone verify your position? Why did your tactical weave spike so aggressively at 0247 hours?

I'd lied through my teeth, each word tasting like ash. Said I was coordinating evacuations. Managing containment protocols. Never mentioned Cole Blackwood. Never breathed a word about the shadow-man who'd appeared out of nowhere to drag me from certain death.

My hand trembles as I reach into my tactical belt, fingers closing around something solid and heavy that definitely wasn't there before. The object is warm against my palm, wrapped in layers of electrical tape that feel oddly deliberate—protective even. Carefully, I pull it free into the dim glow of the dashboard lights.

A transponder. No—not just any transponder. This is Gnome-forged, unmistakably Undercity tech. The soldering work is crude but brilliant, wires wound around a 2005 watch battery in a pattern that would make standard BATC engineers weep. Green insulation tape, copper wiring that catches the light like veins of gold. Someone assembled this by hand, probably in some sweatbox workshop beneath Newark's industrial sector.

*Cole.*

The realization hits me like a physical blow. He'd been there. At Pylon Two. In the chaos of the collapse, during those few seconds I thought I was dead—he must have slipped this onto me. A breadcrumb trail. An escape route.

I look down at the device, turning it over in my hands. There's no visible power indicator, but I can feel it—minute vibrations pulsing through the metal casing. Active signal.

My Sony MiniDisc player sits on the passenger seat where I left it, its green LCD screen glowing softly. I yank out the optical cable, my fingers working faster than my conscious thoughts. The port matches perfectly with the transponder's output jack. As soon as I plug them together, the player's display flashes: *SIGNAL DETECTED - ENCRYPTED FREQUENCY*.

Of course it's encrypted. Gnomes don't believe in making anything easy.

But I know their style. South Side frequency brokers use raw radio-telemetry to bypass BATC wards—they're too cheap for proper magical encryption, so they rely on complexity and obfuscation instead. It takes twelve minutes of manual key sequencing before the player cracks enough header information to spit out plaintext:

*PULSING SIGNAL: GPS COORDINATES [30.267N 97.743W] NEON HOLLOW SHIPYARDS PYLON THREE*

Pylon Three.

The words burn themselves into my retinas. That's not just any location—that's where this whole nightmare started. Where we first detected the breach in the Millennium Seal. Where Cole had been investigating before Pylon Two went dark.

I grab my Motorola Razr from the dashboard mount, thumb already moving across the keypad to check for active jammers. Clear. But that doesn't mean anything. Cole could be anywhere by now—or nowhere at all.

The Crown Vic's heater kicks on with a soft rumble, fogging the windows until the outside world dissolves into gray silence. Just me, this impossible device, and a set of coordinates pointing straight into the heart of what remains of our operation.

I think about Inquisitor Chen, about his questions, about how long before IA realizes I'm lying. How long before they notice inconsistencies in my report. The weave spike he'd mentioned—he knew something was wrong even if he couldn't prove it yet.

My tactical belt feels heavy around my waist. Standard gear: sidearm, spare magazines, flash-bang rounds, one canister each of iron dust and silver solution. Nothing special. Nothing that would explain why my weave is still reeking like charred wool despite two hours of meditation attempts.

*Because you're suppressing it,* whispers a voice in the back of my mind. *You've been suppressing it since you felt Cole touch your arm.*

I didn't want to believe it then. Don't want to believe it now. That kind of connection—it goes both ways. If Cole planted this transponder on me, if he's using Gnome tech to ping my position...

He knows exactly where I am too.

The MiniDisc player pulses another signal. Different frequency this time—not coordinates but text:

*MOVE NOW*

No explanation. No context. Just two words and an imperative that makes every instinct in my body scream *run*.

Through the fogged windshield, I can barely make out Pylon Two's location across Kearny Marshes—a dark silhouette against the pre-dawn sky where there used to be lights and life. Where forty-three agents lost their lives because we didn't know enough fast enough.

Pylon Three is waiting.

Cole is waiting.

And somewhere between here and there, answers—or death—are inevitable.

I start the Crown Victoria's engine.

## CHAPTER 3: THE VAULT & THE NOSEBLEED

The blast doors groan open like a throat clearing phlegm. I step into the belly of Sub-Station Seven and the air hits me first—a cocktail of industrial recirculation and something else, something my brain tries to process as "clean" but smells like wet charcoal soaked in ammonia. On the fire escape of the adjacent building, maybe twenty feet away through the rain-shrouded darkness, a Goblin splice-runner sits perched like some kind of gargoyle. His eyes catch what little light there is and throw it back, twin yellow disks set in a face that's too wide, grin too many teeth.

We make eye contact.

He could be here for any number of reasons. Could want to sell my position to the highest bidder. Could want to gut me for whatever tech I'm carrying. Could just be passing through and decided I was worth his attention.

I reach into my pocket with deliberate slowness, pulling out a silver cred-stick—the good stuff, government issued, traceable only if someone knows where to look. I hold it up between two fingers, making sure the reflectivity catches what little light exists.

The Goblin tilts his head, considering.

Then he holds out one clawed hand, palm up.

I toss it underhanded, an easy arc that even the wind doesn't manage to screw up completely. He snatches it from the air without taking his eyes off me, examines it for exactly one second, then pops it into his mouth like candy.

"Mmph," he says around the metal, "Vance."

My blood runs cold despite the warmth radiating from my chest. Despite knowing I should have expected this. Splice-runners don't just appear by accident. Someone sent him. Someone who knew I'd be here.

"Who?" My voice comes out sharper than I intend.

He grins wider, showing more teeth than should fit in any mouth. "Pretty please with sugar on top?"

Before I can respond—or decide whether responding is even smart—he drops backward off the fire escape and vanishes into the smog-choked alley below. Just... gone. Like he was never there at all.

Except now I know someone's watching. Someone close enough to track my movements through the storm.

The Nextel repeater crackles in my hands. Static. Then something else—a signal trying to break through the telluric interference that's been making comms a nightmare since midnight.

Green light blinks once. Twice.

Encrypted Black-Circuit frequency.

And then it goes solid.

Not yellow. Not red.

Solid green.

My heart hammers against my ribs hard enough to hurt as my wards spike bright enough to make everything around me glow white for just a second—the concrete, the rain droplets suspended mid-air, the blinking aviation beacon on its tower that suddenly seems much closer than it should be.

Cole's frequency.

It's Cole.

I press the receive button so hard my knuckle goes white.

* * *

The Nextel chirps—a harsh, digital intruder in the symphony of rain hammering against the rooftop. My hand fumbles for the device in my jacket pocket, fingers already slick with moisture and adrenaline. The plastic case is cold against my palm, the screen lit by that familiar sickly green glow.

"Vance."

Cole's voice cuts through the static like a razor blade through silk. Distorted. Compressed. Each word arrives wrapped in layers of interference—the lead shielding of the Old Mint below us, the telluric radiation bleeding from Pylon #4, the atmospheric conditions turning this into a nightmare of signal degradation.

"Commander." He corrects himself, and I can hear blood in his voice. Literal blood. The wet rasp of breath fighting past ruptured lung tissue.

I press myself harder against the HQ's access panel, my white-lilac wards flaring bright enough to cast dancing shadows across the concrete. They're screaming warnings now—urgent pulses of protection magic that taste like copper and ozone. But I ignore them. Need to hear this.

"The vault," Cole continues, another wet cough interrupting mid-sentence. "Under Pylon four. You were right about the—"

A heavy metallic thud echoes through the connection. Something large hitting something metal. Then hissing. Steam? No—it's him again, breathing through what sounds like a throat full of broken glass.

"Bring your MiniDisc. There aren't three of them, Little Halo."

My stomach drops. The Nextel nearly slips from my grip as my white-knuckled fingers lose their purchase on the casing. Rain runs down the screen in rivulets, distorting the display but not enough to hide the call status: ACTIVE - 2:37 REMAINING.

"There aren't three?" My thumb jabs at the push-to-talk button with enough force to crack plastic. "Cole, what do you mean there aren't three? Who is the third signature?"

The static swells. For one terrible moment I think he's gone, that the interference has swallowed him whole, dragged him down into that dead space between transmission and reception where signals go to die. My heart hammers against my ribs hard enough to hurt.

Then: "Not... person." His voice fragments across multiple frequencies, reassembling itself like shattered glass being pieced together by an amateur surgeon. "Corporate... entity."

I'm already moving before the words fully register, my boots slipping on the wet rooftop surface as I lunge for the access ladder. "Cole, stay with me! What corporate entity? Morvain Estate—"

Another sound. This one final. The distinctive click-clack of a handgun's slide releasing. The hollow echo of metal against stone. Then silence.

Complete silence except for the hiss of static.

"No no no NO—" My free hand slams against the Nextel's housing, as if brute force could resurrect a dead connection. The wards around me spike, flaring so bright they cast everything in harsh ultraviolet. The rain sizzles where it touches the magical field, turning to steam.

But Cole is gone.

I stare at the phone's display until my eyes burn. Until the rain has thoroughly soaked through every layer of clothing I'm wearing. Until my white-lilac wards have burned themselves out, fading to barely-there whispers of protection magic that smell like scorched lavender.

There aren't three signatures because the third wasn't a person.

The Morvain Estate isn't a house.

It never was.

Slowly, mechanically, I unfold the torn authorization document from 1999—the one we'd found in the Lincoln Tunnel maintenance shaft. The one with two clear signatures and one illegible smear of ink. Rainwater runs across its surface, smearing what little text remains visible.

The third signature wasn't a name.

It was a seal.

A corporate seal.

And underneath it, partially obscured by water damage and time: THE MORVAIN ESTATE HOLDING COMPANY - PROPERTY DEVELOPMENT DIVISION.

My hands are shaking now—not from cold or fear, but from the weight of revelation. Twenty-eight years. Twenty-eight years of hunting ghosts when we should have been hunting...

*Shit.*

The MiniDisc in my other pocket suddenly feels like a lead weight. All those signatures. Every single one we've collected since 1980. Cross-referenced against known syndicate operatives, confirmed metahuman assets, vetted contacts.

What if none of them were people?

What if they were all shells? Corporate entities designed to obscure ownership, to create plausible deniability, to wash money through layers of bureaucratic red tape so thick you couldn't tell where one organization ended and another began?

"Commander Vance." The voice makes me jump—Lieutenant Kowalski's face appears at the top of the access ladder, rain plastering his dark hair to his skull. "We have movement below. Multiple contacts approaching the Old Mint perimeter."

I look down at the Nextel still clutched in my hand. At the authorization document. At my reflection in the phone's dark screen—a ghost of myself standing in a storm that just got infinitely worse.

"There aren't three," I whisper to no one. To Cole, wherever he is. To the Syndicate, to the Morvain Estate, to whatever fresh hell is waiting for us inside that vault.

Then louder, speaking into the rain itself: "There never were."

I pocket both documents—the paper and the phone—and descend into BATC Headquarters. Behind me, the wards finally collapse entirely, their scent fading to nothing as the rooftop returns to ordinary darkness.

But I know better than that.

Nothing about tonight is ordinary anymore.

* * *

The door explodes inward with the crackle of police-grade breaching rounds.

I don't turn around. Don't break my lock on Pylon #4, where Cole's signature pulses like a dying heart—faint, erratic, bleeding out inside that godforsaken vault.

"BATC Security! Freeze!"

The voice is dwarf-timbered, used to being obeyed. I can hear the weight of their gear clanking—ceramic plates under tactical vests, sidearms unsnapping from holsters, combat boots crunching across wet gravel.

My white-lilac wards flare hotter, casting everything in that sickly lavender glow. The rain hammers down harder now, each droplet striking my skin like ice needles. But underneath it all, threaded through the tempest, I smell hearth-coals. *Warm*. *Desperate*.

Cole's fear.

"Commander Vance! Stand down!" The second guard, different voice—elf maybe, from the way they modulate frequency for maximum carry. "You are in violation of Section 7-A of the Aegis Protocol. Surrender your weapons and comply!"

I'm already running.

Not toward them. Away from everything—the safety rails, the proper exits, the goddamn rules that got us here in the first place. My boots slip once on the wet rooftop, gravel scattering, but I catch myself and push harder, legs burning as I sprint across forty stories of exposed concrete and neon-slicked danger.

Behind me, heavy footsteps give chase. Radios crackle. Someone's shouting into their comms: *"She's moving! Armed suspect—wait, she's not armed, she's—where the fuck is she going?!"*

The edge approaches fast. Too fast. Forty stories drops away into blackness punctuated by sodium-orange streetlamps and the occasional red flash of a traffic signal. Below, the Pulaski Skyway cuts through rain-soaked darkness, trucks rolling like slow-moving metal beetles along the elevated highway.

I don't stop.

"VANCE!" That dwarf again, closer now. Desperation creeping into his voice. "DON'T DO THIS!"

My wards detonate around me—a supernova of white-lilac light that sends the security team stumbling backward, hands raised against the glare. In that moment of blindness, I reach deep. Deeper than I've ever reached before. Every ounce of my weave, every thread of kinetic dampening I can spin, pouring it all into a cocoon around my body.

The Myocardial Geas throbs against my sternum—not pain exactly, more like pressure, a living thing beating in time with the telluric grid that runs beneath this whole rotten city.

And then there's nothing but air.

Wind screams past my ears, a physical force that tries to tear the breath from my lungs. The building falls away. Newark Bay spreads out below me, a sprawling dark mirror reflecting neon signs that blur into streaking ribbons—red, blue, orange, all bleeding together as terminal velocity kicks in.

My wards burn so bright they hurt to look at even for me. The mathematics cascade through my mind: trajectory angles, deceleration curves, impact vectors. It's beautiful and terrible all at once.

*"She's gone over! She actually jumped—the Commander just went over the edge!"* 

That elf's voice fades behind me as I fall, tumbling end-over-end through sheets of rain. Below, something moves. Something *fast*.

The freight train on the elevated tracks.

It emerges from beneath a bridge like a steel serpent cutting through black water, its boxcars stacked high, moving north toward Kearny. North toward the Old Mint where Cole is dying in darkness.

I adjust my vector—just slightly—but in freefall, slight means everything. The train rushes up to meet me, growing larger by the second. Boxcar. Bridge support. Another boxcar. A maintenance ladder.

"Please," I whisper to whatever gods might be listening. "Just this once."

My kinetic field flares impossibly bright.

Impact.

The world becomes white noise and violence. My bones scream. The kinetic dampening absorbs what it can—a shockwave that tears across the rooftop with enough force to shatter three streetlamps in a row—but physics is unforgiving. Even with magic, forty stories has weight. Has consequence.

When the light fades and I can see again, I'm lying across rusted metal, rain pelting down, train wheels clacking beneath me. Inside my mouth, copper. Taste of blood. But alive.

Alive and moving north.

I push myself upright, legs shaking, ribs definitely cracked somewhere under the left side. But my wards still burn steady, and Cole's signal—that desperate coal-scent of his fear—pulls me forward like gravity itself.

The Old Mint appears ahead, squatting against the night sky like a tombstone made of brick and despair. As the train carries me closer, I see the breach point. See the way the vault walls have been peeled open like an orange, raw concrete edges glowing faintly with residual weave.

And there, deep inside, barely perceptible but *there*:

Cole.

I don't wait for the next bridge. Don't wait for anything except momentum and rage and the bone-deep certainty that if I don't reach him right now, in this moment, I'll fail at the one thing that matters.

At 1:25 AM on November 16th, Commander Mara Vance leaps from a moving freight train into the heart of the Old Mint.

Ready to tear reality apart to save her partner.
