## CHAPTER 1: CHAPTER 1

I'm staring at the screen when it happens—the second prophecy bleeds through my thoughts like oil spreading across black water.

*Two Mothers Who Died Twice*

The words aren't written anywhere. They just *are*, coiled tight behind my sternum where the phantoms live. My fingers freeze over the Panasonic's trackpad, hovering above the stolen Morvain Estate schematics that should be my entire focus right now.

But they're not.

Can't be.

The amber streetlight cuts through the blinds in horizontal slats, painting everything in this sickly honey-gold. Outside, somewhere down on the South Side industrial sprawl, a car's subwoofer sends out its rhythmic thump-thump-thump—probably some late-night crew moving product between the abandoned warehouses. Normal sounds. Human sounds.

Except nothing's been normal since Sub-Station Seven.

My nose catches it again—that goddamn smell of wet charred wool. Ash-gray static crawls up from my collar, wrapping around my throat like a scarf made of ghosts. I force my breathing to cycle: four counts in, hold, four counts out. The white-lilac and cedar wards try to push back, but there are too many dead commuters in this memory. Three-hundred-and-fourteen souls screaming in the dark.

Three-hundred-and-fucking-fourteen.

I slam the laptop shut. The sudden darkness helps.

The MP5 sits on the workbench, field-stripped into its component parts. Bolt carrier group, upper receiver, lower receiver, trigger mechanism. I pick up the bolt assembly—the metal feels solid, real, *tangible*—and begin cleaning with Hoppe's No. 9. The chemical scent is sharp enough to cut through the phantom smells. I run the brush along the rails, watching black carbon residue fall away into the oil tray below.

*Mechanical. Precise. Real.*

Vane Thorne would smile that smile if he could see me now. The High-Spire Inquisitors don't care about mechanical precision or street-level reality. They operate in absolutes, wrapped in their tailored charcoal suits and that predatory gleam in their tapetum lucidum eyes. Thorne especially—always the perfect 2007 fashion, always three steps ahead, always ready to quote Elvish doctrine while the gutter-runners bleed out on pavement that doesn't give a shit about prophecies or purity or any of their sanctimonious bullshit.

Let them come.

The words feel true as I think them. My jaw sets. The suits don't bleed any different than the runners.

My hands move automatically through the familiar ritual: reassemble the weapon, check the headspace, test the trigger pull. Click. Smooth action. Good.

Back in the Toughbook. Morvain Estate spread across the screen in tactical overlays—heat signatures, pressure plates, sensor arrays. Three floors of fortified sprawl along the Passaic River where the water runs brown and thick with industrial runoff. Somewhere in those blueprints is the key to Prophecy #1.

*The First Mother*

At least that one made sense. Or seemed to. The Second Prophecy—*Two Mothers Who Died Twice*—that's the one that tastes like copper in my mouth. Like I'm remembering something I never lived.

The bass thumps again from outside, closer this time. Headlights sweep across the ceiling, then vanish into the industrial grid.

I lean back, rolling my shoulders against the tension there. Every muscle wants to coil tight, wants to prepare for violence before it even comes. That's the Aegis training. That's what they built me for.

But tonight isn't about what they built.

Tonight I'm going rogue with Cole because someone has to ask the questions Thorne won't let get voiced in his pristine High Spire offices. Someone has to dig through the ash and charred wool and find out why three-hundred-and-fourteen commuters had to burn.

Someone has to know if it was worth it.

The prophecy pulses again behind my ribs:

*Two Mothers Who Died Twice*

I pick up the cleaned MP5, feel its weight settle into my hands. Solid. Real. Ready.

"Let's see how deep this rabbit hole goes," I whisper to the empty safehouse.

Outside, the night breathes. Newark Bay waits beyond the marshlands, and somewhere out there, Cole's probably doing the same thing—checking gear, steeling himself, preparing to break every rule we swore to uphold.

We're both already dead men walking.

I just hope when we find the truth, whatever's left of us is worth saving.

* * *

The chalk scrapes against the exposed concrete pillar like fingernails on bone. I trace the first rune—a spiral descending into itself—and immediately taste copper in my mouth.

My breath fogs in the drafty safehouse. November cold seeps through the industrial district's porous walls, but that's not why my hands shake. The piece of chalk feels slippery in my grip, coated now with a fine gray powder that isn't from the concrete.

*Ash-gray static.*

I close my eyes. Breathe through my nose. Count backward from ten. But the smell hits me anyway—that phantom stench of wet charred wool mixing with burned hair, with *flesh*, with three hundred and fourteen commuters who never made it out of Sub-Station Seven alive. My lungs burn. The chalk drops from suddenly numb fingers, clattering across the floor.

"Fuck," I whisper to the empty room.

This is happening again. Psychogenic bleed, straight out of the textbook my therapist at Bellevue tried to make me read during those mandatory sessions after the Breach. Three years later and my mind still rewinds to that moment—the screeching metal, the screaming that went silent too fast, the way the thermal wave hit my face even from half a mile away.

I press my palm flat against the cold concrete. Feel the grain of it beneath my skin. Real. Present. Now.

The geometric sequence glows faintly where I've already marked it—my mother's maiden sector code, passed down through generations of Vances who actually knew what they were doing with their gifts instead of just muddling through like I do. Each transit-rune connects to the last, forming pathways through the layered reality of North Jersey's threshold zones.

I pick up the chalk again. Continue tracing.

"This is insane," I mutter, my voice carrying that flat South Side drawl my mom never quite lost even after forty years in Newark. "You're drawing secret knock patterns for ghosts while the whole fucking system's about to go sideways."

But it's necessary. The Sector Code creates a localized anchor point—a psychic safehouse within the safehouse, if you will. If midnight hits and things go south, this pattern will give me a fallback position, a way to drag consciousness back to solid ground if my tether gets severed.

My phone buzzes. I snatch it off the makeshift workbench—an old door propped on cinder blocks—and flip open the Motorola Razr with a sharp *clack*. 

No new messages. Just the time: 22:05 HRS.

Cole should be gearing up by now in his chop-shop across town. I picture him there, surrounded by salvaged electronics and jury-rigged arcane hardware, probably muttering blessings in some dead language he half-remembers from his grandmother's stories. The thought makes something in my chest loosen fractionally.

"Hold the line, ma," I whisper to the concrete pillar, addressing the runes as if they're listening—or as if my mother somehow embedded her own will into these shapes before she died. "Just a few more hours and we cut the head off this snake."

The ash-gray static sparks at my fingertips. My vision blurs. The smell intensifies.

*Breathe.*

I can't do this alone. Can't carry the weight of what's coming without fragmenting completely. The memory rises unbidden: Cole Reyes's hand closing around mine during that training exercise last month, his calloused thumb brushing over my knuckles while the simulation engines pushed us both toward collapse. The contact had grounded me then. Had pulled me back from the edge when the phantom smells were already overwhelming everything else.

I let myself remember it fully now—intentionally, deliberately. His warmth against my cold fingers. The way his pulse matched mine until our heartbeats synchronized.

The effect is immediate and visceral.

Frost-cobalt light blooms behind my closed eyelids, cool and clean and cutting through the gray static like a knife. When I open my eyes again, the safehouse has changed. Where there was that sickening stench of wet charred wool, now rises the rich, grounding aroma of warm hearth-coals burning low and steady. Not hot enough to hurt. Just enough to remind you that fire means life when you've seen too much death.

The ash-gray static dissolves. My hands steady.

I finish the sector code with practiced strokes, each rune falling into place with satisfying certainty. As I complete the final transit symbol, faint blue-black iron-gall residue flakes away from the chalk marks—the material world rejecting the arcane geometry, unable to fully contain what I'm weaving into its fabric.

"Better," I say quietly. "That's better."

But even as I speak, I know this is just the beginning. Twelve hours until midnight. Twelve hours until whatever's been festering in the Passaic River since 1987 decides whether North Jersey survives or becomes another cautionary tale whispered in threshold zones from here to Weehawken.

I drop the spent chalk onto the floor and check my gear one more time: Aegis-issued sidearm (fully loaded), backup ammunition clipped to my belt, the obsidian focus stone my mother left me tucked into an inside pocket where it sits heavy and dark against my ribs.

All present and accounted for.

The concrete pillar hums softly now, the sector code settled into its structure like roots finding purchase in stone. I press my forehead against it, feel the slight vibration that means it's holding.

"Twelve hours," I repeat, testing the words like a prayer or a curse—maybe both. "Just gotta make it twelve more hours."

* * *

I strap the last magazine into my vest pouch, feeling the familiar weight settle against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Silver-jacketed hollow points—standard issue for what we're dealing with now. Not that standards mean much anymore.

The harmonic dampener clicks into place on my belt, its low vibration thrumming through the fabric of my cargo pants. Good. The tech's holding. For now.

Chalk dust coats my palms, white and fine as bone powder. I wipe it across my tactical pants, watching the residue fall away in lazy spirals. The air around me shimmers with white-lilac light, steady and clean—not the corrupted mess it was an hour ago. Though if I let my guard down, even for a second, I can still smell that ash-gray static. Wet charcoal. Burnt wool.

*Sub-Station Seven.*

Three hundred fourteen commuters, turned to cinders by something that shouldn't exist outside of nightmare fuel.

I shake my head hard, dispersing the thought before it can take root. The frost-cobalt pulse comes automatically—that stabilizing warmth Cole's presence leaves behind, even when he's not here. Even when we're both about to walk into separate versions of hell tonight.

My keys jingle as I snag them from the table. Crown Vic's waiting downstairs, full tank, ammo cached in the trunk. Standard prep. Professional.

The Nextel chirps.

I freeze mid-turn, hand hovering over the walkie-talkie clipped to my belt. That harsh, electronic *chirp-chirp* cuts through the safehouse silence like a car alarm in a graveyard.

"Vance, you on the channel?"

Torin Kaelen. Torque. His voice rumbles through the speaker—gravel and gravelly machinery, the way it always does when he's working the deep excavations beneath Newark Bay. I can hear it in his tone: the whine of drills, the groan of earth-moving equipment, the metallic scrape of gold-bismuth ore being sorted and shaped into collars for the really nasty things we've been pulling out of the Passaic River lately.

"Yeah, Torque. Talk to me."

I press the push-to-talk button, thumb holding it down with practiced ease. The Nextel crackles with interference, shadows of conversations bleeding through from other channels—the BATC grid alive and buzzing even at this hour.

"The kids are awake," Torque says. His voice softens, just barely. Enough that I notice it. He's a dwarf who spends twelve-hour shifts in the dark, shaping metal with his bare hands, but something about those words makes him sound almost... gentle.

"They know you're going off-grid tonight." A pause. Machinery hums louder in the background. "You better get down here."

My chest tightens. Not from fear—from something worse. Responsibility. The weight of promises made to children who've already seen too much. Who've lost too much.

The Nextel clicks back into sleep mode. I unclip it slowly, running my thumb over the rubberized surface. Downstairs, past the industrial grime of the South Side, past the rusted girders and abandoned warehouses, Torque is waiting with the only thing keeping us human right now.

I kill the safehouse lights.

Darkness floods the room, absolute except for one detail—the faint blue-black sizzle still clinging to the concrete pillar where my mother's sector code burned itself into existence. That glowing script, counting down. Always counting.

To what? To when? To whether any of us make it through what's coming.

The safehouse door groans as I pull it open.

## CHAPTER 2: CHAPTER 2

The staircase groans beneath my boots, each step down another descent into the earth's dark memory. Rust flakes like orange snow, catching what little light bleeds from the surface world above. My hand never leaves the rail.

"Watch your footing on the lower landing, Commander," Ramos warned from three steps above me, his heavy tactical flashlight throwing a jaundiced yellow beam through the falling rust. "The salt damp has eaten right through the stringers. One wrong step and you're taking a thirty-foot bath in raw drainage."

"I've swum in worse than Passaic runoff, Sergeant," I said without looking back, keeping one hand braced against the cold brick wall. "Check the telluric meter on your chest harness. What's the ambient reading?"

"Forty-two millivolts and climbing," Ramos answered, the faint ticking of the needle audible over the drip of condensation. "Whatever Morvain's running down here, it's drawing off the main grid. The whole foundation is vibrating."

"It's not drawing from the grid," Cole muttered from the top of the stairwell, his silhouette dark against the drizzle outside. "It's feeding into it. The bastard's running an illegal siphon. Keep moving before their maintenance crew does their midnight rounds."—forty-three steps total, I've counted them before, though counting doesn't make returning here any easier.

The air hits me first. Always does. Ozone sharp enough to taste, mixing with decades of accumulated dust and that particular smell of desperate human habitation. Someone's cooking something in a hotplate—cabbage maybe, or worse—and underneath it all runs the metallic tang of the Vault itself. Concrete and rebar and fear, old fear baked into the walls like sedimentary rock.

I pause halfway down. Let my lungs adjust. The white-lilac begins its slow crawl up my spine, that familiar warmth spreading through my shoulders and chest. It wants to bloom, wants to push outward with full strength, but I rein it in. Not yet. Never scare them with power when they need safety most.

Thirty-eight souls down there. Thirty-eight ghosts walking in children's bodies.

I take the last seven steps slower now, deliberately softening my footsteps, altering my posture even before I clear the stairwell. Shoulders relaxed instead of squared. Hands visible and open at my sides instead of ready at belt level. The tactical mind stays sharp—it always does—but the armor goes away. Can't protect these kids if they can't see past the uniform to the person underneath.

They're clustered mostly on the far side, near the makeshift sleeping quarters someone's cobbled together from salvaged couches and military-issue cots. The sodium lighting casts everything in that sickly yellow-gold, making shadows pool too deep beneath their eyes, in the hollows of their cheeks. I count faces as I move forward: twelve humans, eight Elves, nine Dwarves, nine Orcs. All different. All the same now.

A young Dwarven girl—Maya, her name tag says, though she's outgrown it twice over—peels away from the group. Her braids have come loose, dark hair hanging wild around a face still streaked with dust from this morning's evacuation. She tugs at my cargo pants, fingers small enough that I feel the trembling through the fabric.

"Are the suits coming back, Commander?" Her voice cracks on the question, barely more than a whisper against the drip-drip-drip of condensation from the exposed pipes overhead.

"No," I say immediately, kneeling until we're eye-to-eye. "Not tonight."

My hand settles gently on her shoulder—not grabbing, not controlling, just there. Present. Real. "Tonight, I'm going to them."

Her dark eyes search mine, looking for lies or reassurance or maybe both. "Promise?"

I should lie. Should tell her something soothing about how everything will be fine, how the adults will handle it all. But I'm done lying to children who've already seen too much truth.

"I can't promise forever," I tell her instead, my voice low, grounded, no mystical inflection whatsoever—just one cop talking straight to a scared kid on what used to be the South Side before the Breach redrew every map. "But I promise tonight. And tomorrow night, if you need me here again, I'll be here. That's what I do."

She considers this with the seriousness of someone too old for their years. Then nods once, sharp and certain, and returns to her corner where three other Dwarven kids wait. They huddle together like birds making themselves smaller in the face of something vast and hungry.

The white-lilac aura pulses outward now, gentle as breath, washing over the entire Vault in waves of cedar warmth and hearth-coal comfort. It finds the terrified Elven boy hiding behind an overturned milk crate—the one whose parents didn't make it out, whose screams I'd heard through three city blocks—and wraps around him like a blanket. His bio-wards are gray-trauma saturated, ash-colored and brittle, but my aura moves through his defenses softly, giving his exhausted systems permission to rest without letting go of vigilance entirely.

Someone has a Sony MiniDisc player going in the corner—tinny hip-hop from 2003 bleeding through the speakers, some artist I vaguely recognize but can't name. The bass is completely absent, just mids and highs fighting against the ambient noise of the Vault. A teenager with headphones around his neck keeps the volume low, considerate despite everything. Respectable.

I move deeper into the space, acknowledging each cluster with a nod or brief eye contact, letting them know they're seen without overwhelming anyone. The humans gather near the makeshift kitchen area, two mothers sharing a thermos of something that smells more like coffee than it probably is. An older Orc woman sitting alone on the edge of a cot, rocking slightly, staring at nothing. Her son died in Sub-Station Seven. I remember her testimony from the investigation, remember the precise way she described watching him burn.

"Mrs. Kesh," I say quietly as I approach. "Can I sit?"

She blinks up at me, recognition slow to surface. When I lower myself beside her, she rocks faster for a moment before settling back into rhythm. "They said you found him."

"My team did." Not me personally—I was three blocks away coordinating air support—but close enough that the distinction matters less than what she needs to hear. "He didn't suffer long."

A lie. Maybe. The forensic data suggested minutes but felt like hours even compressed. But I'm learning that grief doesn't need accuracy, only shape. Something to hold onto when everything else dissolves.

Above us, a fluorescent tube flickers once, twice, then dies completely. Someone should replace it—the dying light casts worse shadows than steady dimness would—but no one moves. We've all gotten used to the decay. The Vault was never meant to be permanent, just survival until better options emerged. Three years later, it's still here, still necessary, and there are nights I wonder if we'll ever truly escape its gravity.

The white-lilac aura pulses brighter now, pushing harder against the accumulated trauma weighing down this space. Cedar scent mixing with ozone. Hearth-coal warmth fighting dust and fear. It won't fix anything—can't bring back the dead or unbreak broken children—but it gives them temporary shelter from the worst of it.

Tomorrow I'll go back above ground. Tomorrow I'll face the Syndicate suits who let Sub-Station Seven happen, who prioritized profit margins over safety protocols, whose negligence turned a Tuesday morning shift change into an inferno that consumed forty-seven souls including thirty-eight children's parents.

But tonight is for the ghosts. Tonight is for bearing witness to their survival.

* * *

I kneel beside them on the cold concrete floor, my knee joints protesting slightly as they always do after too many hours in tactical stance. The bunker reeks of damp earth and old fear—something you get used to when you run black-site operations beneath union halls in the Kearny Marshes.

Jax is maybe seventeen, if that. Hard to tell with Orcs sometimes—they mature fast or they don't make it past fourteen. His skin has that characteristic leathery texture, rough as sanded oak, and I can see where the Syndicate's runners got careless with their brand. The faint green glow of the Geas mark pulses just below his wrist, a sickly luminescence that makes my teeth ache.

"Burns less when you do that," he grunts through his lower tusks. His voice carries the flat affect of someone who's learned pain is temporary but debt is forever. South Side accent, probably from over near Newark Bay where the waterfront docks still operate under pre-Concordance rules.

Beside him, the Razor-Wire elf can't be more than twelve. Her ears are decorated with safety-pins—cheap street jewelry that catches the dim red emergency lighting strung across the ceiling beams. Her tapetum lucidum eyes reflect the light back at me like a cat's, yellow-gold and wary.

I'm already pulling off my tactical gloves, fingers working the Velcro releases without looking. The fabric peels away revealing my bare hands, and immediately the white-lilac aura flares to life around my fingertips—not from intent but from necessity. The children need calm right now more than anything else.

Fresh cedar floods the space. Warm hearth-coals.

It's all I can do to keep the ash-gray trauma from bleeding through because god help me, I remember what this feels like. Remember standing in a basement somewhere down near the Passaic River watching my own sister's brand pulse while some Syndicate runner explained how much she was worth on the arcane black market.

My hand hovers above Jax's forearm. "This will hurt worse before it gets better."

"Lady, everything hurts worse before it gets better." He doesn't pull away though. Just stares past me at the far wall like there might be something interesting painted on the concrete blocks.

I place my palm against his skin.

The moment contact makes, frost-cobalt warmth floods out—not mine, not from any magic I've trained with at Aegis Command. That's Cole's imprint, lingering psychic residue from when he touched these kids earlier. The Butcher has... peculiar talents when it comes to pain transference. Nasty business usually, but right now it means temporary relief for two terrified metahuman teenagers who've been property since they could walk.

Jax's breath hisses between his tusks. The green glow of his brand flickers, dimming slightly as my aura pushes against it—white-lilac suppression protocols kicking into high gear. Not enough to break the tether. Never enough for that without hitting the master console itself.

But enough to give him a few hours' respite from whatever hell the Syndicate's coding inflicts on its bonded assets.

"You're going to cut it," the elf girl says. Statement, not question. Her voice is surprisingly steady for someone so young. "Going to the Morvain Estate tonight. My cousin works security there—before they slot her last week. Said you were coming."

"I'm going to shatter their master console," I tell her, meeting those yellow-gold eyes dead-on because children need truth even when adults can't handle it. "Cole and I are making the approach through the Pulaski Skyway tunnels around midnight. By dawn, every Geas mark in this district goes dark permanently."

"By dawn." Jax repeats it like he's testing how the words feel in his mouth. Testing whether promises mean anything anymore.

"They better not miss, Vance." The elf's name tag reads 'Kira' in faded permanent marker scrawled by someone with terrible handwriting. Her ears twitch toward the sound of distant sirens—police or something else, hard to say from down here. "They'll slot us all if you do. Everyone in Local 405. Everyone at the Union Hall. Whole building gets red-flagged."

I don't flinch at the threat. Don't show fear because children learn what adults tolerate.

My hand moves from Jax's arm to Kira's wrist where an identical brand pulses sickly green against her pale skin. Same rough treatment as the boy's—a syndicate runner who didn't care about collateral damage while applying corporate property marks.

The moment my fingers touch her forearm, ozone sharpness explodes across my senses. That unique smell of electricity and magic fighting each other like rival gangs over territory rights.

"Then we'd better not fucking miss," I say quietly.

And make a vow to these kids that I'll be breaking before sunrise comes to North Jersey: I am going into hell itself if that's what it takes.

* * *

I stand up slowly, my knees protesting after too long kneeling beside those terrified children. The white-lilac of my aura hangs in the air like morning fog, still clinging to them, still whispering promises that everything will be alright even though I know better than to make such promises.

The vault's interior lights cast harsh shadows across the assembled families—twenty-seven souls total, crammed into a space meant for emergency supplies and union archives. Some have pulled blankets over their heads. Others stare at nothing, eyes hollow. A toddler begins to cry somewhere in the darkness, quickly shushed by a parent whose hands are probably shaking.

I ignore the pull to comfort them again. Every minute I linger here weakens what needs doing tonight.

Fidget sits hunched on an overturned crate near the vault entrance, his gnarled fingers dancing across a modified PalmPilot, stylus clicking against plastic in rapid-fire succession. The Gnome barely reaches four feet tall, his coat patched and re-patched until it's more duct tape than fabric. Years of tinkering in cramped server rooms have left him perpetually stooped, shoulders curved inward like parentheses around his chest.

"Got the frequencies masked, Commander," he squeaks without looking up, his voice like gravel tumbling through a pipe. "Bouncing our telemetry off three different cell towers downtown. The High-Spire won't see a blip."

His workspace is cluttered with soldering irons, multicolored wires snaking across the concrete floor, and three half-empty coffee cups that smell stale enough to be considered hazardous waste. A pack of Marlboros peeks out from his breast pocket—they'll need changing soon if the smoke-stain pattern on his yellowed fingertips is any indication.

I slip my tactical gloves back on, feeling the synthetic leather mold to each knuckle. "Keep them safe, Fidget. Nobody gets in."

"Not even if they come with federal warrants?" His dark eyes finally flick toward me, sharp despite the perpetual redness that comes from inhaling too much solder smoke over too many decades.

"Especially then." I move closer to examine his setup—three separate laptops running parallel intrusion detection systems, all wired directly into the bunker's localized defense grid. If someone tries to breach this vault, he'll know before their boots hit the stairs.

Fidget nods once, short and definite. He taps something else into his PalmPilot, and I hear the heavy, echoing *thud* of the vault's deadbolts sliding into place. Just a test. The sound reverberates through the concrete chamber like a death knell.

"Four hours," I tell him, checking my watch. "If I don't check in by 04:00 HRS, you scramble the frequencies, lock this vault from the inside, and initiate Local 405 evacuation protocol. Understand?"

"Understood." He doesn't ask where I'm going or what I'm doing. Good. That would mean I chose poorly when selecting ward-crackers.

Behind us, one of the children begins to sob quietly. A woman's voice hushes them again.

I turn to look at the digital clock mounted on the concrete wall. The red LED numbers flip to 23:15 HRS.

Outside, rain has begun to fall harder against the ventilation grates. I can hear it now—a rhythmic drumming that promises no letup before dawn. The storm is moving in from the east, pushing across Newark Bay with the kind of force that turns streets into rivers and floods the Kearny Marshes until they remember what they were before civilization tried to tame them.

It is time to head to the pines. Time to meet the Butcher.

I move toward the vault entrance, but pause at the threshold, looking back at Fidget one last time. "You know what happens if I fail."

His fingers continue their dance across the keyboard. "Then these people die anyway. No sense borrowing trouble."

Wise old bastard. I follow the stairs up through darkness that smells of rust and decay, each step taking me further from safety and closer to whatever fresh hell awaits beneath the Pulaski Skyway.

The night swallows me whole.

## CHAPTER 3: CHAPTER 3

The pine forest breathes around me as I move through it, each step deliberate and silent despite the squelching mud that tries to suck my combat boots down into the saturated earth.

"Keep your head down, Cole," I whispered over the tactical frequency, clicking the transmit switch twice. "Thermal scope on the perimeter tower just swept the tree line. Two seconds on, five seconds off."

"I see 'em," Cole's rasp came back through the earpiece, low and gravelly, accompanied by the wet squelch of his boots behind me. "Old Westinghouse mercury-arc lamps. They've got blind spots wide enough to drive a flatbed through if you hug the ditch."

"We aren't driving a flatbed," I replied, checking the bismuth charge in my sidearm. "We're cutting the telluric feed to their main gate before the midnight shift changes. How are the grounding straps?"

"Holding at four ohms," he said. "Cold-iron wire's biting into my wrists, but it beats having my marrow cooked if we trip a subterranean lead. Move on my mark. Three... two... go." Rain isn't just falling—it's attacking, driving in horizontal sheets under the low-hanging clouds that have swallowed what little night there should be at this hour.

My tactical gear is already soaked through. Water beads off my canvas jacket like oil on water, but my face feels like someone's pressing ice needles into my skin, again and again. The cold seeps past every layer. November in North Jersey has teeth, and they're biting hard tonight.

Somewhere ahead, beyond the veil of rain and darkness, the Morvain Estate sprawls across acres of prime North District real estate. The perimeter wall catches sodium-vapor light from within—a sickly orange-yellow glow that makes everything look infected, diseased. Those lights pulse in rhythm with Telluric generators humming somewhere deep below ground. Old money, old magic, and older secrets all wrapped up in brick and mortar.

I don't need those lights though. Not really.

The 4th Law pulls at something deep in my chest, a faint tugging sensation that cuts through the white noise of the storm. It's like following a thread through a labyrinth, invisible except to those who know how to look for it. Cole Reyes bleeds frost-cobalt energy when he's killing. Right now, that thread feels different—warmer, less predatory. Still dangerous. Always dangerous where he's concerned.

A shadow detaches itself from a massive pine trunk maybe fifteen meters ahead.

My hand doesn't go to my weapon. Instead, I adjust my grip on my sidearm while keeping both hands visible as I step forward into the open space between trees. The rain hammers down harder here, the pine canopy offering no shelter. My breath comes out in steamy puffs.

"Commander Vance." His voice carries easily over the storm's percussion, low and rough-edged. "You're late."

"Not by much," I reply, matching his tone. "Traffic was shit on the Pulaski Skyway."

That's a lie. There was no traffic tonight. Hardly anyone drives after midnight anymore, not with the things that hunt the highways after dark. But I needed time to think, to prepare myself for this meeting.

Cole emerges fully from the shadows, and the cold disappears.

Not gradually—it vanishes. One moment I'm freezing, my muscles tensing against the bite of November rain. The next, warmth floods through me like someone just opened an oven door. It smells like burning wood and hot iron, with undertones of cedar and something else I can't quite place. Something wild and primal.

He's wearing a grease-stained mechanic's jacket over dark tactical pants, his pump-action shotgun slung across his back. Water cascades off the brim of his baseball cap, but his dark eyes are dry, locked onto mine with an intensity that makes my chest tighten.

There's no frost-cobalt bleeding off him right now—not the dead-silent, zero-remorse energy he reserves for when he's working. What I'm seeing instead is white-lilac, warm and almost gentle compared to what he usually projects. Still dangerous, still Cole Reyes, but… different.

"You look like hell, Commander," he says, taking a step closer.
