## CHAPTER 1: THE FINAL MINUTES OF THE OLD WORLD

The sodium-vapor lights turn everything the color of old bruises. I can feel the vibration of the rig's engine through my boots, a deep thrumming that travels up my spine and settles behind my sternum. The cold from outside seeps in around the door seals, making my breath fog in small clouds every time I exhale.

Leo lays on the horn—that guttural, mechanical bellow that cuts through the New Year's Eve chaos outside. We're barely moving, maybe two feet forward every thirty seconds. Through the frost-trimmed windshield, I can see shapes stumbling between our rig and the intersection ahead. Silhouettes of partygoers who've forgotten what a traffic light means, or don't care.

"The fuck is this shit?" Leo mutters, tapping the brakes in a useless rhythm. His voice carries that flat, nasal quality that comes from twenty years breathing Newark air. "Yuppie piece-of-shit tourists and local drunkards think they own the goddamn street."

I don't respond. Instead, I'm checking my jump bag for the third time in ten minutes—a nervous tic I picked up during my first month on the job. Trauma shears, still wrapped. Syringe kit. IV start kits, both 14-gauge and 16. Tourniquet. Oropharyngeal airways, sizes 3 through 5. Everything where it should be, exactly as it was five minutes ago, exactly as it will be five minutes from now unless we actually get a call worth a damn.

The radio crackles with the night's usual litany: 278-E at the Riviera Motel—probable alcohol poisoning. 291-J downtown—overdose, possible fentanyl. A car accident on Route 22, three ambulances already on scene. The dispatcher's voice is bored, professional, reading off addresses like she's ordering groceries.

"Rig 42, Rig 42, dispatch."

Leo reaches for the mic, but I beat him to it. "Dispatch, this is 42. Go ahead."

"Cardiac arrest, possible MI. Club Zanzibar, 341 South Orange Avenue. Multiple bystanders performing CPR. Priority one. ETA?"

I glance at the GPS display mounted above the radio. Three blocks. Maybe four if these assholes don't move their cars soon.

"We're two minutes out," I say. "Update us on patient status."

"Last report, patient down approximately six minutes. Consciousness unknown. Bystanders initiated compression protocol."

Six minutes. The magic window is closing fast. I feel my pulse quicken despite years of training designed to suppress exactly that reaction.

"Copy that." I release the mic back into its cradle and look at Leo. His eyes meet mine in the rearview mirror's reflection, then shift forward again. He doesn't need me to tell him what I'm thinking.

"We got a shot?" he asks.

"Their CPR might've bought us something." I lean forward, bracing my hand against the bulkhead as we lurch forward another foot. Then another. The sodium-vapor lights smear across the windows in amber streaks.

Leo works the wheel, easing right, trying to find a gap between the double-parked sedans and the cluster of people dancing—actually *dancing*—in the middle of the street. Someone's blasting techno from an open car door. Glass bottles clink against asphalt. A woman laughs, high and sharp enough to cut through the engine noise.

"Move your asses!" Leo shouts through the open window, but nobody hears or cares. He taps the horn again, longer this time. The diesel engine grumbles in protest.

Then, finally, a gap opens. Some drunk stumbles sideways. Another couple pushes their way toward the sidewalk. Leo doesn't hesitate. He drops the rig into gear—that grinding, metallic whine—and we surge forward with enough force to rock the whole vehicle on its suspension.

The radio crackles again: "42, patient now unresponsive. Compressions ongoing but weakening. Need you there yesterday."

My hand finds the handle above the door. "One minute out," I call to Leo.

He nods, jaw tight, knuckles white on the steering wheel. We're flying now, relatively speaking. Forty miles per hour through streets meant for half that speed when they're clear, which is never. The sodium-vapor glow becomes continuous instead of intermittent, washing over everything in that sickly orange light that makes everyone look like they've been dead for a few days already.

Club Zanzibar comes into view around the corner—a three-story brick building wrapped in neon pink and electric blue letters spelling out its name in a font designed by someone who'd never met a curve they didn't want to turn into a hard angle. Bouncers in matching black shirts are trying to control the crowd spilling onto the sidewalk.

And then I see him.

A man face-down on the pavement, three people taking turns compressing his chest while a fourth one tilts his head back, pinching his nose. His shirt is dark with blood—either his or someone else's, impossible to tell from here—but he's not moving except for the mechanical motion being forced into him.

Leo cranks the wheel hard left. We skid to a stop behind an idling taxi, close enough that I can hear the music thumping from inside the club even through the rig's insulation. Close enough that when I throw open the side door and drop to the pavement, my boots hit frozen mud that squelches between the cracks in the asphalt.

The air hits me like a slap—cold, wet, thick with exhaust fumes and the sharp chemical smell of spilled beer. My breath comes out in clouds as I run toward the scene, jump bag slung over my shoulder, the cold biting at every inch of exposed skin despite the heavy parka.

"Step back!" I'm shouting before I fully register what I'm seeing. "Let me through!"

The woman doing compression looks up, eyes wide, mascara running down her cheeks. Her hands are red with the man's blood—or maybe just from pressing so hard against his chest.

"He was dancing," she says, voice breaking. "He just... stopped."

I kneel beside him without responding, fingers finding his carotid artery. Nothing. I press harder, sliding two fingers deeper along the neck's curve. Still nothing. No pulse. No rhythm. Just the flat line of a heart that's forgotten how to beat.

"Time?" I snap, looking at Leo who's crouched on the other side of the patient, already setting up our portable monitor.

"Eleven-fifty-eight," he says. "We got maybe thirty seconds to get him on board if we're gonna try anything here."

I don't answer. Instead, I'm already working, checking for airway obstruction while Leo tears open the trauma package. The man's face is gray, lips blue, pupils blown wide and unreactive. This isn't going to be pretty.

"Bag-valve-mask!" I call out.

* * *

I slam the side door of Rig 42 open before Leo even fully stops rolling. The street reeks of diesel fumes and melted rubber—the usual New Year's Eve chaos of South Newark, but worse tonight. Every other car on 4th Street is honking, trapped in a parking lot of humanity trying to get somewhere that doesn't matter anymore.

"Male, mid-forties, collapsed outside the bodega," Dispatch crackles through my headset. I'm already moving, boots hitting pavement, the cold December air burning in my lungs.

The patient is face-down between a pile of empty beer cases and a puddle of something I don't have time to identify. I drop to one knee beside him, gloves already snapping on—sweaty nitrile that'll probably tear by shift's end. Two fingers to his neck. Nothing. Thumb pressed hard against the carotid artery. Still nothing.

"Unresponsive, no pulse, apneic," I snap into the radio. My partner appears at my shoulder, already pulling out the Stryker stretcher.

We work like we've done this a thousand times because we have. Leo gets the board while I perform the rapid assessment—no obvious trauma, chest rise absent, skin already showing that waxy pallor that means circulation's failing fast. Mid-forties guy in a stained t-shirt and jeans, probably celebrating the millennium wrong, now celebrating nothing at all.

"Asystole," I mutter, pressing two hands to his sternum. "Start compressions."

Leo takes position while I grab the LifePak from our kit. My fingers are already slick with sweat inside the gloves as I rip open the packaging, slap the pads onto his bare chest—first the upper right, then the lower left, positioning them just above where his heart should be fighting to keep him alive. The machine whines its initialization sequence, that high-pitched electronic moan that never sounds hopeful.

"Charging," I announce, though nobody needs to hear it. We're alone here except for the drunk who called 911 stumbling back into the bodega, probably more concerned about whether we'll catch him for public intoxication than whether this man lives or dies.

I press the shock button.

His body jerks—that violent arch of electromyographic tetany—but when I check the rhythm again, the screen shows what I already feel in my gut: ventricular fibrillation. Chaotic, quivering chaos where organized electrical activity should be.

"Epi, one milligram push!" I bark, already recalculating. Epinephrine first, then another shock if we're lucky. Behind us, Leo's secured the patient to the stretcher, and together we lift—counting down the move, swinging him up and sliding him into the back of Rig 42.

The moment the doors close, I'm on him again, compressions hard and fast—two inches depth, hundred beats a minute, allowing recoil like my instructor screamed at me during certification. The ambulance rocks as Leo cranks the engine, and we lurch forward with a violent jolt that throws me against the cabinet. My knee cracks into metal, but I don't stop compressing.

"One of epi going in now!" Leo calls from his position near the medication drawer.

The defibrillator charges again. "He's still in V-fib, charging to two-hundred!" I shout over the siren building outside—Leo's hit the lights and we're tearing through traffic that parts before us like the Red Sea, though not nearly fast enough. County General is six miles away through these streets, and every intersection is a nightmare of gridlocked celebration.

*Clear!*

Another jerk. Another check. Still V-fib.

My chest burns from leaning over him, my arms screaming from compressions. The ambulance smells like burnt hair—that's the conductive gel on the defibrillator pads—and underneath it all, the acrid tang of fear sweat. Mine or his or both of ours, doesn't matter.

"Leo, how far?!"

"Fourth Street's total fucking gridlock! Pulaski Skyway's backed up past Kearny! We aren't gonna make it to County, Mara!"

I don't answer. Can't answer. There's nothing but this rhythm—compressions, checking, charging, shocking—and the clock on the dashboard that I can see through the gap between seats: 11:58 PM.

The ECG monitor suddenly changes its tone. That chaotic squiggle smooths out, flattens. A solid line appears across the screen.

Flatline.

A shrieking alarm fills the back of Rig 42 just as the dashboard clock clicks forward:

**11:59 PM**

"I've got asystole!" I scream into the chaos, even though Leo can hear the monitor's shrill cry over everything else. My hands are already moving—the compressions harder now, desperate—but I know what this means. Asystole after V-fib. No organized rhythm to rescue. Just cellular death spreading outward in waves.

Behind us, the city lights paint the passing buildings in red-and-white strobes. Somewhere out there, people are counting down to midnight, celebrating the end of one millennium and the beginning of another. They're kissing strangers, popping champagne, making wishes they'll actually get to keep.

In here, in this metal box racing toward a hospital we'll never reach in time, a man who had a whole new century ahead of him is flatlining before my eyes while I crush his sternum with my palms and pray to gods I stopped believing in years ago.

"Leo," I say quietly, still compressing. "He's gone."

* * *

I'm pressing down hard on the chest cavity, feeling cartilage crack under my palms. The ribcage gives way like wet wood. My whole body leans into each compression—fifteen per minute, I count in my head, but really it's just muscle memory now, pure primal rhythm.

"Come on," I breathe out between compressions. "Don't you fucking die on me."

The patient—a construction worker who'd fallen through scaffolding at the new apartment complex near Newark Bay—has been flatlining for three minutes. Three minutes since his heart stopped, three minutes of nothing but the mechanical thudding of my hands against dead tissue. His skin has gone that gray-blue color that means we're already too late, that the brain cells are starving and dying by the second.

Leo's working the bag, squeezing the ambu sac in time with my compressions. Every few seconds he checks the monitor, watches those flat green lines refuse to spike. The defibrillator charges and discharges uselessly—the machine knows what we already do. But protocol demands we try. Protocol says we don't call time of death until the supervisor arrives.

"Dispatch, this is Rig 42," Leo speaks into the radio mounted above us. Static answers him back. Not the usual white noise or cross-channel chatter we get driving past the cell towers in Kearny, but something thicker. Wet. Like breathing into a microphone held under running water.

He tries again. Nothing. He flicks switches, adjusts frequencies. The static doesn't change.

"That's weird," he mutters. "We're right next to that tower off Pulaski Skyway."

I keep compressing. *Thump-thump, thump-thump.* My arms burn. Sweat drips down my spine despite the December cold seeping in around the ambulance doors. Outside, through the small rear windows, I can see the South Side traffic jam stretching for miles—everyone trying to get home for New Year's Eve. Car horns create a distant, frustrated symphony.

The smell inside the ambulance shifts suddenly. Diesel exhaust from the rig's engine, yes, and the metallic tang of blood from our patient, the antiseptic sting of rubbing alcohol from the bottle in the med kit—but underneath all that, something else. Burning metal. Sharp and acrid, like someone's welding without a mask nearby. It makes my eyes water.

"Mara." Leo's voice cuts through my focus. "The lights."

I glance up at the overhead fluorescents. They're flickering now, cycling between their normal harsh white and something else entirely—a bruised purple, the color of old contusions, of bruised fruit about to rot. Each pulse of light seems to slow down, stretch out longer than it should.

"What the hell?" I lean harder into the compressions.

My Nextel pager starts vibrating on my belt. Then Leo's does too. Both of them buzzing violently against our hips, rattling until I think the plastic casing might crack. I reach over with one hand while keeping rhythm with the other, squinting at the screen.

No text message. Just numbers counting backward:

*...01:47...*
*...01:46...*
*...01:45...*

A countdown. But from what? The New Year's ball drop in Times Square still has almost two hours to go. This doesn't make sense.

The temperature inside the ambulance plummets. My breath comes out in visible puffs. The patient's skin goes clammy, tiny ice crystals forming along his jawline even though we've got the heater running full blast. The cold isn't natural—it's wrong somehow, like being locked in a freezer that keeps getting colder no matter how much heat you add.

"Leo, grab another blanket," I snap. "And see if you can get anything on that radio."

He nods, fumbling with the thermal blankets stored under the bench seat. His fingers are shaking—not from fear, but from the unnatural cold spreading through the vehicle. Outside, I can hear the traffic noise changing pitch, car horns stretching and warping like audio tape being slowed down or sped up.

Then, cutting through all the static and distorted sound, a voice emerges. Clear as crystal. It's coming from the ambulance radio, from Leo's pager, from somewhere deep inside my own head.

*"Ten..."*

Male voice. Smooth. Calm. Professional, like a game show host announcing lottery numbers.

*"Nine..."*

The countdown continues. My heart hammers against my ribs. This is impossible. Radios don't broadcast simultaneously to pagers. Pagers don't pick up radio signals. These systems don't talk to each other.

*"Eight..."*

I look at Leo. He's staring at me with wide eyes, his mouth slightly open. He heard it too.

*"Seven... Six..."*

The air inside the ambulance starts moving wrong. Not blowing, not circulating—the way air should move—but roiling, eddying, creating currents that shouldn't exist in an enclosed space this small. Paper scraps lift off the floor. A pen rolls across the med kit counter. My hair lifts around my face despite every window being closed.

*"Five..."*

The voice drops lower now, deeper into subsonic frequencies that vibrate in my chest cavity, in my bones, in the fillings of my teeth. The fluorescent lights above us stop flickering and instead pulse in time with the countdown, bathing everything in that sickly purple bruise-light.

*"Four..."*

Through the rear windows, I catch movement beyond the traffic jam. The sky over Newark Bay is doing something it shouldn't. Rippling, maybe? Like heat waves rising from summer asphalt, except we're in December and this isn't heat distortion. Whatever it is makes my stomach turn.

*"Three..."*

The countdown voice distorts horribly, stretching out, slowing down until each syllable takes an eternity. "...Thhhhhrrreeeee..."

"Mara," Leo whispers, his face pale as chalk. "What's happening?"

*"Twoooo..."*

The temperature drops another twenty degrees. The windows frost over instantly. Inside the ambulance, ice crystals form on every surface—on metal, on plastic, even growing along the walls themselves like some organic growth.

*"Oooooooone..."*

The radio screams. Both pagers scream. Every electronic device inside Rig 42 lights up at once, flashing error messages, bleeding sound, producing a cacophony that makes my ears ring. The defibrillator charges itself again without anyone touching it, arcs of electricity jumping between its contacts.

Then silence.

Absolute. Total. Silent as a tomb.

I can still hear my own heartbeat though—that frantic rabbit-drum against my ribs. And outside, very far away, the sound of midnight fireworks beginning their celebration across the city.

But here, now, in these final moments before the stroke of midnight, time itself seems to have stopped breathing.

The patient beneath my hands hasn't changed. Still flatlining. Still gray-blue. Still dead or dying.

## CHAPTER 2: THE Y2K BREACH

The horn died mid-blast—a choked gurgle that cut to silence like someone snapping a guitar string. Then came the sound.

Not a sound. A *thickness*. The air itself turned viscous, pressing against my skin like warm tar. I felt it through my seat, through my boots, through the fillings in my teeth—

The world went white.

My spine compressed against the driver's seat as Rig 42 lifted straight up. Two feet. Maybe three. Time stretched like taffy while my stomach dropped, then we *crashed* back down hard enough to crack the chassis. Metal shrieked. Something exploded beneath the hood—a shower of sparks cascading across the windshield, dying wetly on the rain-slick asphalt.

"VANCE!"

I was already moving when Leo screamed my name, thrown sideways over the stretcher rack, my left shoulder connecting solidly with the oxygen tanks. Pain shot through my collarbone like lightning. Glass shattered—the safety glass behind me, fracturing into a million spiderweb cracks.

Outside, through the cracked windshield, Newark Bay Avenue had become something out of a fever dream. Every sodium-vapor streetlamp erupted simultaneously—blue-white blooms that burned away in microseconds, leaving afterimages seared onto my retinas. Traffic lights melted into cascades of colored light before popping like fireworks. The rolling blackout swept down the avenue in a wave, but between each extinguished light there was a moment of impossible illumination—revealing things that shouldn't exist.

Things in the shadows between buildings. Things with too many limbs. Things that moved wrong.

The asphalt heaved upward in a series of violent convulsions, buckling like a living thing. A manhole cover launched ten feet into the air, spinning end-over-end before disappearing into the chaos. Water mains snapped—geysers of dirty water erupting through the pavement, mixing with raw sewage and God-knows-what-else from the Undercity below.

"Fuck! FUCK!" Leo's hands were already on the crumpled door, kicking it open with his boot. The metal groaned, buckled, finally gave way. He tumbled out into the storm of debris, blood streaming down his face from a gash above his temple. His two-way radio hit the ground, skittered under Rig 42's wheels.

Dead. Everything electronic was dead.

I dragged myself upright using the stretcher handle, tasting copper and ozone. My tongue felt numb. The air *hurt* to breathe—invisible needles pricking at my lungs with every inhale. That battery-taste, metallic and acrid, coated the back of my throat.

"Leo!"

He was bent over, hands braced on his knees, coughing up what looked like black phlegm onto the ruined street. Behind him, the revelers who'd been celebrating the new millennium just seconds ago had all collapsed simultaneously. Bodies sprawled across the sidewalk and broken glass, convulsing in perfect unison—

No. Not quite perfect. Their movements synched, then desynched, then synched again, like marionettes on tangled strings. Mouths opened in silent screams that somehow I could still hear—the sound bypassing my ears entirely, vibrating straight through my skull.

One by one, they stopped moving.

The silence after the chaos was worse than the noise.

* * *

The moment arrived with no fanfare, just a pulse—like swallowing lightning and having it lodge behind your sternum.

I was checking the rig's oxygen tank when the world went sideways. The digital clock on our dashboard flickered from 11:59:59 to 12:00:00, and that split-second hung in the air like a held breath before the scream.

It started as a feeling—a wrongness crawling across my skin like static electricity. Then the screams began, echoing down the length of the Neon Hollow streets where we'd been dispatched for what should have been a routine New Year's Eve drunk driver.

"Vance?" My partner Tommy grabbed my shoulder, his knuckles white. Through the windshield, the avenue had transformed into something out of a fever dream.

A woman in a sequined silver dress stood frozen under a flickering streetlight. Her mouth opened in a soundless O before her spine extended upward with an audible *crack-crack-crack*, her vertebrae telescoping like some cosmic accordion. Her ears pulled back from her head, narrowing to points, cartilage twisting and reshaping with wet, organic sounds that made my stomach clench. She collapsed to her knees, clutching her transforming skull, blood trickling from her nose as her face sharpened into angular elvish features.

"Jesus Christ," Tommy whispered. "What the hell is happening?"

I didn't answer because I couldn't tear my eyes away. Across the street, a massive bouncer—the kind of guy who looked like he bench-pressed Cadillacs—dropped to all fours. His bones snapped and compressed with sounds like pistol shots, his body condensing and broadening simultaneously. He screamed in a voice thick with North Jersey accent: "My friggin' legs! What did they put in the blow?! Somebody call a goddamn doctor!"

The man kept shrinking, his frame compacting until he was hunched over, stocky and dense, his arms thickening with muscle mass that defied physics. His beard grew outward, coarsening, his face widening. A Dwarf. The word flashed through my mind uninvited, clinical despite the horror of watching it happen.

Near an alleyway between a shuttered video rental store and a smoke shop, a homeless man sat against a dumpster. His transformation was slower but somehow worse—a gradual dissolution where his features melted and reformed. His eyes dilated until the pupils swallowed the irises whole, leaving nothing but vast black pools. He shrank, his clothes hanging loose on suddenly tiny limbs, becoming a Gnome before our horrified gaze.

The air tasted metallic, like licking a car battery during a lightning storm. Blue-black ozone hung visible in the cold January night, swirling around people like malevolent fog. Everywhere I looked, someone was changing—bones breaking, skin rippling, bodies contorting into shapes nature never intended.

A car slammed into the one ahead of it with a crunch of metal and shattering glass. Then another. The pile-up cascaded down the avenue as panicked drivers lost control. Brake lights painted everything red, reflecting off the smog-thick air.

"Mara!" Tommy shook me hard. "We need to move! We're sitting ducks here!"

I nodded, hands shaking as I fumbled for the rig's keys. Around us, chaos erupted into full-blown riot. The newly transformed stumbled and ran, some fleeing in terror, others attacking their surroundings in confusion. A Street-Elf woman clawed at her own face, screaming. Two Dwarves had begun fighting each other, their thick arms swinging with enough force to dent hubcaps.

The crowd surged toward our stalled ambulance, pounding on the sides with desperate fists.

"Help us!"
"Please, my daughter—she's changing!"
"My husband is dying out there!"

I could see them through the windows—faces twisted in pain and fear, bodies still transforming, some half-way between human and whatever they'd become. Blood ran from noses, ears, eyes. People convulsed on the pavement, their forms shifting and solidifying in agonizing increments.

"Vance!" Tommy's voice cut through the cacophony. He was pointing at the dash. Our radio crackled to life, but instead of the dispatcher's voice, there was only static mixed with something else—something that sounded almost like screaming, but not quite human.

The pounding on the rig intensified. Hands appeared pressed against the windows, dozens of them, all begging.

* * *

The moment stretches into an eternity of three seconds. One. Two. Three.

The world ends at midnight on January first, year two-thousand, and I'm trapped inside an aluminum box with a dead man.

Or dying. Maybe still alive. I can't tell anymore.

Leo slams the rear doors shut from outside—*CLANG*—the sound echoing through the cramped space like a judge's gavel. Through the small rectangular window, I catch his silhouette wrestling with someone in the street, trying to keep the panicked mob from swarming our rig. Hands pound against the exterior walls. *Thump thump thump.* Like we're a ship caught in a storm.

But this isn't water. It's worse.

The Breach hits like God's own fist.

Everything electronic dies simultaneously—the LifePak monitor goes black with a pathetic *fzzzt*, the overhead lights flicker once then plunge us into near-darkness, illuminated only by those hellish blue flashes seeping through the windows. The strobing light catches our patient's face in sickening intervals: pale skin, slack jaw, eyes rolled back showing only whites.

"Come on, come on," I whisper to myself, my hands already finding the carotid artery beneath clammy skin. "You don't die in my rig. Not tonight."

My fingers press deeper, searching for that telltale pulse. There's nothing. Or maybe... there. Faint as a prayer.

I grab the defibrillator paddles—still warm—but when I check the readout, it's dead. Completely fried. The screen shows only static, crawling across the darkness like infected flesh.

"Shit!" My voice cracks. "Shit, shit, shit!"

The pounding on the exterior intensifies. *BANG BANG BANG!* Someone screaming incoherently outside. Another voice joining in—a chorus of panic spreading through Neon Hollow like wildfire through dry brush.

I abandon the useless equipment and lean over the stretcher, both palms pressed flat against the man's sternum. His chest cavity is cold under my gloved hands. Too cold.

"How long?" I mutter aloud though he can't hear me. "How long has it been? Three minutes? Five?"

Time collapsed when the millennium hit zero. Every clock in three states probably died at the exact same moment—the Breach didn't care about temporal order or cause-and-effect chains. It just *was*, flooding through every crack in reality's foundation like water through a broken dam.

The patient's skin feels wrong beneath my palms. Not just cold but... empty somehow. Like there's no one home anymore.

"No." I shake my head violently. "No, you're not gone. You can't be."

My thumbs lock together and I begin compressions—hard and fast, rhythm established by muscle memory rather than conscious thought. One hundred per minute. Two inches deep. Counting out loud to maintain focus: "*and one, and two, and three*—"

The rig rocks slightly as more bodies slam into its sides. Glass cracks somewhere above my head—windshield or passenger window, I can't tell which. The blue flashes grow brighter, closer, accompanied now by sounds that aren't quite human screams mixed with something else. Something ancient and hungry.

"*and forty-seven, and forty-eight, and forty-nine*—"

My arms burn from the exertion but I don't stop. Can't stop. Won't let this man die while I'm holding his life between my fucking hands.

The defibrillator sits useless on the floor board beside my feet—a monument to modern medicine rendered impotent by whatever cosmic horror just tore through the fabric of our world. We used to think we could cheat death with machines and technology. Used to believe in our training, our protocols, our science.

All lies.

"*and ninety-six, and ninety-seven, and ninety-eight*—"

I lean down to check for a pulse again because protocol demands it even though everything inside me knows what I'll find—and that's when it happens.

A sensation blooms deep in my chest cavity like a second heartbeat but wrong. Too fast. Too hot. Burning upward through my sternum toward my throat where—

The scent hits me.

Not ozone anymore. Not that electrical-copper taste coating every surface of my mouth from the Breach's arrival. This is different. Organic. Familiar yet alien.

*Cedar.*

Fresh cut cedar planks, sawdust still clinging to aromatic wood fibers—the smell floods the cramped interior of Rig 42 so completely that it pushes aside all traces of electricity and panic and fear. My vision whites out at the edges as heat explodes behind my breastbone—

And then stops.

Just... stops.

Leaving only silence inside my ribcage where two hearts should be beating but somehow aren't.

## CHAPTER 3: WHITE-LILAC AND FRESH CEDAR

My hands are shaking—not from shock or fear, but from something else entirely. Something I can't name yet.

The fluorescent lights overhead flicker once, twice, then settle into a steady hum that doesn't match the rhythm of my heartbeat. Through the windshield, the Pulaski Skyway stretches out like a concrete artery clogged with abandoned vehicles, their hazards blinking red against the predawn darkness. Rig 42 sits dead on the shoulder, engine silent, dashboard dark except for the emergency flashers painting everything in bursts of crimson.

I should be checking vitals. Running cardiac monitoring. But I can't look away from my hands.

They're glowing.

Not bright enough to be mistaken for anything except what they are—a soft, pulsing luminescence that seeps through my nitrile gloves like bioluminescence in deep-sea creatures. White-lilac. That's the only way to describe it: pure white shot through with threads of lavender so faint you'd miss them if you blinked. The light moves beneath my skin, tracing the veins in my wrists in lazy spirals that match my pulse.

"Jesus Christ," I whisper, lifting my left hand closer to my face. The heat hits me first—not burning, but warm. Soothing. It travels up my forearm in waves, each one stronger than the last, spreading across my shoulders and down my spine like someone pouring liquid warmth between my vertebrae. My breath catches as the tension in my neck—weeks of twelve-hour shifts compressed into knots—melts away.

And the smell.

Sweet Christ, the smell.

Fresh-cut cedar. Not the musty scent of old closets or cheap air fresheners, but the clean, sharp aroma of wood shavings right after the saw blade passes through the grain. It fills the cab of the ambulance, pushing back everything else—the copper tang of blood on my uniform, the acrid stench of electrical fire from the shorted alternator, the sickening sweetness of vomit pooled near the stretcher mounts. All of it just... gone. Erased by cedar and something else underneath, something floral and impossibly pure.

"It's a concussion," I tell myself, trying to steady my voice. "You hit your head when the rig stalled. You're hallucinating." But even as I say it, I know it's bullshit. Concussions don't feel like this. They don't smell like this. They sure as hell don't make your hands glow.

I tear my gaze from my hands and look at the patient.

The man who shouldn't be alive.

He's maybe fifty, maybe younger—hard to tell with half his face swollen shut and bruised purple-black. Road rash covers his arms where he was thrown from his car during whatever chaos brought down the skyway tonight. His shirt is torn open, revealing pale skin stretched tight over ribs that should have snapped under the force of impact. But it's his chest cavity that makes me forget about the impossible thing happening to my hands.

Beneath his skin, barely visible through flesh and muscle, something pulses. Faint. Weak. But there.

His heart.

I can see it beating—or rather, struggling to beat. White-lilac and cedar.

## CHAPTER 8: BLACK-IRON COUGH

The rain hammered down like bullets against my tactical helmet as I sprinted across the collapsed tenement site. The acrid stench of crushed brick and something worse—something organic and wrong—filled my lungs with each breath.

"Commander Vance! Over here!" A firefighter waved frantically from behind a towering pile of twisted rebar and pulverized concrete. His face was blackened with soot, eyes wide with the kind of terror that comes from watching people die who shouldn't be dying.

Her left leg was pinned under three thousand pounds of debris, but she was conscious—her eyes tracking me with desperate hope even as blood bubbled at the corner of her mouth.

"Not yet," I whispered, placing my palm flat against her forehead. "Hold on."

The familiar warmth started in my core, that liquid light I'd learned to summon during the Breach years. It flowed up through my arm like molten gold, pooling where flesh met flesh. Usually, this is where the healing begins—the cells remembering their original blueprint, the tissue knitting itself whole. But tonight, something fought back.

It felt like touching an oil slick made of shadow. My magic rippled against an unseen barrier in her lungs, sliding off like water on glass. When I pushed harder, concentrating until sweat beaded on my temples, I caught a glimpse of what was wrong: dark, viscous sludge moving through her bronchial tubes, thick as motor oil, *alive* in some terrible way.

"What the fuck..." Miller had dropped to his knees beside me, running his scanner over the woman. The readout flickered red. "Commander, her vitals are collapsing. Pulmonary edema, but the scanner says... it says there's foreign matter in her system. Like she inhaled something that won't let go."

The sludge moved with purpose. Not random contamination, but something directed, almost intelligent. Each particle seemed to carry a tiny fragment of intent—not human, not even close to anything I'd encountered during the Breach. "You're losing them," Miller said quietly. His voice carried no blame, just the flat statement of fact that paramedics learn to live with.

Above it all, the strobing lights continued their hypnotic rotation—blue and white against red and amber—a Technicolor nightmare painted across the tenement's skeletal remains.

Miller caught my eye, his expression unreadable behind his respirator mask. He understood. We couldn't heal what we didn't understand.

I looked around at the growing line of stretchers, at the faces twisted in pain or frozen in death, at the black sludge staining the pavement like oil spills. Whatever had caused this collapse, whatever had contaminated these people's lungs with something that resisted divine healing...

It wasn't over.

And I had no idea what we were going to do when the next wave came.

I grabbed my med kit and yanked out a syringe, jabbing it into the toddler's thigh without hesitation. Pure adrenaline—nothing fancy, just what I needed to keep his heart pumping while I figured out what the hell was happening inside him.

I pulled back, looking down at the child's face. His mother was still screaming somewhere behind me, but her voice sounded distant now, muffled by the pounding of blood in my ears. I checked his pulse—weak, erratic, fading fast.

The black phlegm on the pavement caught the emergency lights from the surrounding vehicles, making it look like oil shimmering across water. Except oil didn't sizzle when it hit rain. And oil definitely didn't feel like it was actively fighting my healing magic, trying to choke this kid to death while I held him.

My magic smashed against the dark barrier inside his lungs, and for just a second—a fraction of a heartbeat—I thought I broke through.

Black-iron flecks, sizzling against the cold February air. The same goddamn substance I'd seen coating the inside of that collapsed basement support column. Only this wasn't oxidized steel. It moved with wrongness, writhing across the pavement like oil on water before fading to rust-brown.

"The whole building's got it," someone croaked from the triage line forming behind me. "Vance!" My radio crackled—Substation Seven dispatch, voice tight with urgency. "We've got reports of similar incidents spreading through Neon Hollow. Tenement collapse at Seventh and Marston, another at Kearny Road. All showing identical symptoms."

My jaw clenched hard enough to ache. Spreading. Systemic. Deliberate.

"Sir, I need you to breathe for me," I said softly, pulling out my trauma shears. The standard protocol wouldn't work here. This wasn't a simple obstruction or infection. This was... I didn't even have a name for what this was yet.

I pressed my palm against his chest, feeling the labored rise and fall, and reached for the Fourth Law. Warmth built between my fingers—not the clean white-gold of healing, but something darker, more primal. My magic had saved lives in County General ER a thousand times before. But this...

He coughed again, and I saw blood flecked with those same metallic particles.

Behind me, more victims were arriving via stretcher and ambulance. The coughing chorus grew louder, more desperate. Dozens of people, all showing the exact same symptom.

All poisoned by the same source.

I pulled my hand back from the old man's chest, my breath coming hard through my teeth. The black-iron residue coating his airway wasn't just physical matter. It was corrupted telluric energy—ground magic twisted into something toxic, pumped directly into the South Side water supply.

Someone was poisoning us. Methodically. Deliberately.

And I was on the front lines with no way to stop it.

## CHAPTER 9: THE REYES CROSSING

## CHAPTER 10: THE BURIED VIAL

The fluorescent lights hummed overhead like dying insects as I waited in the observation room behind thick glass. "Note here—the manufacturing markers match three different BATC subsidiaries operating out of the Newark Bay industrial zone. My hands were shaking now. Eighteen months. That meant Martinez, Reyes, everyone we'd lost in that district... they hadn't died from shoddy infrastructure or bad luck. They'd been murdered by their own company. By our company.

"We need to file immediate reports," I said, my voice strained. "Evacuations, medical interventions for exposed populations, criminal investigation—"

Chen's hand came down on mine, stopping me from reaching my radio. Her grip was surprisingly strong. "Commander, you're upset. Understandable. But you need to consider the broader picture."

You'll continue your investigations but focus on prevention measures rather than source identification. We'll redirect contaminated water flows, implement filtration systems, treat affected residents quietly through our medical network."

"I want you to be strategic." She pulled a document from her briefcase—a formal promotion packet bearing my name. "Commander Mara Vance, promoted to Section Chief of South Side Operations. Authority over all field teams in Districts 7-12. Substantial salary increase, executive benefits package."

My eyes fixed on the paper even as disgust churned in my stomach. A promotion. They were offering me a gold-plated cage.

You acknowledge that while contamination exists, its source is indeterminate—possibly industrial accidents, possibly terrorist sabotage targeting infrastructure. You recommend increased security funding for your district instead of external investigation."

I looked back through the glass at Piotr, who was carefully sealing the original sample vials in evidence lockers. He didn't look happy about it either.

A comprehensive lie. A managed truth where BATC could control the narrative and limit their liability without ever admitting culpability.

She let the implication hang in the air. "And I'm sure we can find another commander better suited to execute our new strategic directives. Someone more... flexible."

The pen felt heavy in my hand. Through the glass, I could see Piotr finishing up, boxing away the evidence that would prove corporate murder. My other hand moved unconsciously to my chest pocket where I kept my personal notes, the ones I'd been keeping separate from official channels.

If I signed, I became complicit. But if I refused, they'd just bury someone else—the investigation, the victims, all of it. And I'd be out of position to do anything about it.

The fluorescent lights hummed on, indifferent and cold as justice.

Just one tear. Just one. But if I started, I knew I wouldn't stop, and there wasn't time for breaking down. Not when whoever had done this was still out there, walking around free while my mother burned in whatever hellhole they sent her soul to after what they did on that bridge.

The Golden Saint.

Just power.

"Let them come," I whispered to the empty locker room. "Let them all fucking come."

I grabbed my helmet from the bench and headed for the exit, my boots echoing in the fluorescent-lit corridor beyond. Whatever Morvain wanted, whatever the High-Spire needed—they were about to get exactly what they'd created.

I was ready to burn the whole system down if that's what it took.

And if I had to become a monster to do it?

Fine by me.

## CHAPTER 15: THE DIRECTOR'S OVERWATCH

The elevator doors slide open with a pneumatic sigh that echoes through an impossible silence.

Gone is the chaos of County General—the screaming patients, the copper-salt stench of blood and antiseptic, the electric urgency that crackles through every shift. Here, there is only silence so complete it has weight.

Black marble floors stretch before me, polished to such a mirror finish I can see my reflection fractured across its surface. My white EMT jacket looks out of place, contaminated, bleeding reality into this sterile environment. The lighting is muted, indirect—expensive lighting designed to make powerful men look benevolent. It doesn't work on me.

My boots click against the marble as I walk down the glass corridor toward Director Morvain's office. Then I pass a doorway left ajar.

The room beyond is a server farm, all steel racks and humming equipment. But these aren't ordinary servers. IBM ThinkPads stacked in towers, their asset tags gleaming under harsh fluorescent lights. And between the racks move figures in bespoke suits—not the cheap off-the-rack affairs worn by most corporate drones, but tailored silk that speaks of old money and older power.

High-Spire Elves.

They're running arcane-magnetic scrubbers over the hardware, handheld devices that crackle with barely visible energy. The sound is wrong—a high-pitched whining squeal that makes my teeth ache, like nails on a chalkboard translated into pure sonic torture. Each pass wipes another layer of data, erasing gigabytes of information into digital nothingness.

I know those asset tags.

Lysandra's research. Her notes on the Newark Bay water toxicity. The Apep's Venom logs she'd been compiling, cross-referencing infection rates with corporate shipping manifests. All of it, disappearing into the electronic void one hard drive at a time.

An Elf adjusts his silk tie while speaking into an earpiece, his voice carrying just enough for me to catch:

"Wipe the redundant backups. If anyone asks, Vance was a rogue contractor. Total scrub."

My hands clench into fists inside my jacket pockets. The white-lilac flame burns steady in my chest, its warmth the only thing keeping me from freezing solid in this tomb of marble and silence. I keep walking. Eyes forward. Triage armor locked down tight around every emotion, every reaction.

"They're burying her twice," I whisper, so low even I can barely hear it beneath the scrubbing machines' scream.

Because there was Lysandra the woman—the researcher who died believing she could make a difference. And then there was Lysandra the legend we built around her corpse, the martyr whose death meant something, who changed something.

Now they're taking even that away.

"Commander Vance." Her voice is professionally warm but her eyes are flat. "Director Morvain will see you now."

She opens one door without waiting for my response, gesturing me through into an antechamber. Beyond, through another set of glass doors, I can see him.

Elias Morvain. Director of BATC Operations, puppet master extraordinaire, and apparently the man responsible for erasing his own daughter's legacy from existence.

He sits behind a desk that could serve as a landing strip for small aircraft, surrounded by windows that offer a god's-eye view of Newark Bay below. Even from here, I can feel his attention focus on me like a predator tracking prey.

The flame in my chest burns brighter, casting shadows across the marble floor that shouldn't be possible in this lightless place.

Whatever game he's playing, whatever role he's written for me—I won't make it easy.

The interview has begun.
