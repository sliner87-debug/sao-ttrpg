## CHAPTER 1: CHAPTER 1

The grease under my nails has its own geography by now. Four years at South Side Auto-Yard means I can tell you which valleys came from the '88 Trans Am and which ridges belong to diesel trucks. Right now though, I'm focused on the crown vic's underbelly—a '99 model that some Newark kingpin needs for his daughter's sweet sixteen. Custom exhaust job, extra thick steel because apparently she's got daddy issues and death wishes.

My creeper rolls another inch backward. The welding torch sits heavy in my glove, familiar weight. The boombox—Sal's ancient Sony tricked out to play mini-discs—hits a commercial break between songs. Some radio jock up in a tower somewhere is doing the countdown thing. Ten seconds to Y2K, ten seconds to nothing probably. Everyone's panicking about computers turning into pumpkins but here we are, same as always.

"Nine... eight..."

I strike the arc. Orange sparks shower down like dying fireflies onto the concrete. Standard shit. Been doing this since I was seventeen, dropping out of Weequahic High because fuck that noise. Fuck college loans and future plans when you've got hands that actually know how to build things.

"Three... two..."

The arc sputters.

Not right. Never sputters like that unless—

Everything goes cold.

I mean *cold*. Like walking into a meat locker cold. My breath hits the air in front of me, visible suddenly, impossible. The back of my neck prickles with gooseflesh. Through the crown vic's chassis gaps, I see the sodium-vapor lamps overhead shift color—golden orange bleeding to something wrong, something between blue and violet and the color of frozen bruises.

The boombox screams.

Not music anymore. Static violence, like someone dragged a magnet through the speaker cones and kept dragging. The LED display cracks, little spiderwebs spreading across the plastic face before it dies completely. The lights above flicker once, twice, then settle into that unnatural cobalt glow.

My stomach lurches.

That's not metaphorical. I feel it physically—the sensation of swallowing something heavy and metallic, like I'd chugged a glass full of iron filings mixed with broken glass. My blood feels thick, sluggish, *wrong* moving through my veins. Pressure builds behind my eyes until I think they might pop.

"Fuck," I grunt, voice strangled.

The torch drops from my hand. It clatters against concrete but doesn't spark, doesn't make any sound except a dull thunk that somehow sounds muffled under water even though we're in dry air. The weld I was working on—half-finished steel—crackles. Tiny crystalline patterns spread across its surface like frost in time-lapse photography.

Outside, past the roll-up door's bottom gap, silence.

Newark never sleeps. You hear traffic, distant sirens, people living their lives at all hours. But right now? Nothing. Not even crickets in the Kearny Marshes a few miles east. Just a low hum underneath everything else—a subsonic vibration that makes my teeth ache and sets every loose bolt in this yard rattling.

Sal's voice comes from the office, muffled: "Reyes! You seeing this shit?"

I try to answer. Can't. My mouth won't form words. The cold is getting worse, seeping up through my coveralls, down from those wrong-colored lights overhead. Every breath burns.

The exhaust pipe I was welding suddenly goes completely black—not painted, not shadowed, but absorbs light like it's hungry for it. Then it snaps bright cobalt blue, glowing without heat, without flame, impossible geometry shifting along its length.

My hands are shaking. Not fear-shaking. Something deeper. My fingers curl involuntarily into fists, and when I look down—

Jesus Christ.

Trails of metallic silver trace across my knuckles as if someone's drawing lines with liquid mercury. They fade almost immediately but they were there. They *were*.

"What the fuck," I whisper finally, and my voice sounds wrong too—echoing slightly like I'm speaking in a space bigger than this cramped auto-yard.

The cobalt glow intensifies.

* * *

The acetylene torch feels wrong in my hand.

I lift it from the workbench, rolling the cold brass cylinder between my palms like I've done a thousand times before. The familiar weight, the slight resistance of the valve trigger—it should be muscle memory by now. But tonight, December 31st, 1999, at eleven-fifty-eight PM, everything feels off-kilter.

Maybe it's the Y2K hype. Maybe it's the fact that I'm working New Year's Eve while half the world parties like they're watching Armageddon come true. Or maybe it's something else entirely—the way my blood feels too hot beneath my skin despite the January cold seeping through the auto-yard's rusted walls.

I click the flint.

The spark catches, but the flame that erupts from the nozzle isn't orange. It's not yellow-white like every welding torch I've ever lit. This one burns a color that doesn't have a name—a cobalt so deep and cold it looks like frozen lightning, like the heart of an ice storm made visible.

My breath catches.

The temperature in the bay plummets instantly, violently. Ten degrees, maybe more. I can see my exhale misting in the air, hanging suspended for a heartbeat before dissipating into the harsh sodium-vapor light overhead. The frost-cobalt flame casts long, sharp shadows across the concrete floor, stretching them until the entire space looks like it's been warped, distorted by some trick of perspective I don't understand.

"Jesus," I whisper to myself, holding the torch away from me like it might bite. My voice sounds hollow in the sudden cold.

I flick the trigger off. The blue flame vanishes immediately, leaving only darkness and the lingering smell of burning ozone—sharp, electric, wrong. Nothing smells like ozone during a simple weld. That scent belongs to lightning strikes and downed power lines, not acetylene tanks.

I try again. Click. The same freezing cobalt erupts, steady and silent where the orange roar should be. No hiss. No pop. Just absolute, dead-silent blue consuming everything in its path without heat, without sound, just that impossible cold that makes my teeth ache.

The Crown Vic sits on the lift in front of me, engine block exposed like a patient on an operating table. Sal needs this transmission replaced by midnight—some rich asshole from Sterling Manor promised him double pay if he could get his luxury car back before New Year's. I've got maybe twelve minutes now.

I need to work fast.

Holding my breath, I bring the torch closer to the steel chassis supporting the engine. The blue flame licks against the metal and—

Nothing.

It doesn't melt. Steel should flow at temperatures like this—or at least glow red-hot. Instead, it ripples beneath the flame like water disturbed by a stone. The metal *moves*, responding to something I'm not consciously commanding. I feel it in my chest first, then spreading down through my arms: a strange connection, like I've suddenly gained a second sense that can taste iron in the air the way sharks taste blood in water.

The steel bends around the transmission mount, reshaping itself into a configuration that would take hours with conventional tools but happens in seconds. Not melted. Not cut. Reformed. As if the molecular structure understands what I need and rearranges itself accordingly.

My hands are shaking now. I drop the torch onto the concrete with a clatter that echoes too loud in the cold silence.

"Fuck," I say, louder this time, spinning away from the workbench. My breath comes faster, misting the air between us. "What the hell is happening?"

The smell of ozone intensifies. Burning wires? Downed power line somewhere nearby? But there's no sound of electricity arcing, no distant thunder. Just that scent and this impossible cold and my heart hammering against my ribs like it wants out.

Behind me, I hear footsteps on the metal stairwell. Heavy boots. Sal coming back early from his smoke break, probably wondering why I haven't started welding yet.

I turn, forcing my expression into something resembling normalcy even as my breath clouds in front of my face.

But when Sal reaches the bottom step and sees what I've done to the Crown Vic—when he sees the way the steel has been reformed without heat marks or grinding scars, when he catches sight of that frost-cobalt residue coating my gloves in blue-black iron-gall patterns—his jaw drops open.

"Cole?" His voice cracks. "What... what did you put in those tanks? That's not standard gas mix. Nothing looks like—"

He can't finish. He just stares at the car, then at my hands, then at the torch lying discarded on the floor where cobalt-blue flame continues flickering harmlessly across its nozzle despite being unattended.

I don't have an answer for him because I don't understand it myself.

All I know is that when midnight strikes tonight, everything changed—and I'm standing at ground zero with frozen lightning dancing between my fingers and solid steel bending to my will without conscious thought.

The Y2K bug was supposed to be about computer systems crashing.

Turns out, it was about other things waking up too.

* * *

The sirens hit like a chorus of dying angels.

I was still wiping the blue-black soot from my knuckles when they started—first one, then three more, until the whole South Side was screaming. My heart hammered against my ribs as I fumbled for my Nokia, the green LCD glow cutting through the chemical twilight of the auto-yard.

**911-ELENA**

Three letters. Three numbers. That's all it took to turn my blood cold.

"Sal!" I shouted, already moving toward my truck. "Something's wrong at the clinic!"

Sal didn't answer. He was staring at his welding torch like it had just sprouted wings. The flame that had been orange thirty seconds ago now burned silent and cobalt, no heat, no pop, just pure frozen light that made my teeth ache.

I yanked open the driver-side door of my '87 Ford. The engine coughed once, twice, then roared to life. I didn't bother closing the door all the way as I threw it in reverse, tires screeching on cracked asphalt.

The city looked wrong as I peeled out onto Pulaski Street. Wrongo. Like somebody had taken a photograph of Newark and smeared it with greasy fingers. The sodium-vapor lamps flickered, casting everything in sickly yellow-green pulses. But underneath—that was the problem. Underneath, there was something else. Something that shouldn't exist.

My hands were shaking on the steering wheel. Not from fear. From *feeling*. Every piece of metal within twenty feet suddenly felt like it was humming. The brake lines. The frame. The highway overpass up ahead. It was like walking past a radio station you couldn't quite tune in to, static crawling across your skin.

Then I saw them—the first ones.

People stumbling out of tenement buildings, eyes glowing faint-white, mouths hanging open. One woman collapsed on the sidewalk, her spine twisting at an impossible angle. Another man was clawing at his own face, leaving trails of black sludge that steamed when they hit pavement.

"Jesus Christ," I whispered.

The Nokia vibrated again. **911-ELENA**

I floored it.

The truck lurched forward as I took the on-ramp for the Pulaski Skyway. My eyes kept flicking to the rearview mirror. Behind me, more figures emerging from doorways. Ahead, smoke—no, not smoke—*something* rising from downtown. Great plumes of it, dark and writhing against the night sky, backlit by fires I couldn't see yet but knew were burning.

My mother worked the night shift at St. Jude's Free Clinic on Martin Luther King Jr. Boulevard. Right on the edge of the Ironbound district where all the factories still ran. Where the water mains were older than dirt.

As I crested the viaduct, I saw it.

A geyser. A fucking *geyser* of black oily sludge erupting from what had to be the main water treatment facility, maybe a mile south. The column shot up into the sky like a fountain of pure nightmare, spreading outward, heading straight for my neighborhood. For Danny's elementary school. For the housing projects where half my cousins lived.

"Shit! Shit SHIT!"

I slammed my fist against the steering wheel hard enough to feel bones protest. Blue sparks erupted from the impact point—not orange. Never orange anymore. Cobalt. Silent. Frozen.

The Nokia buzzed one last time before dying completely.

I didn't need it to tell me this was bad.

## CHAPTER 2: CHAPTER 2

The frost-cobalt magic hummed beneath my skin like a living thing, cooling the sweat that beaded along my spine. I shouldn't have taken this route home—should've just gone straight back to my place in Kearny—but something gnawed at me from the moment I left County General. The way those nurses had looked at each other when they thought I was unconscious. The smell of ozone that clung to the air even blocks away from the hospital.

So here I am, crouched behind a rusted chain-link fence near the water treatment sub-station, watching steam billow from the underground access hatch. Danny's apartment building draws its water from this very system. If anything's wrong, he'll know before most people figure it out.

I slipped through the gap I'd cut months ago, landing soft on concrete stained dark with decades of mineral deposits and worse things. The pump room sprawled before me—a cathedral of industrial decay, all massive turbines and hissing valves. The noise hit me immediately: a deafening roar that vibrated through my chest cavity, drowning out everything else.

I pressed myself against the nearest turbine casing, felt the metal throb with pressure. My breath came slow and measured. The cobalt magic pulsed in response, keeping my skin cool despite the oppressive heat radiating from the machinery.

Then I saw them.

Three figures moved through the steam with practiced coordination, their tactical flashlights cutting sharp beams through the haze. Not city workers—their gear was too clean, too military. Black tactical vests over heavy hazmat suits, rune-etched MP5s slung across their chests. Sterling-Voss private military contractors, if I had to guess. Which meant trouble, because Voss only sent guns when someone important wanted something done quietly.

The lead man held what looked like a Motorola radio, speaking into it with cold precision. Even through the face shield of his mask, I could see the sharp angles of an elf's features—High-Spire corporate type, probably made a fortune off the metahuman boom before joining Voss's security arm.

"Bypass the filters," the Elf said, voice clipped and emotionless. "The Four Heads want the tanks emptied. Flush it all into the South Side mains."

My stomach dropped. The Four Heads—what kind of name was that for a client? Sounded like something straight out of a bad comic book, but I'd learned not to underestimate anything these days. Not after what I'd seen at County General.

"The residential towers will get the full concentration," another PMC called back, his voice barely audible over the turbines. "Estimated time until contamination reaches peak levels: thirty minutes."

"Confirm bypass is complete," the Elf commanded. "I want visual on all filtration systems disabled before we proceed."

I watched as two of the PMCs moved toward a massive control panel, their gloved hands working with efficient speed. Warning lights blinked red across a dozen different stations. They weren't shutting down the system—they were perverting it, turning the city's water supply into some kind of delivery mechanism.

For what?

The question turned in my mind as I crouched lower behind the turbine casing. My magic coiled tighter, defensive instincts firing hot. This wasn't just corporate sabotage or gang activity. This felt calculated, orchestrated. Someone had gone through considerable effort to make this happen tonight.

And Danny's apartment was smack in the middle of the affected zone.

I needed to move, needed to warn him, needed to figure out exactly what they were flushing into the system. But first, I had to survive getting out of here without those Voss contractors spotting me.

I waited, watching them work, memorizing every movement, every detail of the station layout. When they finally stepped away from the main controls, I made my decision.

Time to see what Cole Reyes could do when he really tried.

* * *

The lever fell with a metallic CLUNK that echoed through my skull.

Time stretched thin as spider silk. I saw the High-Spire Elf's fingers release the primary valve, watched the ancient brass mechanism begin its rotation, felt the massive underground pumps groan awake beneath our feet like some slumbering god stirring toward apocalypse.

Then the world exploded in sound.

A sound like reality itself tearing apart—the shriek of steel veins rupturing under impossible pressure. Eight hundred thousand gallons of Experimental Sludge, thick as tar and twice as malevolent, had found its freedom. It screamed through pipes designed for clean water, and those pipes remembered they were mortal.

The secondary intake manifold near me simply ceased to exist.

Metal peeled away like wet tissue paper. A geyser of black iron sludge erupted upward, thirty feet of pure chemical nightmare arcing through the sub-station's humid air before gravity claimed it. The stuff hit the concrete floor with the impact of a train wreck, splattering outward in a fan of viscous horror.

It looked like boiling obsidian studded with veins of sickly green fire. Like liquid darkness given form. The stench hit me next—a wall of smell that made my eyes water and my gorge rise. Battery acid. Rotting meat left too long in August sun. The metallic tang of blood mixed with something older, something that predated human civilization and promised to outlast us all.

My frost-cobalt magic flared without thought, without permission—pure defensive instinct born from three years of fighting creatures that wanted me dead. Cold cobalt blue light rippled across my skin, spreading down my arms, crystallizing in the air between my palms. The ambient temperature in the chamber spiked as the industrial pumps labored against their corrupted load, heat pouring off the ruptured pipes like fever sweat.

And then my magic pulled the temperature down. Down hard.

Ice formed on my fingertips. Frost spread across my forearms. My breath misted in air that suddenly tasted clean, almost, compared to what was flooding this room.

The wave of sludge rushed toward me—a wall of corrosive death moving at walking speed, inexorable, hungry. I thrust both hands forward.

"FREEZE!"

My voice cracked like a whip over the screaming metal and panicked shouts. Magic detonated from my chest cavity, a shockwave of crystalline cold that met the advancing sludge head-on. Where my frost-cobalt energy touched Apep's Venom, the iron within instantly solidified. Black iron became brittle blue ice. The sludge froze mid-wave, creating an improvised barricade of treacherous frozen slime between me and oblivion.

It wouldn't hold. Already I could see cracks spider-webbing through the temporary barrier, could feel the strain in my ribs where the magic had torn loose. But it held long enough.

Long enough for me to breathe. Long enough to think. Long enough to realize I'd just bought myself maybe thirty seconds before eight hundred thousand gallons of apocalyptic poison flooded the entire municipal water system for half of North Jersey.

Behind the High-Spire Elf, three PMCs had already started running. Their boots clattered against wet concrete as they abandoned their positions. One stumbled—slipped in spreading sludge—and went down hard. His partner grabbed his arm, hauled him up, kept moving.

But the fourth one wasn't moving at all.

He lay twenty feet from the rupture point, his hazmat suit bubbling like acid-eaten plastic. The sludge had splashed across his legs, eating through the protective material in seconds. Exposed flesh beneath was already blackening, tissue dying by the microsecond. The man's screams tore through the mechanical chaos—a sound pure and animal, all human reason stripped away by unimaginable pain.

"Seal the bulkhead! NOW!" The Elf's voice cut through everything else, cool and controlled despite the disaster unfolding around us. He didn't even look at the screaming man. Didn't spare a glance at his dying subordinate.

Two PMCs spun toward the massive circular door that separated this chamber from the main pumping station. They grabbed the emergency wheel, began turning. Each click of the locking mechanism echoed like a gunshot in my ears.

The dying man crawled forward on ruined hands, leaving a trail of necrotizing flesh and blood behind him. "Please... please help me..."

They sealed the bulkhead.

CLANG-CLANG-CLANG-CLANG—the heavy locks engaged one after another. The sound finished what the Elf had started: it was over. This man was dead, and they knew it. Three years of working security details for metahuman corporations hadn't made them cruel, just practical. You leave someone behind when you have to. You seal the room and save whoever's still salvageable.

I watched the bulkhead's lights go red—sealed. Watched the two PMCs who'd survived turn away from the dying man without another word. Watched the High-Spire Elf tuck his clipboard under one arm and head toward the exit with perfect composure.

And I watched the man whose legs were melting into blackened sludge reach one last time toward salvation that would never come.

My ice barrier cracked.

A fissure ran down its center, spreading outward. Cold sweat dripped down my spine—not from exertion but from understanding. Eight hundred thousand gallons of Apep's Venom now flooding through Sub-Station Seven's pipes. Heading north toward Newark Bay. East toward the Kearny Marshes. South toward the residential districts where families slept unaware.

The New Year's celebration fireworks exploding overhead suddenly seemed obscene.

* * *

The water rises faster than I can think.

My boots hit the rusted grate first—metal screaming against metal as I shoulder-barge the thing open, tearing my jacket sleeve on the jagged edge. I don't stop to bleed. Can't afford to. Behind me, that goddamn experimental sludge churns like living mercury, eating through concrete, dissolving the pumping mechanisms into slag and sludge.

Steam hisses from every surface. The ozone stench burns my lungs with each gasp, chemical rot coating my tongue like battery acid. My skin goes numb where frost-cobalt magic ripples across my forearms, trying to compensate for the hellish heat pouring off those industrial pumps. They're failing. All of them.

The ladder is slick with something that isn't water. I climb anyway, fingers finding purchase even as the metal rungs groan under my weight. My shoulders burn. My lungs scream. But I push upward, toward the circle of moonlight visible through the ceiling hatch.

Something shifts below. A wet, organic sound that shouldn't come from machinery.

I don't look back.

The manhole cover comes free with a heavy *clank* that echoes through the empty streets. Rain hits my face immediately—cold January rain mixing with the industrial smog hanging over Kearny, creating this greasy film in the air. I haul myself up onto asphalt, coughing hard enough to taste copper.

My legs give out. I catch myself on the edge of the opening, breathing hard, watching foam bubbling up from the pipes beneath the street. It's spreading. Already in the municipal grid.

I stagger upright, wiping blood from my split lip. There—a municipal map painted on the wall of the nearby substation building. Street names blur together until my eyes focus: South Street. Fifth Ward. The tenement district.

Right. Fucking. There.

My apartment is three blocks from here.

"Shit." The word comes out strangled. I'm already running toward the corner payphone, boots splashing through oil-slicked puddles. The phone booth glass is cracked, graffiti-covered. I slam the receiver against my ear.

Dead tone. Just a flat *BZZZZZ* that goes straight to my teeth.

"The fuck?" I hit the cradle harder. Nothing changes. Y2K grid failure—they'd warned about it for months, but I didn't think it would actually happen like this. Not tonight. Not while I was down in those tunnels.

Can't call. Can't warn her.

I'm moving before the thought completes, sprinting back toward where I left my truck parked behind an abandoned warehouse. My lungs are fire. Every breath tastes like chemicals and fear. The frost-cobalt magic is still humming under my skin, defensive, waiting, but useless against what's coming.

Keys in hand. Engine roaring to life.

1:10 AM according to the dashboard clock.

Forty PSI. That's how fast the sludge moves through the mains—faster than any car should have to drive. But I floor it anyway, tires screaming as I take the corner onto South Street, heading into the heart of the Fifth Ward where the buildings lean against each other like drunk men sharing secrets.

Steam rises from manholes now. The streets are starting to fog.

My building looms ahead, brick and poverty and everything I've ever called home. The front door is unlocked—I never lock it properly, stupid habit when you live alone. When you spend your nights in places like Sub-Station Seven instead.

I'm taking the stairs two at a time. Three flights. Four. My legs are concrete. My chest is collapsing inward with every step.

Third floor. Hallway stretches forever.

The apartment door—

I don't knock. Just slam through it.

"Emma!"

## CHAPTER 3: CHAPTER 3

The city breathes its last gasp of the twentieth century through my cracked windshield, and I'm already counting the seconds like they're bullets in a revolver I can't fire fast enough.

The Pulaski Skyway stretches ahead of me—a concrete serpent choking on darkness. Every light dead. Every car stalled. The Y2K bug wasn't some computer nerd's wet dream; it was real, and it had just bitten Newark in the throat hard enough to crush its larynx.

I slam the Ford's transmission into neutral, coasting until momentum dies, then I'm out and running before the metal even stops rocking. My steel-toed boots hit pavement that's still warm from the day's heat, but the air? Christ, the air tastes wrong. Like copper pennies and burning plastic.

Behind me, the gridless city groans.

Emergency lights sputter in the distance—County General probably, the only place that might still have backup generators worth shit. But Danny isn't there. Danny's at home, where I left him an hour ago watching MTV with a bowl of Cocoa Puffs, oblivious to the fact that the whole fucking world was about to tear itself apart at the seams.

My lungs burn. The cold doesn't touch me yet—the way it should, the way it would if I were normal—but sweat stings my eyes as I cut across someone's lawn, vault their chain-link fence, and hit South Street running again.

The apartment building looms four stories of peeling brick and rust-stained fire escapes. Emergency lights flicker in the stairwell windows like dying stars trying to remember how to shine.

I don't slow down.

Mrs. Gable is already in the hallway when I hit the third floor. She's crying, her hands pressed against her face, and for a second—I swear to God—for a second she looks human. Then her jaw cracks sideways, bones shifting beneath skin that's turning the color of spoiled meat.

"Stay away from the sinks," I yell, shoving past her hard enough to send her stumbling back into her apartment door. "Don't drink anything! Don't—"

But she's already changing, and I can hear others doing the same. All over this side of Newark. All over North Jersey. The tap water. It's not safe anymore. Nothing is.

I take the last flight of stairs three at once, my shoulder crashing against the wall when I misjudge the landing. The door to our apartment—my apartment, since Mom's been gone six years—stands open just a crack.

Just enough.

Danny's not in the living room.

The TV still plays, soundless now without electricity, frozen on some commercial for pizza that probably tastes like ashes even if you could get them to deliver. His Cocoa Puffs sit on the coffee table, milk going warm and flat.

Then I see it—the glass by the sink. Broken. Water spreading across the linoleum floor, catching the moonlight through the window like spilled blood.

My stomach drops into my boots.

"Danny?"

The bathroom door is closed.

I know what I'm going to find before I turn the knob. I know it with every fiber of my being because this is how stories like mine always start—with something precious poisoned, with a choice that wasn't really a choice at all.

The iron in me wakes up then, slithering through my veins like liquid mercury, and the temperature in the hallway plummets so fast I can see my breath fogging in front of my face.

* * *

The door splintered off its hinges as I kicked it open, the rotten wood giving way like tissue paper. The apartment was black—pitch dark except for the moonlight slicing through the frosted windows.

"Danny?" My voice cracked. Sounded wrong in my own ears.

Silence. Then—a drip. Drip. Drip.

Water running.

I moved through the living room, hands outstretched, fingers brushing against familiar furniture. Coffee table. Couch. The TV stand with Mom's picture sitting crooked on top, her smile frozen from before everything went to hell.

The dripping got louder as I rounded the corner into the kitchenette. The faucet was on full blast, water pouring out but it wasn't clear. It was *wrong*—thick as motor oil, sluicing down the drain with a gurgle that made my teeth hurt. The smell hit me next: sulfurous rot, like something dead had been sitting in the pipes for months.

And there he was.

My little brother stood on our mother's wooden stool, the one she used to reach the high cabinets, his oversized New York Giants pajamas hanging off his skeletal frame. He looked so small up there, eleven years old and already too thin, his dark hair wild around his face. 

In his hand: a glass tumbler filled with tap water.

But it wasn't water.

It was black. Not brown, not gray—*black*. An iridescent, oily black that seemed to swallow the moonlight instead of reflecting it. Little motes of light swirled inside like dying stars.

"Cole?" Danny's voice was sleepy, confused. "The faucet wouldn't turn off. I'm thirsty."

"No." The word ripped from my throat. "Danny, NO!"

I launched myself across the linoleum floor, arms stretched, desperate. Too far. Always too fucking far.

He blinked once at me. Then his lips parted. Then—

He drank.

Swallowed that poison like it was fresh lemonade. The black liquid disappeared down his throat. His eyes widened for just a second, pupils dilating until there was no white left, just two pools of midnight staring into nothing.

"Danny!" My hands caught him as he crumpled, the empty glass slipping from his fingers. It shattered against the floor with a sound like breaking bones—*CRACK-CRACK-CRACK*—and the shards scattered across the yellowed linoleum.

He convulsed in my arms, back arching unnaturally. A wet, rattling cough exploded from his mouth, and what came out wasn't spit or bile.

It was *iron*.

Flecks of rust-red metal spraying across my face, stinging like acid, sticking to my skin where blood should have been. His lungs were filling up. I could feel it—the weight of iron settling inside him like ballast.

The apartment temperature plummeted. My breath became clouds in front of me. Frost crawled across the kitchen windows, spreading like veins, like death creeping inward.

"Mom," Danny whispered, blood trickling from his nose now, mixing with the iron dust on his upper lip. "Cole... can't... breathe..."

My magic didn't ask permission. It didn't think. It just *was*.

Heat flooded through me—not the warm kind but the burning kind, the kind that felt like swallowing molten glass. Power surged outward in desperate waves. I slammed both palms against Danny's chest, trying to pull, trying to draw that iron *out*, trying to reverse whatever the fuck had happened.

But you can't put iron back once it's in your lungs. You can't unwring the towel.

The frost spread faster, covering every surface. The running water froze solid mid-drip, icicles forming and shattering, forming and shattering. My hands burned ice-cold as they pressed into my brother's collapsing ribcage.

"Please," I begged something—I don't know what, God or devil or the universe itself. "Please please please *please*—"

Danny's eyes rolled back until only white showed. Then even that went gray, turning ash-colored, lifeless.

The faucet finally stopped running. The last droplet of black water froze in mid-air between the spigot and the sink, suspended like a tear, like a promise, like everything I'd failed at crystallized into one perfect moment before it shattered on the floor with all the rest.

And my little brother stopped breathing altogether.

* * *

The air in my lungs burns colder than January wind off the bay.

Danny's body goes rigid against me, a seized engine locking up mid-torque. His spine arches backward with a sound like tearing fabric and snapping cable. I barely catch him before his head cracks against the linoleum.

"DANNY!"

My voice splits the apartment.

He convulses once, twice—a marionette with cut strings jerking on invisible wires. Then his mouth opens and that *thing* comes out.

A spray of arterial red hits the wall behind him, but it's wrong, all wrong—the blood runs thick and viscous, speckled with fragments that catch the overhead light like crushed glass. Black glass. Metallic shards that scatter across our kitchen floor with the sound of breaking machinery.

I drop to my knees without thinking, grabbing him by the shoulders. His skin feels wrong under my palms—too hot where it should be cold, fever-burning through the chill already spreading from my hands. Because they're glowing now. That same frost-cobalt light that killed the men at Sub-Station Seven is bleeding from my fingers, casting dancing shadows across Danny's face.

His breathing makes a noise like grinding gears.

No—worse than gears. Like someone trying to breathe through a fistful of rusted nails.

"Shit, shit, SHIT—"

I slam my glowing hands against his chest, right over his heart. My boots find the right spot. Here, where the foundation gave way. Here, where the second story imploded down into the basement. I stop at the edge, looking into the collapse. Melted steel I-beams jut up at impossible angles, twisted like pretzels. The concrete has turned to dust and fused slag.

And there—buried under three tons of wreckage—I see red.

Not blood. Worse than blood.

Ceramic shards, painted the color of arterial spray. Pieces of the containment vessel Elena used for her research. The one she swore was stable. The one that burned clean and hot enough to melt steel in seconds flat.

"You stupid fucking woman," I whisper to the ruins. "You beautiful, brilliant, dead idiot."

The words taste like copper. Like I'm chewing on old coins.

My hands are shaking—not from cold, not from fear. From the blood-iron magic coiling tight inside me, retreating deeper and deeper as shock sets in. It wants to help. Can't. There's nothing left to heal. Nothing living to pull back from death's edge.

Just ash and slag and the acrid stench of failure.

I drop to my knees at the crater's edge. Dawn light catches the white-hot slag, makes it gleam like diamonds. Beautiful. Deadly. Final.

Somewhere behind me, Grask coughs into his radio. The city wakes around us—the distant sound of traffic on the Pulaski Skyway, the rumble of early-morning trains crossing Newark Bay. Life goes on.

For everyone else.

I dig my fingers into the pulverized brick. Feel it sift through my grasp like sand. Like time. Like all the moments I should have spent here instead of out there, playing vigilante, thinking I could make a difference.

"Cole."

* * *

The dawn comes cold and gray over the ruins. I can't feel it.

My breath clouds white in front of my face as I stand at the edge of what was the Clinic. The fire's gone out—burned itself into exhaustion sometime in the last few hours—but the ashes are still alive with heat. Hundreds of degrees. Hot enough to melt glass, hot enough to fuse steel. I can see the glow of it pulsing beneath the gray surface like the heartbeat of something dying.

I drop to my knees without thinking about it. The impact sends a jolt up through my legs, but I barely register it. My hands hang above the ash, trembling slightly. They're covered in soot already, black streaks running down my wrists, under my shirt sleeves. My knuckles split somewhere during the night—I don't remember when—and there's dried blood caked around the cuts.

Then the change starts.

It always begins the same way. A coldness that has nothing to do with temperature, spreading from deep inside my chest outward. My blood-iron magic responding to something. Fear? Grief? Rage? I don't know anymore which emotion triggers it. Maybe all three at once. The warmth that kept me going through the night—the fever-bright certainty that pushed me through the smoke and flames—dries up like spit on summer pavement.

And then the frost comes.

My skin begins to pale, starting at my fingertips and creeping up toward my elbows. That familiar cobalt hue spreads across my hands like veins of ice beneath the surface. The color deepens until my fingers look carved from frozen moonlight, translucent and deadly cold. But I know better than to trust appearances. This isn't real cold. It's the opposite—a burning chill that protects rather than damages, a magical sheath that lets me touch things that would normally flay the flesh from my bones.

The moment the frost-cobalt coating settles fully over my hands, I plunge them into the ashes.

The heat hits me like a solid wall. Even through the protection, I feel it—a crushing pressure of thermal energy trying to force its way past my defenses. Steam hisses where my skin touches the hottest embers, tiny puffs of white vapor erupting around my fingers as moisture in the ash flashes to gas.

I dig anyway.

My hands move frantically now, scooping and sifting through the debris. Ash cascades between my fingers like black sand, revealing fragments beneath: a twisted lump of aluminum that might have been part of a filing cabinet, now warped into impossible angles; melted plastic fused into glass-like sheets; chunks of insulation turned to sludge.

I find what used to be the IBM ThinkPad first. Or pieces of it. The chassis has collapsed inward on itself, the keyboard melted into an unrecognizable pancake of carbon and silicon. There's still a faint smell coming from it—burnt electronics and scorched polymers—but no glow from any components. Whatever data was on those drives is gone now, reduced to molecular chaos.

Deeper into the wreckage. My breathing gets ragged, each exhale coming harder than the last. The cold inside me spreads further down my arms now, creeping toward my shoulders. My blood-iron magic is pulling inward, retreating from everything except the absolute minimum needed to protect these searching hands.

There. Another piece of tech—the Nextel phone, maybe? It's hard to tell. Just a fused lump of plastic and metal components, all the separate parts welded together by the fire's fury. I pull it out slowly, holding it up to the gray dawn light. Nothing recognizable remains. Just garbage. Worthless slag.

But I keep digging.

The ashes are deeper here, nearer where the main explosion must have occurred. The heat intensifies with every inch I descend into the ruins. More hissing steam. More white puffs erupting around my frozen-blue hands. My knees ache from pressing into the charred earth, but I don't stop. Can't stop.

Another metal fragment. Larger this time—a section of desk support beam, twisted beyond recognition. I toss it aside without looking. Not important. None of it matters unless—

My fingers brush against something different.

Harder than the ash. Smoother. Acrylic, maybe? Some kind of plastic that didn't melt completely in the inferno. Buried deep beneath layers of debris, protected by the collapsed structure above it.

I freeze for just a second, my heart hammering so hard I can feel it in my throat.

Then I dig faster.

Ash flies everywhere as I excavate frantically around the object. More pieces of melted furniture. Chunks of ceiling tile turned to blackened pulp. A shard of what might be window glass, fused into grotesque shapes by the arcane heat.

And there it is.

Barely visible through the haze of settling dust and steam: a dark shape buried in the soot. Scorch-resistant acrylic, definitely. Intact enough that even after everything—the explosion, the fire, the collapse—I can make out its general form.

My hand reaches for it, frost-cobalt skin trembling inches away from the surface.

I pause.

For the first time since I started digging, I actually hesitate. Because part of me—the part that's slowly dying inside like the fire died during the night—that part whispers that some things aren't meant to be recovered. That some ashes are better left undisturbed.

But I've never listened to that voice before.

Not when it mattered anyway.

My fingers close around the object and pull.

The acrylic comes free with a soft tearing sound as old soot clings to it reluctantly. I lift it up toward the dawning light, watching the gray gloom illuminate the surface slowly, revealing—

My breath catches.

Oh god.

I stare at what's in my hand like it's something from another world entirely. Like finding this here makes no sense whatsoever. Like it changes everything or nothing depending on how I look at it.

Which maybe it does.

Or maybe it doesn't.

Hard to say anymore which thoughts are real and which ones are just the shock talking.

I stand up slowly, still holding the scorched acrylic fragment, still coated head-to-toe in ash and soot and blood. The dawn wind cuts across the ruins, bringing with it the smell of burnt wood and worse things underneath.

Behind me, the Clinic is just memory now. Whatever secrets it held—whatever truth about what really happened last night—are buried under tons of debris and confusion.

But I have this.

Whatever it means.

Whether it matters or not.

I turn away from the ashes and start walking back toward where I left the car, frost-cobalt magic fading from my skin as the cold inside me finally reaches critical mass and shatters into numbness.

The acylic piece fits in my palm like a coffin.

Like an answer.

Like another question wearing a different face.

Hard to tell which anymore.

* * *

The fire is dead now. Just ash and steam rising from wet charcoal, the stench of burnt plastic and cooked meat hanging thick in the dawn air. My boots crunch into the soft debris as I move through what used to be The Clinic's eastern wing.

I'm not warm anymore. Haven't been since I watched the roof cave in. The blood-iron magic that keeps my core temperature regulated has pulled back inside, conserving itself, leaving my skin cold despite the residual heat radiating off the ruins. Each breath comes sharp and clear—no fog, no warmth. Just function.

My hands are steady as I sift through the rubble. Methodical. This is what I do when engines seize or transmissions fail. You don't panic. You look for evidence. You diagnose the damage.

Except there aren't any engines here.

Just pieces of chairs. Twisted metal beds. A melted IV stand bent into a pretzel shape. I turn over chunks of drywall with my boot, watching black flakes cascade down like dirty snow. The rain came during the night—I can tell by how the ash clings together, wet and heavy.

That's when I see it.

Half-buried under a collapsed ceiling beam, barely visible beneath a layer of gray sludge, something catches the early light. Not wood, not metal. Plastic. The warped edges of an ID badge, still attached to its lanyard, which has burned away to frayed threads.

My heart stops. Actually fucking stops.

I drop to my knees without thinking, ignoring the heat radiating up from the coals below, and dig it out with bare fingers. Ash gets under my fingernails. Doesn't matter.

It's hers.

The acrylic is clouded white along the edges where the fire touched it, the plastic bubbled and warped. But the photo in the corner is still there. Still *her*. Smiling at some hospital administrator during orientation, her dark hair pulled back, those tired eyes crinkling with professional politeness.

*ELENA REYES - HEAD TRAUMA NURSE*

The name is embossed in raised letters that have survived the heat somehow. Still legible. Still real.

Still *true*.

I hold it between both hands, turning it over slowly. The magnet strip on the back has bubbled and separated from the card. One corner is gone, melted completely away. But it's enough. More than enough.

This isn't conjecture anymore. Isn't assumption or hope or denial. This is proof. Physical. Undeniable.

She was here. She wore this badge. And she died wearing it.

The realization hits me like a hydraulic press—slow, inexorable, crushing every rib inward until I can't breathe. Can't think. Just *know*.

My sister is dead. My mother's already dead. My father's been dead for years except in memory, and even that's fading. Everyone who ever gave a shit about Cole Reyes, everyone who mattered, everyone who made me human instead of just another machine grinding through shifts at Sub-Station Seven—

Gone.

The badge trembles in my grip. I look down at my hands and realize they're shaking. Not from cold. From something deeper. Something fundamental breaking apart inside my chest.

But I don't cry.

Can't. The shock has frozen whatever part of me could produce tears. Instead, there's just this hollow ringing in my skull and a pressure building behind my eyes that feels like it wants to crack bone.

Something snaps.

Not loud. No dramatic sound effect. Just a heavy, metallic *clack* as something essential inside me locks shut. A vault door sealing. A switch being thrown. The difference between before and after so absolute you could measure it with instruments.

I am not Elena's brother right now. That identity burned up with her.

I'm something else.

I clutch the badge to my chest, feeling its heat through my jacket—the last warmth I'll touch today—and close my eyes. The wind picks up, cutting through my clothes like knives. Ash settles on my shoulders, my hair, coating me in gray dust.

When I open my eyes again, everything looks different. Sharper. Colder. The ruins aren't tragedy anymore. They're evidence.

In the distance, beyond the police barricades, a corporate drone in a pressed suit steps out of a black sedan. Elf, by the pointed ears visible beneath his expensive haircut. He's got one of those new PalmPilot devices in his hand, thumb moving across the screen as he takes notes.

Looking at the damage. Assessing liability.

Probably working for whoever paid to burn this place down.

The hatred comes on slow, deliberate, settling into my bones like sediment. Cold and heavy. Permanent.

I stand up, joints popping from staying too long in one position, and carefully fold the badge into my pocket. It presses against my hip—a weight that won't go away.

I walk out of the crater.

Each step is measured. Deliberate. My boots leave prints in the ash, but I don't look back.

I'm not a son anymore. Not a brother.

Just a mechanic who's found a problem that needs fixing.

And I know exactly where to start looking.

## CHAPTER 9: THE THREEFOLD CORD

The chop-shop smells different when I return. Not better, just different. Like copper pennies left out in the rain.

I push through the hanging plastic sheeting—some kind of waterproof tarp that used to cover Uncle Sal's delivery truck before he stopped showing up three months ago—and descend the metal stairs to the sub-basement. Each step rings out too loud, echoing off concrete walls covered in rust stains that look like blood splatters under the single flickering bulb overhead.

Danny is awake inside the Iron-Lung.

His eyes find mine through the glass portal immediately. Those same brown eyes Mom had, wide and dark and full of questions I don't want to answer. The machine makes its rhythmic wheeze behind him—the *thump-hiss* of the bellows driving Telluric pressure into his collapsed lungs, keeping him alive when every law of physics says he should be dead.

"Cole," his voice comes through the speaking tube, muffled and small. "Where's Mom?"

I stop halfway down the last flight of stairs. My hand grips the charred ID badge in my jacket pocket so hard it hurts. The metal is still warm against my palm, singed around the edges from the fire at County General. I can smell it—that chemical stench of burning plastic and cooked meat that won't wash off no matter how many times I scrub my hands.

"I told you to stay quiet, kiddo," I say, my voice flat. Empty. I'm surprised how easy it is to sound this way now. "Conserve your strength."

But Danny isn't listening. He never does. That stubbornness he gets from her—from Mom—is already hardening in him even as his body softens inside the brass cage.

"Why are you looking at me like that?" His head tilts slightly to the left, trying to track my movement as I finish descending the stairs.

I walk past the half-dismantled Buick in the corner, its engine block split open like a ribcage. Past the workbench cluttered with socket wrenches and brake lines. Past everything that used to matter before Tuesday night when the accident happened.

Before Mom died.

I set the charred ID badge down on the workbench next to a greasy socket wrench, watching the way the light catches the melted plastic. Dr. Sarah Chen. County General ER. The woman who tried to save him and couldn't.

"She had to go into hiding," I say, sitting down on a milk crate beside the Iron-Lung. The wood creaks under my weight. I rest my elbows on my knees and look directly through the glass at my little brother's face. "The people who did this... they're still looking for witnesses."

Danny's expression shifts from fear to confusion. His eyes dart away from mine, focusing somewhere past my shoulder. "But... she was here yesterday. When you were—"

"The situation changed." Each word tastes like iron. Like blood. I force myself to hold his gaze. To lie straight into those brown eyes that trust me absolutely. "She sent word this morning. Said it's not safe anymore. That we need to lay low for a while."

*Lay low.* As if this massive brass machine keeping Danny alive counts as laying low. As if anything about our lives will ever be normal again.

The Iron-Lung continues its mechanical breathing behind him. *Thump-hiss. Thump-hiss.* The condensation on the glass is starting to fog over, obscuring parts of his face. Making it easier to pretend.

"You have to promise me something, Danny." My voice doesn't crack. Doesn't break. Somewhere deep inside me, something cold and hard has already crystallized around Mom's death—a frost-cobalt shell that won't let the grief out. Won't let anything out except words. "When people ask questions, you tell them Mom went away on business. That she'll be back soon. Can you do that?"

He nods slowly, but there's hesitation in it now. Doubt creeping in where innocence used to live.

"Promise me," I repeat.

"I promise." Barely a whisper through the speaking tube.

I stand up from the milk crate and walk over to the concrete wall above my workbench. There's still space left there—I can see it clearly now with a kind of terrible clarity. Space for another name. Another sacrifice.

My hand reaches into my jacket pocket and pulls out a grease pencil, its tip worn smooth from use over the years. I uncapped it without thinking, turning back toward the wall.

* * *

The sub-basement air tastes like rust and old diesel fuel, but underneath that industrial tang runs something else now—a crystalline cold that wasn't there before. My breath fogs as I kneel beside Danny's bed, the grease pencil thick and waxy between my fingers like a surgeon's scalpel.

I press it to the concrete wall above where his still body lies in the Iron-Lung's embrace. The first word scrapes free:

**PENANCE**

The sound echoes wrong in here—too loud, too final. The pencil's wax freezes solid against the concrete as frost-cobalt bleeds through my palm, through the tool itself. When I lift it, the letters remain sharp and black and slightly raised, rimmed with ice crystals that catch the red emergency lights.

My hand moves again without thought. Muscle memory from years at the machine shop, from blueprints and schematics, from building things that work even when they shouldn't.

**REVENGE**

Two words sit there on the wall, and I can feel them vibrating in my chest cavity like tuning forks struck by some cosmic hammer. The sub-basement grows colder. The Iron-Lung's gauges tick-tick-tick in their eternal rhythm while Danny remains suspended in that horrible twilight state, neither living nor dead, his lungs full of black iron filings from the men who thought a thirteen-year-old boy was expendable collateral damage.

I write the third word slower, savoring the scrape of wax against stone:

**JUSTICE**

There. Three pillars. A trinity of purpose.

But I stare at that last word—*Justice*—and the grease pencil trembles in my grip. The cold intensifies until my breath comes out in sharp white plumes. Because I see what's coming now. I *understand* what this path actually means.

I cross out "Justice" with a violent slashing motion, shattering the frozen wax into glittering fragments that scatter across the floor like broken stained glass.

Below where I've crossed it out, I write smaller, more precise, the frost-cobalt magic flowing through my fingers until the pencil becomes an extension of bone and will:

*A SLIVER OF JUSTICE*

That's all I'm ever going to get. That's all anyone gets.

The truth hits me like a hydraulic press—a physical blow to the sternum that knocks the air from my lungs. This sliver isn't enough. It won't reverse the damage done to Danny. Won't unburn my mother from the ash pile they called a home. Won't bring back any of the nameless ghosts haunting these North Jersey streets, ground down between the High-Spires' gears and thrown away like machine parts that wore out too soon.

A sliver doesn't fill the void. It can't heal. Can't resurrect. Can't undo.

But it exists.

And zero is less than one, no matter how small.

My reflection stares back at me from the Iron-Lung's chrome casing—gaunt, eyes hollowed by grief that's calcifying into something harder, colder. Something that won't break because it's already shattered completely and reassembled itself around razor wire.

I look at Danny, suspended in time, and I know exactly what this means.

I'll have to become something monstrous to carve even a sliver of justice from men who measure human lives in profit margins and inconvenience. The acts required will be abyssal. I'll cross lines that don't have return tickets. Some of the things I'll do won't just bend the law—they'll snap it clean through and piss on the broken pieces.

The thought should horrify me. Should send me running back to the shop floor, to normalcy, to pretending the world wasn't built on bones and broken dreams.

Instead, I feel nothing but clarity.

No guilt. No doubt. No hesitation.

The last remnants of civilian Cole Reyes evaporate like morning frost under the sun—or rather, like frost that got hammered flat by something too heavy to name. What remains is this: the mechanic who understands leverage and pressure, who knows you sometimes need to apply force beyond what seems reasonable to make anything change.

I stand up slowly, my joints protesting from kneeling so long. The words remain on the wall:

**PENANCE. REVENGE. A SLIVER OF JUSTICE.**

My doctrine. My operating system for whatever comes next.

Behind me, the Iron-Lung keeps ticking its eternal countdown.

* * *

The sub-basement feels colder than it should be. February air filtering through cracks in the foundation, maybe, but I know better. This cold comes from inside.

I'm standing at my workbench, and for the first time since moving into this chop-shop space beneath Sub-Station Seven, I see it clearly—every personal object, every reminder of who Cole Reyes used to be. The Sony MiniDisc player sits on the corner of my desk, its green power light still blinking, some indie rock band's album frozen mid-track. There are three photos thumbtacked to the corkboard above it. Me, Elena, and Danny at Great Adventure last summer. Smiling. Alive.

Dead men don't need memories.

My fingers find the power button on the disc player. Click. The green light dies. Silence crashes over everything like water filling an empty well. I reach up and pull the first photograph down—the one where Danny's losing a tooth and Elena's laughing about something stupid I said. The cardboard backing bends as I fold it in half, then quarters, then shove it hard into the metal drawer beneath my feet.

Danny's watching from behind the reinforced glass partition. His face is pressed against it, breath fogging the surface. Through the distortion, I can make out his expression. Fear. Good. He needs to understand what's happening here.

"Cole?" His voice comes through the speaker system, tinny and distant despite being only thirty feet away. "You're scaring me."

I don't turn around. Can't. If I look at him now, if I really see those eyes—the same eyes as mine—I might hesitate. And hesitation gets you killed in this world. Gets people you love killed.

"Good," I say, my voice flat, mechanical. "If I scare you, imagine what I'll do to them."

The second photo comes down next—Elena teaching Danny how to change a tire on her Beetle, both of them covered in grease and grins. Fold. Quarter. Drawer. The third follows immediately after. Our whole life, compressed into confetti, buried beneath spare parts and ammunition.

My workbench is still cluttered with evidence of my former self. A coffee mug with "World's Best Brother" written in fading marker. A half-finished model car kit. Loose change. A deck of cards. All the detritus of a normal guy living a normal life.

None of that exists anymore.

I sweep everything off with one motion of my arm. The crash of porcelain and plastic and metal echoes through the sub-basement like breaking bones. Danny flinches behind the glass, and I feel something twist inside my chest—but it's not pain exactly. It's more like watching someone else's emotions through thick ice.

I pull open all three drawers of my tool chest and begin sorting. Screwdrivers go in the first. Wrenches in the second. Third drawer is already full of weapons maintenance kits—cleaning rods, bore brushes, oils, solvents. My hands move with practiced efficiency, each item finding its proper place.

From the corner of my eye, I see the rune-etched MP5 submachine gun leaning against the wall where I left it last night after coming back from the Kearny Marshes. Blood on the barrel. Not mine. I lift it now, feeling its weight settle into familiar grooves in my hands. This is what matters. Tools for survival. Instruments of vengeance.

The weapon finds its new home on my workbench, centered, ready to be disassembled and cleaned. Beside it, I place the blueprints for the Iron-Lung—my masterwork, the armor that will let me breathe in places others suffocate. The paper crackles as I smooth it flat.

There. That's better. Everything unnecessary has been removed. Only purpose remains.

"Cole, please," Danny says again, his voice higher now, cracking. "What are you doing? Why won't you look at me?"

Still I don't turn. Because if I do, I'll see his face, and Elena's face, and Mom's face, all overlapping, and I'll remember why any of this matters. Love. Family. Human connection.

Weakness.

"I'm doing what needs to be done," I tell him, pulling out a rag and weapon oil, beginning the methodical process of breaking down the MP5. Click-click-whirr—the bolt release. Slide the charging handle. Field strip. Each piece laid out in order: upper receiver, lower receiver, trigger mechanism, barrel assembly.

The cold in the room deepens. My breath comes out in visible puffs now, though I know the temperature hasn't actually changed. This cold is magical, alchemical, born from blood-iron resonance crystallizing in my veins. Where once there was warmth—passion, rage, grief, love—I feel nothing but stillness. Dead-silent frost-cobalt.

I should be terrified. Some part of me understands this transformation is wrong, dangerous, a violation of whatever human essence makes us more than animals or machines. But terror requires emotion, and emotions are what I'm excavating from myself like cancerous tumors.

Danny's crying behind the glass now. I can hear it in the wet catching of his breath, see it in the way his shoulders shake. Good. He needs to understand that the brother he knew died with Elena. What's left is something else entirely.

Something necessary.

"Cole Reyes is gone," I say aloud, not to Danny, but to myself—to whoever or whatever I'm becoming. "He died on February 14th, 2000."

I look down at my hands as they work, assembling and disassembling the submachine gun with mechanical precision. The veins beneath my skin have lost their warmth. No more pulsing red-gold warmth flowing toward my heart, no more heat radiating from my palms when I grip tools or weapons.

Just stillness.

Terrifying, perfect stillness.

And underneath it all, deeper than thought, runs one truth like a river through frozen ground:

*They took everything from me.*

*Now I'll take everything from them.*

## CHAPTER 10: FROST-COBALT

I climbed the concrete steps from the sub-basement, my boots echoing in the hollow stairwell. The smell hit me first—motor oil, transmission fluid, the metallic tang of rust sleeping in scrap metal. The chop-shop was quiet this morning. Too quiet. Like it was holding its breath.

The main garage sprawled before me, a cathedral of broken machinery and stolen parts. Fluorescent tubes flickered overhead, casting everything in that sickly yellow-green light that made the oil stains look like pools of infected blood. I'd spent enough nights down here to know every creak and groan of the building's skeleton.

My eyes found the V8 engine block immediately. It hung suspended from a chain hoist near the center of the space, swaying slightly from residual air movement. Cast iron, rusted through, probably pulled from some flooded sedan out in the marshes. Good weight to it. Solid.

I walked over, my reflection fragmenting across puddles of old coolant. The rust on that engine block was thick—years of neglect crystallized into orange-red flakes. My bare hand hovered over the rough surface, feeling the temperature differential even without touching. Cold metal, warmer air, the whole dance of thermodynamics waiting for a catalyst.

Time to test what's changed inside me.

I pressed my palm against the rusted cast-iron.

The resonance came instantly, but wrong. Not the warm rush I remembered, that flush of heat that preceded the spark-bloom of welding torch flame. Instead, something else entirely. A coldness spread through my fingers, up my wrist, settling in my chest like a slab of dry ice. The magic didn't pulse or crackle. It hummed—a dead-silent vibration that felt less like fire and more like the moment before a blade breaks skin.

Frost-cobalt.

The name just came to me, clear as steel forged in shadow. I watched as an eerie blue glow emanated from where my hand met metal. The rust around my palm began to crystallize, forming delicate frost patterns that spread across the engine block's surface with geometric precision. Each crystal formation was perfect, mathematical, merciless.

Then the rust simply flaked away. Not melted by heat, not scraped off by force, but frozen brittle and falling like snow. The metal beneath was exposed, clean, unblemished. The cast iron itself had taken on a faint metallic sheen, as if newly machined.

And the temperature.

Jesus Christ, the temperature dropped exactly ten degrees in the entire garage. I could feel it on my skin, see it in the sudden condensation forming on every metal surface. My breath plumed into white vapor before my face, small clouds dissipating in air that had gone from mild to bitterly cold in seconds.

Ten degrees. Not nine. Not eleven. Exactly ten.

I lifted my hand slowly, staring at my palm. No burns, no blisters, no marks whatsoever. Just clean skin, though it should have been reddened from what I'd channeled through it. But there was nothing. Cold to the touch now, almost clammy.

I felt... nothing.

No rush of adrenaline. No heat in my blood. No passion or rage fueling the magic. Just cold. Mechanical precision. My heart beat steady and slow. My breathing remained even. It was like watching someone else perform the magic, observing it with clinical detachment while I stood here wearing my own skin.

This is the state, I thought. The abyssal doctrine made real. Zero doubt means zero heat. Zero guilt means zero passion. What's left when you strip all that away?

An executioner's tool.

The realization didn't frighten me. Should've. Probably would've, six months ago. But standing here in this cold garage with frost-cobalt magic crystallizing in the air around me, I understood something fundamental about what I'd become. Doubt was friction. Guilt was resistance. Remorse was dead weight.

I'd shed them all.

The rolling metal door rattled violently from the alleyway—three heavy knocks that echoed through the garage like hammer strikes on a coffin lid.

* * *

The button clicks under my thumb. Metal groans against metal as the bay door rises, flooding the garage with February sunlight that cuts through the grease-stained air like a knife.

Bram Kovacs steps inside, carrying a wooden crate that rattles with brass fittings. He's got that dwarven solidness about him—broad shoulders, calloused hands the size of hams, his leather apron dark with decades of machine shop stains. Local 405 South Side union written across the chest pocket in faded white paint.

He stops three steps in.

"Christ," he mutters, exhaling clouds into air that shouldn't be this cold. His eyes narrow at me, then dart around the garage. "What the hell happened to your heating? It dropped ten degrees just walking through that door."

I don't answer. Can't answer without saying something that would make it real. Elena is dead. Elena burned. Elena screaming.

My right hand finds the workbench edge. The frost-cobalt sits heavy in my bones now, locked permanent. No doubt left to bargain with. No guilt to compromise its purity. Just the humming silence underneath my skin where warmth used to live.

Bram sets down the crate. The brass fittings clink inside. He pulls off thick work gloves, shoving them into his apron pocket while studying my face like he's reading blueprints.

"You look like shit, kid." His gravel-voice softer now. "And you're standing there like a corpse at a wake. What happened?"

"The clinic burned." The words come out flat. Wrong. Like I'm reading someone else's dialogue. "Elena didn't make it."

The temperature drops another degree. Bram notices, shifting his weight, rubbing his forearms.

"That's... Jesus, Cole, I'm sorry. I know she meant something to you."

"I don't want sympathy."

His jaw tightens. Good. He understands. "Right. Fair enough." He gestures at the crate. "These are the custom fittings for the pressure regulator. Should fit Danny's lung assembly perfect. But if you need anything more than standard medical grade—"

"I do." My fingers drum against the bench. "I need Telluric pressure-valves. Illegal ones. High-capacity."

Bram goes still. Really still. The kind of stillness that means he's calculating odds and consequences and probably praying to whatever dwarven forge-gods he believes in.

## CHAPTER 11: THE BLACK-CIRCUIT CONTRACT

Dead line.

"They came for us three weeks ago," he says, voice hoarse. When he breathes, I can see his ribs through torn fabric. "Said our contracts were terminated. Said we'd been... reassigned." He touches the collar with his free hand, and the red LED pulses harder. "This is what happens when you try to organize. This is what happens when you demand benefits."

Fuck.

My frost-cobalt magic pulls moisture from the air, forming crystalline patterns around my shoulders. The cold helps me think. Always has.

These aren't corporate thralls brainwashed into aggression. These are discarded employees. Replaced by cheaper labor or automation or just plain malicious downsizing. And instead of severance packages and unemployment benefits, Sterling-Voss had equipped them with shock-collars and dumped them in the sewers.

To die slowly.

Or be used as a threat against anyone else who might consider joining a union.

A massive shadow parts the crowd.

I raise my MP5 instinctively, but something stops me. Size maybe. The sheer fucking *mass* of whoever's moving through there.

Six-foot-eight if he's an inch. Orc. Muscles like railroad ties wrapped in scarred green skin, chemical burns creating white patches across his arms and chest. He moves with a predator's economy—every motion efficient despite obvious weakness from malnutrition.

His eyes lock on mine.

And I recognize him.

"Grimjaw?" The name comes out before I can stop it.

The Orc's face twists in surprise that quickly shifts to suspicion. His gaze drops to my weapon, then back to my face. Recognition flickers there too.

"Cole Reyes," he growls, voice like grinding gears. "Didn't expect to see your ass down here."

* * *

The air hits me like a wet towel soaked in motor oil and rot. I step off the maintenance ladder into ankle-deep sludge, the frost-cobalt already spreading from my boots in crystalline patterns across the surface. Blue-black ice cracks beneath my weight as I chamber a round in the MP5.

"Hell of a welcome committee," I mutter, breath misting in the cold I'm projecting.

The sewer junction spreads out before me—Junction 4A according to the rusted marker bolted to the wall. Three tunnels branch off like arteries from some diseased heart. The main chamber is maybe forty feet across, concrete walls weeping moisture that runs in dark rivers toward the center grate. Overhead fluorescents flicker and buzz, casting everything in sickly yellow light that makes the puddles look like pools of liquid gold.

Then I hear it—the metallic *clink-clink-clink* of chains dragging across concrete.

My finger tightens on the trigger guard. The frost spreads wider, freezing the toxic water with a sound like breaking glass. Ice forms in intricate fractals around my position, creating a barrier between me and whoever's coming.

They emerge from the eastern tunnel.

First one steps into the light—a massive Orc, easily seven feet tall, shoulders like a linebacker but wrong somehow. The green-gray skin has this black-iron discoloration creeping up his neck, similar to what's killing Danny. His jaw juts forward too far, teeth filed to points, but there's intelligence in those amber eyes. He wears tattered coveralls, dirty work boots, and around his neck—a heavy shock collar with blinking red LEDs.

Behind him come more figures. Smaller ones skittering in the shadows. Goblins, I think, though these are different from any street trash I've seen above ground. Their flesh looks melted, reshaped by something terrible. One has three arms. Another's head is too small for its body, eyes bulging. They all wear the same collars.

And then the real trouble.

Two Ogre-Kin lumber out behind them. Ten feet tall if they're an inch, hulking masses of muscle and malformed bone. The calcification is worse on them—thick veins of black iron running up their arms like tree roots, hardening their flesh into armor. They're chained together at the ankles, dragging fifty pounds of steel behind them as they move.

The Orc leader stops fifteen feet away. Chains rattle as he crosses his arms.

"Well shit," he says, voice like gravel scraping concrete. Pure North Jersey accent, no fantasy bullshit. "They sent another one down here."

I don't lower my weapon. "You Grimjaw?"

His lips curl back from those filed teeth. "Depends who's asking."

"The guy standing between you and getting ventilated." I keep my aim center-mass, MP5 steady despite the dampness seeping through my boots. "So yeah. Who else would it be?"

Grimjaw shifts his weight, chains clinking. His eyes track left to right, measuring me up. Behind him, the goblins have stopped moving, clustered around a drainage pipe. The ogres breathe heavily, steam rising from their mouths in the cold I'm making.

"They flushed that poison down here January," Grimjaw says, talking like we're shooting the shit over coffee. Like we're not in hell's waiting room with shock collars around our necks. "Wanted to see what'd happen when metahuman biology hit the sludge. Me and my pack got grabbed off the South Side, thrown down here like lab rats."

He taps his collar. Red light reflects in the puddles around us.

"This is the petri dish. They wanted to see who'd melt fast and who'd hold shape. Then they put these on us so we couldn't leave. Keep the evidence contained."

I notice something then—the calcification on his chest, spreading up his neck. Same pattern I saw on Danny at County General.

"How long?" My voice comes out harder than I intended.

"Since January? Or since we started hardening?" He laughs without humor. "Doesn't matter. Sooner or later, this iron shit kills you. Turns you into living statues until your heart stops beating."

The ogres shift behind him, chains dragging across concrete. One of them growls low in its throat—a sound like grinding garbage disposals.

"So what are you here for, mechanic?" Grimjaw asks. "Vargas send you? Here to collect a paycheck?"

My radio crackles. Vargas's voice cuts through:

"Reyes. Sensors show you're in the central hub. Detonate the collars and clear the junction, or I want my valves back."

Grimjaw's amber eyes narrow.

"You hear that?" he asks quietly. "That's your boss. The guy who wants us dead."

I don't answer. Can't answer. Because I'm looking at the black iron creeping up Grimjaw's neck and seeing Danny's x-rays superimposed over it. Seeing my little brother dying in some hospital room while men like Vargas dump poison in tunnels and call it business.

"I didn't say I was with Vargas," I tell him.

"No?" Grimjaw tilts his head. "Then why you down here, mechanic?"

I think about the frost-cobalt spreading from me. About how cold this place is getting. About how ice might not be enough.

But it's a start.

"My name's Cole," I say. "And I'm here because someone needs to answer for what happened here."

## CHAPTER 13: BLOOD-DEBT IN THE SEWERS

The Nextel sits heavy in my palm, its weight nothing compared to what hangs in the balance. Water drips somewhere deeper in Junction 4A—the sound echoes off concrete and rusted pipe like a countdown timer I can't stop watching.

Grimjaw's amber eyes track me through the dim light. His knuckles white around that rebar club, veins standing out on muscled forearms scarred from worse than any sewer rat could manage. Behind him, the Dwarves shift their weight, axes still raised but trembling—whether from cold or fear, hard to say. The Elves haven't moved since they dropped their weapons, those collars still blinking their red death-rhythm at regular intervals.

I key the radio. Static hisses across the speaker.

"Reyes here," I say, keeping my voice flat. Professional. Like I'm not staring down six people who look exactly like Danny did before County General took him apart piece by piece. Same hollow-eyed desperation. Same tremor of muscles fighting against something that lives inside them now. "I've got visual confirmation on the package."

Vargas' voice crackles back, distant as if coming from under water. "Good. Proceed with termination. Repeat, terminate all targets."

My thumb hovers over the talk button. The pack watches me, waiting for the word that means their end. Grimjaw doesn't even blink.

*Blood-debt.*

I think about Danny waking up screaming because the iron shards were moving under his skin. About how he'd claw at his own arms until they bled just to feel something real. About the way his eyes would go vacant when the black-circuit addiction hit hardest—that same glassy distance I see in these faces now.

The Nextel feels hot suddenly. Or maybe it's my hand that's turning to ice.

"You know what happens if you don't do this?" Vargas continues, mistaking my silence for hesitation. "The Black-Circuit contract voids. Those valves we discussed? They walk. Your whole neighborhood goes dry. Every pipe bursts. You want to explain that to your neighbors?"

"I'm looking at them right now." My voice sounds wrong—distant even to my own ears. I let go of the talk button and lower the radio slowly toward the sewage water below our platform.

Grimjaw's club comes up faster than thought itself.

"Don't," he growls, but there's something underneath it. Not threat—plea maybe. Or resignation.

I toss the Nextel anyway. It splashes into the sludge with a sound like breaking glass, and then silence.

The red LEDs keep blinking. One-two-three-four-five-six. A metronome counting down to nothing.

"I can't shoot them off," I say, not taking my eyes from Grimjaw's collar. The circuitry is simple enough once you look past all the proprietary bullshit—they built these things cheap on purpose, mass-produced fear-wearables for the lowest bidder. "Tamper-triggers. Kill-switches. If I hit them hard enough to break, they'll fry whatever neural network's holding these bodies together."

"Then what the fuck are you waiting for?" Grimjaw spits the words out like broken teeth. "You gonna stand there admiring your handiwork while those things reset?"

My hands come up between us—bare palms, frost already forming along my knuckles in crystalline patterns that catch the dim light and throw back blue-black sparks.

"Hold still," I tell him.

He doesn't move but his breathing changes. Gets shallower. Dangerous.

"My little brother drank this same sludge," I say quietly. "He's drowning in black iron right now. In some County General ward where they're teaching him how to be someone else's weapon." My voice cracks on the last word but my hands don't shake. "So yeah, Grimjaw. I'm gonna stand here. And I'm going to do this the only way it can actually work without killing you."

The frost spreads across my fingers. The air temperature plummets.

Behind me, water starts freezing at the edges of our platform—solid ice forming with audible pops as it expands into every crack and crevice of concrete.

* * *

My fingers find Grimjaw's collar first—the rusted iron band cinched tight around his throat, wires snaking through corroded contacts like tumors. The explosive charge embedded in the locking mechanism pulses with a sick yellow light, counting down seconds I can't see but feel like wasps under my skin.

I press my palms against the metal.

The cold comes instantaneous and absolute.

Frost spreads across the collar in crystalline fractals, each branch precise as surgical incision. Behind me, I hear the collective intake of breath—forty voices holding air in forty terrified lungs. The temperature plummets. Water begins to crackle into ice beneath our feet, black and glass-smooth. Frost creeps up the walls of Junction 4A, consuming everything in crystalline death.

But on the collar itself, where my skin makes contact, the opposite happens.

Heat.

Blue-white heat that doesn't burn because it exists outside combustion, beyond fire entirely—it's the coldness of space made solid, and when it touches the lock mechanism, the iron screams. Not audibly. Somewhere deeper than ears. It liquifies instantly, running like mercury, dripping onto the frozen water in beads of blue-black slag that *hiss* when they hit.

The collar opens.

Splashes away into darkness below the ice.

Grimjaw stumbles forward, gasping, clawing at his neck where raw red welts already forming. His eyes meet mine—yellow irises wide, pupils blown wide enough to swallow all that amber. He sees what I am now. What the South Side forges.

"Local 405," I say, moving to the next prisoner before he can respond. A goblin girl, maybe fourteen by human standards, her collar so tight it's sunk into green-tinted flesh. Her hands are bound behind her back with zip ties that cut into wrists.

I touch her collar.

The same ritual: absolute zero spreading outward like a shockwave, freezing sewer water into geometric patterns of impossible mathematics. Then that terrible precision-heat focused down to a point no bigger than a pinprick, burning through the lock while leaving the explosive charge untouched, inert, harmless as a paperweight.

*Snap.* The collar falls. She collapses forward, sobbing.

Three more. Four more. I'm moving now on instinct, barely conscious of individual faces—a dwarf whose beard is singed black around the edges, an elf whose pointed ear has been torn half off, another goblin whose left eye socket weeps pus from some surgical enhancement gone wrong.

Twenty-two collars gone before the first one burns my thumb bad enough to draw blood.

The temperature keeps dropping.

Everyone's breath comes out in thick white fog now, each exhale crystallizing in the air before it disperses. The ambient light from failed electronics and dying batteries casts everything in sickly blue-green shadows. My hands are shaking—not from fear but from exertion. Each application of frost-cobalt magic is like balancing a knife-edge between two infinities, and my arms remember every second of it.

Thirty-four collars down.

Six remaining.

A Local 405 dwarf rubs his neck where the collar left raw red grooves, staring at me like I've just pulled a rabbit from a hat that also happened to be made of pure nightmare fuel. "You're union, ain't you kid?" His voice cracks on the words. "Only a South Side machinist got hands that steady."

I don't answer. Just nod once, sharp, and move to the next target—an orc with tusks filed down to nubs, face tattooed with circuitry patterns that might have meant something once upon a time.

My hand touches his collar.

Freeze.

Melt.

Release.

As he stumbles away, coughing up water and worse things, Grimjaw steps forward into the space I've cleared. He pulls a jagged piece of shrapnel from somewhere—maybe his pocket, maybe his belt, maybe he'd been carrying it in his teeth—and draws it across his own palm in one smooth motion.

Dark blood wells immediately, dripping onto the ice, staining it crimson-black.

* * *

The water beneath my boots had gone still and glassy, frozen solid in sheets that cracked and groaned under the weight of standing bodies. Steam rose where Grimjaw's bleeding hand hung suspended between us—a crimson fist against the impossible winter I'd called down from my chest.

"Blood-debt," he growled, his voice like gravel dragged across concrete. The scarred orange hide of his knuckles glistened dark and wet. Behind him, the assembled survivors of Underground Junction 4A stood motionless, their varied forms casting no reflections on the ice. Goblins with their too-wide eyes. A troll whose shoulders scraped the tunnel ceiling even hunched. Two more orcs, twins by the look of their matching battle-scar patterns, both carrying weapons salvaged from who-knows-where.

I looked at that offered hand. Looked past it to the discarded collars scattered across the sewer floor—those chrome-and-plastic restraints that had marked them as property. Some were crushed flat, others split open along their locking mechanisms where teeth or claws had wrenched them free. Dead mechanical snakes coiled in the filth.

"You gave us our necks back, Reyes." Grimjaw's voice dropped lower, intimate despite its volume. "You point, we break it. That's the deal. Lifelong."

The frost-cobalt magic hummed in my veins, eager and hungry for expression. I could feel it pressing against my ribs, wanting out, wanting to transform this whole flooded junction into a cathedral of ice and judgment. But I didn't let it flow. Not yet.

"Your name's not Cole?" one of the goblin twins squeaked up from the pack. His companion shushed him sharply, but the question hung in the frozen air anyway.

"It is now." I met Grimjaw's gaze dead-on. "And you're all working. Starting tonight."

The massive orc's lips pulled back in something that might've been a smile if smiles weren't so far from his natural range of expressions. He stepped closer, his hand still extended, still bleeding onto the ice between us.

"I'm taking your offer," he said simply. "Whatever that means. We're yours. Pack law."

My fingers closed around his wrist—not the handshake he'd offered, but the grip of someone checking pulse points and pressure zones out of pure instinct. The muscle beneath his skin was dense and alive, the pulse strong and steady despite everything. No fever, no infection from the collar wounds marring his shoulders.

"You're going to freeze solid if I don't get us moving." I pressed my free hand flat against his palm, feeling the heat radiating from his flesh even as my own skin began to numb with residual cold. "There's a garage on the surface. North Kearny. You'll know it when you see it—it's the one with the rusted-out Buick sitting three feet off the ground on cinderblocks."

I released his hand, stepping back toward the ladder leading topside. The iron rungs were already rimmed with frost creeping down from where they disappeared through the manhole cover above.

"Vargas." The name tasted like copper on my tongue. "There's a suit named Vargas who thinks I died in the collapse. Let's go disappoint him."

Grimjaw moved with surprising grace for someone his size, gathering up the scattered collars with mechanical efficiency and dropping them into a pile at the base of the ladder. Symbolic. Final. Then he turned back to his pack—his people—and barked something in a guttural language that made the goblin twins flinch and the troll straighten to attention.

They fell into formation behind me without another word. Four heavily armed metahumans arranged themselves into a loose perimeter, weapons ready, eyes scanning the tunnel ahead and behind. Grimjaw took position at my left shoulder, his massive frame casting shadows across the ice even in the dim light filtering through gaps in the sewer grates above.

The manhole cover shuddered overhead—a sound like a bell tolling deep underwater.

I pulled my MP5 from its sling, the weight familiar and grounding despite everything. My finger found the safety, flicked it off, settled into the trigger guard.

"This is going to get loud," I said, more to myself than anyone else. "Stay close. Move fast. And if I tell you to run, you fucking run."

Another shudder ran through the metal above us. Closer now. Footsteps? Vehicle traffic? Or something worse?

Grimjaw reached past me toward the ladder first, his claws finding purchase in the frozen rungs and tearing chunks of ice free as he began the climb. The others followed in perfect sequence, their movements practiced and efficient. Military drills run until they became muscle memory.

I went last.

My boots broke through the ice sheet as I pushed off, sending fracturing patterns radiating outward across the frozen surface. The water beneath groaned and surged, already beginning to thaw without my magic holding it down. By the time I grabbed the lowest rung, steam was rising in thick clouds around my legs.

The manhole cover exploded inward.

Concrete dust and wet debris rained down on the assembled pack below. Someone screamed—a human voice, female, cut short by an impact that made me wince even through the din.

"Reyes! COLE REYES!" A voice I recognized. Carlos Vargas's voice, amplified through a bullhorn. "You're under arrest for—"

Gunfire cut him off. Automatic weapons chatter echoed through the sewer junction, bouncing off walls that suddenly felt much too close and confined.

I hit the street level running, my MP5 coming up in one smooth motion as I took stock of the scene unfolding above. Three black Escalades arranged in a loose perimeter around the open manhole. Tactical teams in full gear spilling out of the vehicles, their weapons trained on the opening.

And standing in the center of it all, hands cupped around his mouth to project over the chaos: Carlos Vargas himself. Suited and polished despite the grime of the street, his hair perfectly styled, his expression caught between triumph and fury.

"You can't hide anymore, Reyes! You're a murderer! An anarchist!"

"Fucking hell," I muttered, leveraging myself fully onto solid ground. Grimjaw's hand shot out, catching my forearm and hauling me clear of the manhole with enough force to make my shoulder pop.

The tactical team's aim shifted instantly toward us—toward *him*. Toward the seven-foot-orange-skinned metahuman whose claws were still dripping sewer water onto the asphalt.

The first rounds went wide. The second burst clipped Grimjaw's shoulder, sending him stumbling back into the manhole's edge. Blood sprayed across the concrete in an arc.

That's when all hell truly broke loose.

The troll lunged from the manhole with a roar that shook windows in nearby buildings. His fist connected with the nearest tactical officer's chest plate hard enough to send the man flying into his squad-mates like bowling pins. The goblin twins emerged firing, their weapons some combination of scavenged military hardware and improvised modifications that made them look more alien than anything standard issue.

## CHAPTER 14: THE CHOP-SHOP EXECUTION

The black-circuit shop sits dead quiet beneath the South Side neon hollow, all striped Crown Vics gutted and waiting like sacrificial altars. No music. No workers. Just the low hum of an IBM ThinkPad cutting through the stillness, that distinct whine of the 600 series that means someone's running numbers.

I know those numbers are mine.

Vargas thinks I'm sewer meat. Thinks the Junction swallowed me whole along with everything else he sent down there. His mistake. My advantage.

The rolling doors grind shut above—heavy steel on steel—but it's not him working them. It's me. The tracks lock with blue-black ice that shouldn't exist in April, shouldn't exist anywhere above the flood tunnels. The frost spreads slow and hungry up the windows of the Crown Vics, creeping over chrome and shattered tail lights.

The fluorescent tubes overhead sputter once. Twice.

Then they die.

Orange sodium-vapor bleeds through the skylights now, painting everything in sickly hospital light. The motor oil on the floor catches it, makes the concrete shine wet and dark as arterial blood.

"Vargas," I call out, my voice flat in the sudden quiet. "Let's do business."

No answer at first. Just the ThinkPad's hum and the distant thrum of the Pulaski Skyway traffic, muffled by distance and earth.

Then movement behind the office glass—the shadow of a man rising from his chair.

"You're late, Reyes." Vargas steps into view, silhouetted against the screen glow, one hand already moving toward the desk drawer where he keeps the .38. "Or maybe you're early for your own funeral. Hard to tell when you look like shit warmed over."

He's right about one thing—I look worse than hell. Blood crusts my knuckles. Something wet and dark has soaked through my jacket on the left side, though most of it isn't mine. The gash across my jaw still weeps sluggish red.

But I'm standing. And he's not.

"I had a long talk with Grimjaw down there," I say, taking another step forward. My boots *clack* loud on the concrete—no rushing this. Let him hear me coming. Let him think he has time to reach that gun. "About how you run a chop-shop but can't keep a fucking secret."

His hand freezes on the drawer handle. "Grimjaw's dead. They're all dead. You saw what I sent down there."

"My handler told me a lot of things." Another step. Close enough now to see the sweat beading on his upper lip despite the cold creeping through the shop. The frost is halfway up the nearest Crown Vic's windows. "Problem is, handlers lie."

Vargas pulls out the .38—old model, probably stolen from some cop in '97—and levels it at my chest. "Stop right there. You don't know who you're dealing with, mechanic. This whole district—"

"You're dead weight," I interrupt, voice still flat as rusted machinery. "The Syndicate wants you gone. The sewer ghouls want you gone. Even your crown vics want you gone—they're half-frozen solid just being near me."

That last part catches him. His eyes flick to the frost-covered chrome, then back to my face. He sees something there. Not fear. Something colder.

"Ghouls don't talk," he says, but his voice wavers now, that arrogance cracking like thin ice over deep water.

"Oh, but they do when you've got something they want." I stop about ten feet away, close enough for the shot to be clean if he makes it, far enough that I'm already beyond where bullets matter anymore. "Seems everyone agrees you need replacing. Efficiency. Profit margins. The usual reasons."

Behind Vargas, through the office window, I can see the manifest glowing on the ThinkPad screen. Digital numbers scrolling past. Inventory lists. Transfer orders. All those cars waiting to be rebirthed into something clean and legitimate, their VINs stripped and sold to whoever pays cash.

All those cars that will never roll out of here under his watch.

His finger tightens on the trigger. "You think you're scary? Some vigilante with a grudge?"

I smile, and even I feel how wrong it looks. No warmth in it. Just teeth showing.

"I think you're surrounded."

* * *

The chop-shop breathes its last dying breath around me—a rattling gasp of hydraulics and cooling metal. Steam rises from the grates in ghostly columns, backlit by the sick neon of Vargas's domain. Somewhere beneath us, in those submerged catacombs where the machine-worshippers chant their prayers to rust and oil, the machinery has fallen silent.

Vargas doesn't know yet. He sits in his office above it all, surrounded by monitors showing nothing but static and snow, his kingdom crumbling stone by stone into the Newark Bay mud below. He thinks he's safe up here. He thinks the glass between us—that expensive safety glass reinforced against bullets and explosions—will keep him alive just a little while longer.

He's wrong about everything except the last part.

"You did good work down there," I call through the partition, my voice flat and empty as February tombstone frost. My hands hang loose at my sides, still dripping black water onto the concrete floor. The temperature in this room has dropped twenty degrees since I came topside. Ice crystals spider-web across the steel beams overhead. "You got what you wanted. The manifest. The data. All of it."

I take one step toward the office. Then another.

Vargas's head snaps up from whatever ledger he'd been pretending to study. His eyes widen—just slightly, barely perceptible—as he reads something on my face. Something that wasn't there before. The frost-cobalt magic isn't just cold anymore. It's *hungry*. And it looks out through these eyes like a predator who's finally found its prey after weeks of tracking.

"Reyes." His name for me sounds strangled, uncertain. He's pulling something from his desk drawer now, fumbling with the metal latch. "You're confused. You're—"

The heavy pistol clears the drawer with a metallic scrape. His hand shakes as he levels it at me through the glass. His finger tightens on the trigger.

*Click.*

The bullet kicks out of the barrel—hot lead spinning through air thick with suspended ice crystals. Time slows. I watch the slug trace its arc toward my chest, see the distorted blur of its passage through freezing atmosphere. 

My right hand rises, fingers splayed. The frost-cobalt surges through my veins, pouring out through my palm in a wave of absolute-zero energy. The bullet hits an invisible wall three inches from my sternum and simply... stops. Suspended. Frozen mid-flight by kinetic energy locked solid in a lattice of crystalline time.

It drops.

*Clink.*

The frozen projectile hits the concrete and skitters across the floor like a dropped icicle.

Vargas stares. His gun arm lowers by half an inch, muscle control failing under the weight of impossible physics. "What... what are you?"

"I'm what you made me," I say, taking another step forward. My boot crunches on broken glass—shards of safety partitioning that shattered when I caught his bullet. "I'm Danny's brother. I'm every kid you chained to those machines. I'm this city, Vargas. And tonight, I'm your goddamn reckoning."

My fist slams into the remaining office glass.

The reinforced panel spider-webs instantly, then explodes inward in a shower of diamond-sharp fragments. Alarms begin shrieking somewhere behind me—motion sensors registering the breach—but I'm already moving through the frame, shoulder-first, scattering debris across his mahogany desk.

He fires again. Twice. Three times.

Each bullet freezes in mid-air before it reaches me, hanging there like suspended stars in a cold black sky. I don't even look at them anymore as they fall one by one, clinking against the floor like coins dropping into a well.

"Stop." The word comes out wrong—too desperate, too human. Vargas is scrambling backward now, knocking his leather chair over onto its side. Blood runs down his forearm where glass cut him. "Please, Cole. We can make a deal. You want money? I've got cash. Cryptocurrency. Bearer bonds that'll never be traced."

I reach down and scoop up the IBM ThinkPad from his desk—the one containing the digital manifest, all those names and locations detailing the Sludge flush into Newark Bay. My fingers close around the warm plastic. Data security worth more than gold. More than life.

"I don't want your money," I say flatly, sliding the laptop under my arm. With my other hand, I palm a thick stack of hundred-dollar bills from the corner of his desk—the balance he owed me for the chop-shop run. It's not about the debt anymore. Wasn't ever really about the debt.

Vargas's breathing comes in ragged gasps now, panicked and wet-sounding. "The Black-Circuit... you understand what happens if you do this? They'll send everyone after you. Everyone."

"They're already coming." I pull back the slide on my rune-etched MP5 with my thumb, chambering a round. The barrel glows with that same blue-black frost-light, absolute zero given form and purpose. "You just made it personal, Carlos. Before, you were just another cog in the machine. Now you're the reason Danny can't breathe without drowning."

"Reyes..."

"You poisoned my brother." Each word falls like ice into hot water—crisp, sharp, final. "You chained these people like dogs and fed them to your engines. Killing you won't fix Danny's lungs. It won't bring back what you took from this neighborhood. It's not enough."

My finger settles on the trigger guard.

"But it's a sliver of justice." The gun rises slowly, deliberately, until the glowing muzzle points between Vargas's wide-staring eyes. "And that'll do for tonight."

His mouth opens but no sound comes out. Just air. Just fear given shape.

I squeeze.

* * *

The MP5K rips through my hands, three rounds punching into Vargas's sternum before he completes his lunge. The depleted-bismuth slugs tear through his chest like paper, each impact throwing him backward against the cinderblock wall. Blood erupts from his mouth in a scarlet fountain, spattering across the corporate manifests pinned there—the very contracts that had sealed the South Side's death warrant.

He slides down slowly, coughing frogs of blood onto the concrete. His eyes are wide, disbelief freezing in them alongside the life draining from his body. I don't give him time for final words. Don't give him time for anything but oblivion.

I step forward, placing my palm flat against his face. Frost-cobalt floods through my fingers, racing across his skin like mercury spreading across glass. I feel the heat of his dying breath against my hand, feel the desperate struggle as his lungs seize. Ice crystals form in his nostrils, his tear ducts, his open mouth. He makes a strangled sound, then nothing.

The silence after is absolute.

Grimjaw stands in the doorway, the pack crowded behind him. They watch me straighten up, watch me step over what used to be Vargas without so much as a glance downward. My boots leave wet prints on the floor where his blood pools.

"What now, boss?" Grimjaw's voice cuts through the cordite smoke hanging in the air.

I rack the bolt on the MP5K, ejecting the spent magazine. It clatters to the floor, joining the scattered shell casings. "We clean up the blood," I say, my voice flat and empty. "Then we get to work."

The garage beyond the office stretches out before us—cathedral-high ceilings disappearing into shadows, rows upon rows of vehicles stripped to their frames. The chop-shop isn't just a building. It's a fortress. A resource depot. A command center.

"This city owes us a debt, Grimjaw." I walk past him, carrying the Telluric valves tucked under one arm and the IBM ThinkPad balanced in my other hand. "We're going to collect it."

The Undercity pack begins moving, hauling the dead handler's body toward the industrial sink in the corner. I don't look back. There's no point. Vargas was never more than a tool—the Syndicate's instrument to carve up the South Side for scraps. Now that tool is broken.

And I've claimed its territory.

Outside, the April night presses against the warehouse windows like something alive. Somewhere in the distance, sirens wail their meaningless songs. Newark Bay exhales its perpetual fog across the Kearny Marshes, rolling toward the Pulaski Skyway like an invasion force.

The chop-shop is ours now. All of it.

Every stolen vehicle, every piece of salvage, every weapon cached in the hidden compartments behind false walls. This will be our base. Our headquarters. The foundation from which we'll drag this rotten city into a new age—not with politics or laws, but with cold, hard violence.

My hand still tingles from the frost-cobalt discharge. The temperature gauge in my mind reads negative forty below. Good. That's where it needs to be.

I carry my prizes toward the main office—a proper workspace, free of corpses and bloodstains. The ThinkPad boots up with its familiar chime, screen glowing pale blue in the darkness. Time to see what secrets Vargas kept locked away inside.

But first, I need to understand what I'm sitting on top of.

The garage doors groan open behind me as the pack finishes disposing of evidence. Grimjaw appears in the doorway, his scarred face half-lit by moonlight filtering through the grimy skylights above.

"Boss, you should see this," he says. "There's enough hardware here to arm a small army."

"I know." I don't look up from the computer screen. "That was always the point."

The Millennium Breach isn't coming—it's already here. It's been eating at the edges of reality for years, chewing through the foundations of this world until everything starts coming apart at the seams. I can feel it in my bones, taste it on my tongue like rusted iron.

And someone has to hold the line.

Someone has to make them pay.

I save the encrypted files from the ThinkPad onto a floppy disk—Ironic that the Syndicate would keep their most sensitive data on obsolete technology—and stand. My reflection stares back at me from the monitor: a machine in human skin, eyes cold as winter stars.

This is my territory now. Every inch of it.

And I'll burn this whole fucking city down before I let anyone take it from us again.

## CHAPTER 15: THE THREE INDEX CARDS

The garage smells like old oil and despair when I push through the door, the Telluric valves wrapped in canvas against my chest. My shoulder's screaming from carrying the weight, but I don't slow down until I'm past the line of rusted Cadillacs and inside the climate-controlled work bay where Danny's locked away.

Bram Kovacs is slumped in the chair next to the Iron-Lung, his mouth open, snoring soft. He's an old bastard—sixty if he's a day—but his hands are steady enough that I kept him around after the others scattered. Loyalty like that doesn't come cheap.

"Bram."

His eyes crack open, bloodshot and confused. Then he sees what I'm carrying and he's awake, scrambling out of the chair fast enough that it topples backward.

"You got them," he says, and there's something like hope in his voice that makes my teeth hurt. I drop the canvas bundle onto the workbench with a heavy *thud* that echoes through the concrete space. The valves clatter against each other, silver catching the overhead fluorescents.

"They're telluric-grade," I say. "Should integrate without too much drama." I strip off the canvas, revealing the three intricate brass fittings. Each one hums faintly—I can feel it through my fingertips, that same resonance the black iron has been singing in Danny's lungs for months now.

Bram leans over, peering at them through his reading glasses. His calloused thumb traces the impossible angles. "Jesus Christ, Cole. Look at these thread pitches. They're not metric, not imperial..."

"They're dead-drop perfect," I cut him off. "That's all you need to know."

He doesn't argue. We've been dancing this dance since December, since the night some bastard from the Guild slipped Danny a cocktail of arcane mercury and raw Tellurium dust. Turned his lungs into a living cage of corrupted metal that keeps trying to spread. The Iron-Lung keeps him breathing, but it's just a bandage on a gunshot wound.

I roll up my sleeves.

The frost-cobalt magic stirs in my veins—dormant since Newark Bay, since watching those men drown under ice I conjured from nothing. It wants to *burn*. Wants to flash-freeze everything in sight. But I breathe through it, channeling the cold down into control, into precision. My hands stop shaking.

"Back up," I tell Bram.

He scrambles backward as I grab the first valve, feeling its weight. The brass casing is already warm from handling. I press it against the primary port on Danny's Iron-Lung, where the original brass fitting had started leaking two weeks ago. Through the thick glass porthole, I can see Danny's face—pale as paper, lips blue-gray, chest rising and falling with those wet, rattling breaths that sound like someone drowning underwater.

The iron lung's pressure gauge is climbing red. Ticking toward critical.

I close my eyes and let the magic flow.

It feels like swallowing broken glass—that's what using blood-iron always feels like. But beneath that pain is something else: certainty. Purpose. The valve glows blue-white as my magic wraps around it, and then I'm welding, not with heat or flame, but with pure intent made manifest. Metal that would normally require hours of precise threading instead flows together like water, merging seamlessly without slag, without flaw.

Bram makes a choked sound behind me.

When I open my eyes, the weld is perfect. Flawless. The silver valve sits locked into the brass housing like it grew there.

"The others," I say, voice rough.

We work fast after that. Second valve goes onto the auxiliary oxygen line. Third goes on the waste vent. Each one takes seconds, minutes at most, while normal machining would take days. By the time we're done, my arms are trembling from controlling the magic, keeping it focused instead of letting it explode outward in a flash-freeze.

But the gauges... the fucking gauges are green again.

Danny's breathing through the porthole has smoothed out. Still labored, still rattling, but steady now. Rhythmic. The wet death-rattle has faded to a hiss.

Bram collapses back into his chair, running his hands through thin white hair. "Christ Almighty, Cole."

I wipe my hands on a rag, watching the blue-black residue fade from my skin. It never fully disappears—just fades until next time. The smell of ozone hangs heavy in the air, mixing with the antiseptic tang of the sterilized workspace around Danny's Iron-Lung.

"How long?" I ask.

Bram knows what I mean. He stares at the valves, at the perfect welds he could never replicate in a hundred years. His face is grim when he looks up.

"A year. Maybe two." He pauses. "But the black iron in his lungs... it's still growing, Cole. These valves just bought us time."

I stare at my brother through the glass. At his pale face. At those lips that used to smile easy, used to crack jokes about my terrible taste in music. Now they're just flesh waiting to stop working.

"Time's all we've got," I say quietly.

Outside, past the garage doors, the South Side is waking up. The morning traffic on Route 28 sounds distant, muffled. Inside this concrete box, there's only the hum of machinery and the hiss of Danny's Iron-Lung keeping him alive one breath at a time.

My knuckles go white around the rag.

The Guild bastard who did this to Danny? Whoever slipped him that mercury-Tellurium cocktail?

They have a name now. A location. Three index cards in my jacket pocket with everything I need to burn their whole operation down.

And I'm done being patient.

* * *

The garage smells like motor oil and burnt copper. I'm sitting in the dark with Danny's chest rising and falling—rising and falling—in that steady, rhythmic cadence the Iron-Lung provides. The light from the IBM ThinkPad casts harsh shadows across my face as I stare at the screen, reading names.

Corporate signatures authorizing sludge disposal into Newark Bay. March 1997. Three months before my mother stopped breathing in County General ER. Four months before Danny coughed up his first lungful of black water.

I read each name aloud, testing how they sound on my tongue:

"Vincent Sterling. CEO. Authorized."

"Richard Voss. Chief Operations Officer. Authorized."

"Marcus Morvain. Senior Vice President of Environmental Compliance. Authorized."

They signed their names without thinking. Without looking at what they were signing away. They authorized poison by the thousand gallons, and somewhere down the line, that poison found its way into Danny's lungs. Found its way into my mother's bloodstream during dialysis. Found its way into the bay where fishermen pulled corpses instead of striped bass.

Elena's charred ID badge sits next to the ThinkPad. The plastic is melted, warped into a crescent shape around a screw she'd been trying to tighten when the fire started. I can still smell it—the acrid sting of burning PVC, the chemical sweetness of scorched human flesh. She tried to save those kids. Tried to pull them out of the clinic when the sprinklers failed and the smoke turned to hell-fire.

I didn't save her either.

The frost-cobalt magic hums beneath my skin now, quiet and controlled. It's not rage anymore. Rage was for when I thought I could still be a good man. Now it's something else—something colder, sharper, more precise. A tool.

Danny's Iron-Lung ticks like a mechanical heart. Every breath costs me something. Every day he survives is another day I carry this weight.

Penance. Revenge. A sliver of justice.

It’s a bad trade. It’s never enough.

But a sliver of justice for Danny is better than no justice for anyone. And I will burn this city to the bedrock to get it.

The three corporate seals stare back at me from the bottom of the manifest: Sterling, Voss, and Morvain. Their faces are in the company photos on the hard drive—smug men in expensive suits, standing in front of factories that belch black smoke into an already poisoned sky.

They don't know who I am yet. They don't see how their signatures led here—to this garage, to this machine keeping my brother alive, to the cold calculus I'm performing in the dark.

Killing them won't scrape the iron out of Danny's lungs. It won't bring my mother back from the ash scattered across County General's incinerator. The sliver of justice they'll receive—the fraction of consequence they'll pay for what they've stolen—it's pathetic. Inadequate.

It's not enough.

But it's something. And something is all I have left.

I close the laptop, the screen fading to black. My eyes lock onto those three names one last time before the darkness takes them. Sterling, Voss, Morvain.

Bram said I was becoming a monster. Maybe he's right. Maybe the frost-cobalt magic has been eating away at whatever humanity I had left since the night I installed that first piece of Arcane Iron. But if being a monster means I can make these men answer for what they've done... if it means Danny gets to breathe clean air someday...

Then let me be a monster.

The Iron-Lung ticks on. Danny sleeps. And somewhere in the distance, beyond the Kearny Marshes and under the Pulaski Skyway, men like Sterling and Voss are sleeping soundly in their beds, dreaming of profit margins and quarterly earnings.

Tomorrow, they'll wake up to find their world has changed.

Tomorrow, I start collecting.

* * *

The sub-basement smells like gunpowder and wet concrete. I've hauled my tool chests down here piece by piece—three trips up and down those fucking stairs, each one carrying the weight of everything that happened at County General. Each descent another nail in the coffin of who I used to be.

The chop-shop Danny acquired for us sits three blocks from Pulaski Skyway, nestled between an abandoned fabric mill and what used to be a deli before the riots. Perfect location. No questions asked. Cash transaction through a shell corporation run by one of Grimjaw's contacts. The kind of place that exists only in shadows.

I drag my main workbench into position beneath the single hanging bulb. It swings as I move, casting moving shadows across the concrete floor like something alive. Good. I want this place to feel like it's breathing. Waiting.

The tools go out first—wrenches, micrometers, valve grinders. Each one finds its home on the magnetic strips I've mounted above the bench. Precision matters now more than ever. Mistakes get you killed, or worse, they let the people responsible walk away. Not happening anymore.

Above the tools, I've hung a corkboard. Blank except for the three index cards pinned there, waiting.

Grimjaw's footsteps echo from upstairs—the heavy thud-thud-thud of his boots on wood, then metal as he descends the stairwell. He appears in the doorway, massive frame blocking most of the light from above. His yellow eyes catch the bulb's glow.

"Perimeter secure," he rumbles. "No movement in two hours."

I nod but don't look up. My hands are busy sorting depleted-bismuth rounds, checking each casing for microscopic cracks. The frost-cobalt hum is quiet now, banked down to embers. Useful cold. Controlled rage.

"How's the kid?" Grimjaw asks.

"He'll live." I slide a round into position under my magnifier. "Luckier than he deserves."

"That's rough, boss."

"The valves are installed." I set down the round. "Danny's stable. That's what matters."

Silence stretches between us. The kind of silence that exists only with people who've seen too much together. Grimjaw knows better than to push when I'm like this—focused, methodical, dangerous.

He glances at the corkboard. At the blank cards. Then back at me.

"You got something planned?"

I reach for the pack of index cards sitting next to my valve tools. The paper feels substantial, heavy. Important. I pull one free and pin it to the top of the board. It swings slightly, catching the light.

The other two remain blank beneath it.

Grimjaw shifts his weight. "Who we hunting first?"

My fingers find the black Sharpie. The cap comes off with a soft pop. I hold the marker over the card, feeling its weight. This moment. This single stroke of ink will change everything. No going back after this.

Not that there was ever any question.

I write the name that's been burning behind my eyes since County General. Since I held Danny's lifeblood in my hands while some faceless bastard gave the order to poison an entire neighborhood just to cover their tracks.

*Julian Sterling.*

The squeak of the marker on thick cardboard cuts through the basement silence like a blade. Each letter deliberate. Permanent.

When I finish, I cap the marker and step back.

Grimjaw leans forward, squinting at the board. His expression doesn't change, but I see the way his jaw tightens. He knows exactly who Julian Sterling is—the head of Sterling Manor operations, the man who signed off on Operation: Cleansing Tide. The official narrative says they were targeting a supernatural nest in Newark Bay. What they don't say in those sterile reports is how many civilians died. How many kids.

How many innocents caught in the crossfire while people like Sterling slept in their fortified estates.

"The boss of Sterling Manor," Grimjaw mutters. "That's... that's not a small target."

"No." I pull down the second card. Pin it next to Sterling's name. "But we start at the top."

"And the third?" He gestures at the remaining blank card.

"Insurance." My voice is flat. Empty. "We're building a list now, Grim. A complete one. Every name connected to what happened on the South Side. Every hand that signed, every finger that pulled the trigger, every person who looked the other way."

I turn to face him fully now. Let him see whatever's left in my eyes—because I know it's not much beyond cold purpose.

"This isn't revenge anymore. It's accounting."

Grimjaw nods slowly. Respectfully. "What do you need?"

"Time to plan. And your boys ready for surveillance work?"

"They're ready, boss."

Good. Because Julian Sterling won't be easy to reach. Sterling Manor is more fortress than house, surrounded by private security, magical wards, and enough firepower to repel a small army. We'll need patience. Intel. The right moment.

The bulb swings overhead as I return to sorting ammunition, but I keep glancing up at the corkboard. At that first name staring back at me in stark black letters:

**JULIAN STERLING**

Target number one.

The war begins with his name written down in permanent ink.
